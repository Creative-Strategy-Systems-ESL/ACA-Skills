# The growth creative machine

## Why this is the best time to be creative

> "You can come up with the wildest idea in your mind, use futuristic tools to bring it to life, and
> **just a few hours later launch it as an ad** that people on the other side of the world can see
> and react to."

**But that raises the question the course exists to answer:**

> "**How do you know if the creative idea is good or not?** Is there even such a thing, good or bad,
> in today's ad world? And if so, **how can you consistently create more good ideas?**"

---

## ⭐ The parable

From **Art & Fear** by David Bayles and Ted Orland, which Udi says he met through *Atomic Habits*.

⚠️ **The recording names the professor as "Jerry Olsman"**, which is how the quote below carries it. The photographer the parable is about is **Jerry Uelsmann**. Quoted as rendered, corrected here.

> "a photography professor named Jerry Olsman who divided his class into two groups. **The left side
> of the room would be graded on quantity,** the number of photos they submitted. **The right side
> would be graded on quality.** They only needed to produce one single perfect photo."

> "At the end of the term, guess who produced the best photos? **It was the quantity group.**"

**Why:**

> "Because they were **constantly shooting.** They experimented, tested, learned from their mistakes
> and got better. Meanwhile, the quality group spent most of their time **overthinking and planning
> for perfection**, and ended up with one pretty average photo."

> "This story perfectly illustrates the power of systemized creativity, where **creative excellence
> can come from practice and process and iteration, not just talent or inspiration.**"

⚠️ **The story is widely retold and its details vary between tellings.** It is a parable that makes an
argument, not a study. Use it as the argument it is.

⭐ **The reason it works on creative teams specifically:** the quality group's failure was not laziness
or low standards. It was *planning*. That is exactly what a team producing four polished ads a month
believes it is doing well.

## ⭐ Nobody can pick the winner

> "I worked at Meta for **nine years.** I've seen so many ads, concepts, campaigns, and I can tell
> you **even the best creative minds struggle to predict a winner.**"

**He demonstrates it rather than asserting it** — three ads, and the audience guesses:

| Ad | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Ad 3** | *"Looked nice but didn't hit the CPA goal"* |
| **Ad 2** | *"Showed early promise but didn't scale"* |
| ⭐ **Ad 1** | *"Scaled massively and drove millions of dollars in ad spend with positive ROI"* |

> "But **no one would have guessed it in advance.** And even ad number one **needed multiple
> iterations before it stuck.**"

⛔ **That last sentence is the one to keep.** The winner was not spotted *and* was not born finished.
Both halves argue for the same thing: a system that produces and iterates, rather than judgement
applied up front.

⚠️ **Note what this does to creative review.** If experts cannot predict winners, then a stakeholder
review that kills concepts on taste is removing options from a process whose whole value is having
options. It does not follow that review is useless — but it does follow that its job is compliance
and brand safety, not picking winners.

## The three parts

### 1. Volume

> "The fastest growing performance advertisers are producing significantly higher volume of ads,
> **between 50 and 100 new ads per week, including iterations.**"

**Why:**

> "Because **ad shelf life is getting shorter.** Listen to this staggering stat. **Only 10% of ads
> last more than two weeks.** To keep your campaign's performance, you need performing. You need to
> keep feeding the algorithm fresh"

⛔ **The 50-100 figure has no conditions attached** — no spend level, no team size, no category. It is
what he says the fastest-growing advertisers do, not a target for any given account. **Course 703's
five variables and Course 601's ladder are where a real number comes from.**

⚠️ **The 10% stat is given without a source.** It is consistent with Course 602's *"a new creative
only performs at its peak for a few weeks"*, which is the nearest corroboration in the programme.

### 2. Velocity

> "Creative teams today move **from idea to execution in hours, not weeks.**"

**His worked example:** the team behind **Photoleap** — from the makers of Facetune — needed a new
ad. *"A creative strategist shared a sound idea in a group chat. The sound creator immediately built
a custom track. And the team built visuals on top of it, storyboarded and **launched a suite of ads
within hours.**"*

**And on tooling:** *"With tools like ElevenLabs, brands generate voiceover instantly using AI. It
allows them to scale production and speed."*

⭐ **Notice the order in that example: the sound came first**, then visuals were built on it. That is
exactly Course 401's sound-first method, arrived at independently and never named as such here.

### 3. Versions

> "Let's talk about **how diverse your creative output should be** in times where we use creative as
> the only targeting mechanism."

**The decision tree:**

> "You create an ad and test it. **If it doesn't work, no problem** — move on and try a different
> angle. **If it does work, now the fun begins.** You break it down, **identify the winning
> elements, and multiply it.**"

⭐ **His three worked examples of what a "winning element" can be:**

| Brand | The element they multiplied |
|---|---|
| **Hismile** | ⭐ **A strong hook** — playing on the product's strength, whitening yellow teeth |
| **BetterMe** | ⭐ **A specific persona or creator.** *"You are probably recognizing this man from their ads. But not only you, also people from Turkey and other places around the world."* — the same creator scaled across markets and languages |
| **Taylor Brand** | ⭐ **A script.** *"identified this script as the key winning element and scaled it word by word to tens of other creators"* |

*"any other format, style or message that drives engagement and sales."*

⭐ **The three examples are deliberately different levels of abstraction** — a hook, a person, a
script. That is the useful part: "identify the winning element" is vague until you see that the
element can be any size, and the job is working out which one carried the result.
