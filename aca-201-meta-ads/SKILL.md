# Course 201: Mastering the Essentials of Meta Ads

**Ashley Vinson**, 20+ years in digital, ex-Meta and ex-Twitter, now consulting through Outcome
Company in Amsterdam. 1h 16m, 8 modules.

The platform course. Not how to make a good ad in general — how **Meta's system decides**, and
what a creative strategist must therefore do differently.

> "Most creative strategists will never upload the ads into the meta ad system. But knowing each of
> these steps and where it can go horribly wrong is critical so you can discuss and check it later."

> **You are a guest at someone else's curated event.**

it does not add value it gets swiped away.

## How to work through it

**System first, then the levers.** Almost every wrong instinct about Meta comes from not knowing what the
auction is actually optimising.

1. **Explain the auction and the objectives** from **How Meta's system decides** (below), including which objectives are
   traps. Nothing later makes sense without this.
2. **Do targeting and creative together** in **Targeting, placements, formats and creative** (below), because the argument for
   broad targeting only holds once creative is the variable.
3. **Add the Andromeda update** from **The Andromeda update** (below). It changes what "enough ads" means, so
   it belongs before any volume advice.
4. **Then testing and measurement** in **Testing, measurement and attribution** (below) — hypothesis-driven tests, and
   ⚠️ what each of the six attribution models can and cannot prove.
5. **Run the watchouts** in **Watchouts** (below) against their account, and use
   **Auditing an account you did not build** (below) if they have one to look at.
6. **Check they can say why broad beats narrow here**, and which objective they would pick and why. Both
   answers are wrong in a specific, common way if step 1 was skipped.

## Answering well

**Objectives are where campaigns are won or lost before creative exists.** *"Ensuring that you
choose the right one can make the difference between a campaign going well or well, not so well."*
If someone describes a goal that does not match their objective, say so first.

**Push back on narrow targeting, with the reason.** Narrow shrinks the audience, which raises
competition, which raises cost in an auction, and it excludes buyers. Ashley's client wanted women
22 to 49 for bras: *"don't women before 22 buy a bra? And I hope they're buying a bra after 49."*

**Be honest about attribution.** The course's position is that you cannot prove a single channel
drove a sale, and that saying so is the professional answer rather than a weak one. Give the
contribution case, not a false certainty.

**Do not oversell engagement.** *"In all my days at Twitter and Meta, we were never able to prove
that there's any correlation between getting a high engagement and actual business outcomes."*

## How Meta's system decides

Modules 1 and 2. The mechanics a creative strategist has to understand even though someone else
does the uploading.

### The scale, as ACA gives it

Meta is the parent of Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Messenger and Threads — *"almost 4 billion
monthly users with over 3 billion active users. That's over 40% of the world."* Advertising runs
across Facebook, Instagram and the Audience Network.

Many formats exist because *"people connect in a ton of different ways depending on what time it is
and what they're doing or what mood they're in."*

### The algorithm, and the mindset it forces

Every feed is uniquely tailored. That has one consequence worth stating plainly to clients:

> "You're not the main event. You're simply a guest at someone else's curated event."

The failure it prevents is the client who reviews their ad in isolation, in a conference room,
where it looks great — and forgets it will appear between things the viewer actually chose.

⭐ **The demo:** record your own feed, splice the client's ad in, show them. *"Clients realize
pretty quickly that their ad doesn't exactly make you wanna watch."*

### The auction formula

⛔ **Bidding highest does not win.** Meta scores each candidate ad:

| Component | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Bid** | What you are willing to pay for a click or conversion. Varies with supply and demand |
| **Estimated action rate** | How likely this person is to take your desired action after seeing it |
| **User value** | Relevance and quality. Formerly called *quality score*. Covers user engagement, overall ad quality, and landing-page relevance |

> **Total value = bid + estimated action rate + user value. The highest total value wins the
> auction and is shown to that individual.**

Why a creative strategist should care: two of the three components are creative's responsibility.
You cannot raise the bid, but better creative raises both estimated action rate and user value.

Meta filters this way deliberately — the system exists to catch *"those low quality and irrelevant
ads"*, because relevant ads keep users on the platform, which keeps the revenue coming.

### Campaign setup, in the order a media specialist works

Ashley's point is that a strategist should be able to check each step and spot where it went wrong.

#### 1. Route: reservation or auction

- **Reservation** — locks in price in advance for awareness or engagement. *"Very rarely used"*,
  but useful for budgeting a known big event.
- **Auction** — *"the one that people use 99.9% of the time."*

#### 2. Campaign objective

⭐ **The single most consequential choice, made before any creative exists.** Choosing an objective
tells Meta which goal to pursue, and it will pursue exactly that.

> "if you tell it, you know, that you want to get traffic, it will serve your ads to only those
> people that go to websites and no one else.... Just because someone likes to click on links and go
> to a website doesn't also mean that they like to buy things. They could just be, you know, a
> digital window shopper"

The six, with Ashley's verdict on each:

| Objective | What it does | Her verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Awareness** | Broadest possible audience; the system still optimises for relevance | ⭐ **A favourite.** Tends to be low cost. *"You can't convert unless people know about you"* |
| **Traffic** | Clicks to a website or destination, paid per click | ⚠️ *"Metas algorithm is really good at this one and will definitely get you clicks. But like I said earlier, clicks do not equal sales."* Expensive and rarely aligned to the real outcome |
| **Engagement** | Likes, watches, reads | ⚠️ Not a favourite. *"In all my days at Twitter and Meta, we were never able to prove that there's any correlation between getting a high engagement and actual business outcomes"* |
| **Leads** | Collects information: email, newsletter, test drive | ⚠️ Expensive. **Never run alone — always pair with an awareness campaign** |
| **App promotion** | Drives downloads | ⚠️ Expensive. Same rule: always pair with awareness |
| **Sales** | Lower funnel conversions | ⭐ *"Can be magical, assuming everything is in place"* — see the prerequisites below |

#### ⛔ The sales objective has prerequisites

It needs, on the client's side:

- The **Meta pixel** installed
- The **Conversions API (CAPI)**
- The **product catalog** connected, where relevant
- A **full-funnel campaign** running alongside, with awareness and consideration ads

⚠️ **And the trap worth catching before a client wastes budget:**

> "If your client hasn't already made any sales on their website yet, right, careful about using
> this objective until you've made some sales. Since meta's tool optimizes based on past sale
> history, it's critical that there's some sales or the ad system won't ever be able to run this ad
> since it will be lacking the data to optimize."

A brand-new store choosing Sales has picked an objective its own data cannot support.

#### 3. Optimisation

Each objective offers optimisation choices that fine-tune what the algorithm maximises. Awareness
can optimise for impressions (possibly showing the same person repeatedly, which is fine). Sales
can optimise toward people the algorithm thinks are most likely to convert.

#### 4. Audience

*"One of the biggest client debates you might have during a campaign."* See
`targeting-and-creative.md`.

#### 5. Budget

Daily spend and total campaign budget. The system recommends a level, and costs fluctuate with
supply and demand — **Q4 and holiday season are always more expensive.**

### The strategist's job in all this

State the campaign objective in the strategy plan, explicitly, *"to ensure that your media person
and you are going after the same ad objectives."* Most of the failures later in this course trace
back to a mismatch that was settled, wrongly, at this step.

## Targeting, placements, formats and creative

Modules 3 and 4. Where the strategist's decisions actually live.

### Creative is the targeting

The history, as Ashley tells it: first advertising bought **space** (a billboard, and hope). Then it
bought **audiences** (TV and magazines promising a gender, age, income, interest). Both were proxies.

> "everyone was using proxies, right? Assumptions about who might buy your product. For example,
> targeting like a 20 to 30-year-old women that earn $50,000 a year and likes kittens, you know,
> thinking that those are the people who would buy your product. **We don't know if that's true.**"

Meta's algorithm removes the need for the proxy. Directed by the objective, it finds the people
most likely to produce the outcome.

#### ⛔ Go broad. Two reasons, and the second is the one that convinces clients

1. **The demographic usually does not matter.** *"Whether the person is a 20-something guy or a
   50-something woman doesn't matter as long as it leads to the desired outcome."*
2. **Narrow targeting costs more and excludes buyers.** Narrowing shrinks the audience → raises
   competition → and in an auction, **costs go up**. It also cuts out people who would have bought.

⭐ **The bra example.** A client wanted women 22 to 49, certain that was the sweet spot.

> "Don't women before 22 buy a bra? And I hope they're buying a bra after 49. Because what they
> were doing by narrowing down like that, they were missing out on potential customers."

**The rule:** *"target as broadly as your budget and logic will allow."* Use Meta's **Advantage
Plus audience** where budget permits. Not every budget allows full breadth, so narrow intelligently
when you must — and note that a genuinely product-linked cut (women, for women's bras) is a
reasonable one.

#### And then the consequence

> "When you're casting that wide net with your audience, your creative becomes the key to
> attracting the most relevant people."

That is the whole argument of the course: broad targeting does not make creative less important, it
makes creative *the* targeting mechanism.

### Choosing which ads to make

The strategist should drive this — you know the objective, the audience insight and the success
measure by now.

⚠️ **The old way is dead.** Agencies used to take the media plan and fill each placement to spec,
reusing one asset everywhere it fit. *"Look, we can make 172 by 90 banner ad and it will fit into
94 placements. Score! Less work."* Now the requirement is **content fit for placement** — an ad
that makes sense where it is seen.

Build a **joint media plan** with the client and media team, so placements bought and formats made
are decided together rather than creative being stuffed into a plan that already exists.

For deciding what *type* of content goes into each format, Ashley points at Mirella's five stages
of market awareness (ACA Course 102).

### Placements and formats

**Formats** are the ad itself: video, carousel, image, and more. **Placements** are where it is
seen: Reels, Facebook Feed, Instagram Feed, Stories, Search Results, Messenger.

#### Variety is not optional

> "Meta's algorithm works best when it has a range of creative formats and placements to optimize.
> More ad formats and more placements, including variations within those, **won't drive up your
> costs.** In fact, having more ads and placements is likely to reduce costs and improve
> performance"

**Meta recommends at least six placements.** The temptation is to back only Reels and Feed because
that is where the eyeballs are; more placements give the algorithm more room to optimise.

⛔ **Do not lean on auto-placement.** Uploading one asset and letting Meta resize it into everything
is convenient and *"not always ideal... often the resized ads don't look their best. Not really
great at all."* A 4:5 image does not become a Reel, and a 9:16 looks wrong in Instagram search.

Build per-placement versions where you can. In a pinch, supply 9:16 **and** 4:5 — and preview every
ad in every placement regardless.

#### Format guidance

- **Video, in multiple lengths.** From a few seconds up to about 15. Longer is a risk: *"anything
  longer and well, you're clearly a risk taker... Videos can be longer, but it better be damn
  interesting."* Hedge long cuts with much shorter versions.
- **Aspect ratios:** everything in at least **9:16, 1:1 or 4:5**.
- **Still images**, especially multi-image ads.
- **Cinemagraphs** — one part of a still animated. Ashley's own favourite, *"really eye catching."*

Every campaign should carry video (even a fast-moving slideshow of stills) **and** compelling
stills. Lower-funnel work needs conversion-driving content too.

### Variations within a format

⚠️ **Read `andromeda-update.md` alongside this section.** The later module revises it: cosmetic
variation is no longer sufficient on its own.

As taught in module 4 — colour variations (backgrounds, designs), copy variations (headlines,
primary text, calls to action), and small edits like a different thumbnail or a reworked headline,
which *"can refresh your ad and reduce ad fatigue."*

Ashley's tooling note: if a creative team is building each variant by hand in Photoshop, *"stop
right now. You are wasting a ton of time and budget."* AI-driven variation tools exist for this.

#### ⛔ The Meta Advantage Creative tool, and why she warns against it

It auto-generates variations inside Ads Manager. Her verdict, explicitly time-stamped:

> "As of this date, it's not the greatest... it can seriously go off brand in terms of visuals and
> messaging. If you just let the AI tool build out your ads, you don't know necessarily what you're
> gonna get because you aren't able to preview all the versions."

**You always need to see every version in its placement.** Treat this as a snapshot judgement, not
a permanent one — she adds *"hopefully they'll improve the tool soon."*

### Creative best practices

⭐ **Why this matters more than anything else in the course**, with ACA's figures:

- **~70% of a campaign's ROI** traces back to the quality of the ad
- Following creative best practices can give **up to 4× the sales impact**
- Campaigns using them typically see **50% higher ROAS**

> "You can have the best strategy, a great product, and a solid media buy. But if your ad creative
> doesn't stop the thumb, your campaign is going to tank."

Ashley's realism about the strategist's role: you are usually not designing it, you are directing
it — *"and trust me, most creatives can't nail a meta ad without a lot of direction. It's really a
niche skill."*

#### The seven

1. **Clear message.** The viewer spends an average of **1.7 seconds** on a piece of content. *"No
   more than 10 words on any frame. Heck, five words would even be better."*
2. **Never assume audio is on.** Subtitles on all video. If audio *is* on, use voiceover or music.
   Subtitle font large enough to read on a phone.
3. **Fit for placement.** Build per-placement versions; preview them; do not trust auto-resize.
4. **Short, but not too short.** Aim **under about six seconds**. *"Yes, you might have a story to
   tell, but if you can't hook them quickly, that story won't even get started."* Longer is fine
   with a real reason — test different edit lengths.
5. **Brand and message up front — in the first two seconds.** *"If you're relying on your audience
   to wait until the end to remember your brand and message, you're doing it wrong. People rarely
   watch ads till the end."* Critical for awareness campaigns.
6. **Variety.** More uploaded creative gives the algorithm more to find a winner among. Five product
   benefits → try an ad per benefit.
7. ⭐ **Every ad must stand on its own.** *"Never assume that your audience has seen your previous
   ads."* You do not control the order Meta serves them in. **Even in retargeting, assume this is
   the first time.**

### The copy that sits outside the creative

Written by the strategist and handed to the media specialist. Not every placement renders all of
it, and Meta accepts multiple versions of each for testing.

| Element | Length |
|---|---|
| **Primary text** | ~125 characters, can run longer. Position varies by ad |
| **Headline** | Up to 255 characters, *"but I would keep it short"* |
| **Description** | ~125 characters or less. Does not appear in every format |
| **Destination URL** | The landing page |

### The line to end on

> "**There is no perfect ad.** Every brand is going to need to experiment and find out what works
> for their brand."

## The Andromeda update

Module 8, recorded later than the rest as *"an extra video to give you a more current update on
meta ads in 2026, especially around Automation, Advantage Plus Tools Andromeda. And why creative
variety matters even more than it used to."*

⭐ **This module partly supersedes module 4.** Read it as the current position where the two differ.

### What Andromeda actually is

Ashley defines it carefully, and the care is the point — it is narrower than people assume.

> "Andromeda is part of Meta's newer ads delivery system. More specifically, it is part of the **ad
> retrieval stage**, which is the part where meta sorts through a massive number of possible ads and
> decides which ones are even worth considering for a specific person in a specific moment."

The mechanic: an ad slot opens, and Meta cannot deeply evaluate every possible ad in the moment
because the person is already scrolling. So it first **retrieves a smaller pool** that look like
plausible fits, and the ranking and auction then decide the winner from that pool.

> "**It is not the whole auction. It is not the whole delivery system.**"

After retrieval, everything from `the-system.md` still applies — objective, bid, estimated action
rate, whether the ad is a good experience.

### Signals

> "A signal again is basically a clue. It is a piece of information the system can use to understand
> what an ad is about, who it might be relevant for, and what kind of result it might drive."

Signals come from the campaign objective, the landing page, the pixel, the product catalog, the
copy, the format, the hook, the visuals, the person in the ad, the message — **and from how people
respond once it runs**: clicks, watches, ignores, comments, conversions, bounces.

Her examples of creative as signal:

- An ad showing a parent packing school lunches → a clue
- An ad opening *"tired of wasting money on cheap shoes"* → a clue
- A creator talking to camera about sensitive skin → a clue

> "This is why the Andromeda update matters for creative strategists. **Meta is getting better at
> reading and using the creative itself as a signal.**"

The consequence, stated plainly: *"your creative inputs matter more, not less."*

## The five things to know and do

### 1. Creative is doing more of the targeting work

The move away from narrow targeting was already true; automation makes it more so. Targeting used
to be the media team's job — they picked audience and placements, creative filled the plan. That
made sense when there were fewer algorithmic signals and everyone was guessing.

Objective, media setup, budget, pixel, CAPI, landing page and measurement all still matter. But the
creative now supplies most of what the system uses to find the match.

⭐ **So the different buyer motivations have to be visible in the creative itself.** First-time
buyers, sceptical buyers, loyal customers, gift buyers, price-sensitive buyers, quality-focused
buyers, people who need to see the product in use — those differences need to show up.

> "You cannot just make one generic ad and expect automation to magically figure it out... that is
> like giving a matchmaker one blurry dating profile and saying, yeah, hey, can you find my
> soulmate?"

### 2. ⛔ Stop thinking "more ads" means five tiny variations of the same thing

This is the correction to module 4, and Ashley is specific about it:

> "I do not mean taking one ad changing the background from blue to green. Moving the logo, slight
> delay the gift and then suddenly say, look, we made five versions."

**She marks the change as a change**, which is why this is an update and not a contradiction:

> "To be fair, some of that used to work better than it does now. There was a time when small
> variations could help refresh performance... and small variations can still have a role,
> **especially once you know a concept has potential**. But if all you are doing is making tiny
> cosmetic changes, you are probably not giving meta enough meaningful difference to work with."

> "Meta does not just need more stuff, it needs **more meaningful and distinct creative inputs**."

What meaningful difference means: different hooks · different messages · different product moments ·
different reasons to believe · different formats · different use cases · different objections ·
different proof points · different creator styles.

⭐ **The line that settles the "how many ads" question:**

> "**More ads is not automatically a strategy.** If you make ten ads that are basically the same
> idea with tiny changes, you have technically made more ads, sure, but strategically, you may not
> have given the system much more to work with."

### 3. Build creative groups around different jobs, not different formats

A single campaign might carry:

- A **product demo**
- A **creator-led use case**
- An **objection-handling** ad
- An **offer-led** ad
- A **proof or quality** ad
- A **short hook** version and a **longer consideration** version
- A **lifestyle** version, a **product-focused** version, a **brand-focused** version

⚠️ **Not a checklist.** *"You do not need every single one of those in every campaign. This is not
a grocery list."* Pick the jobs this campaign actually needs.

### 4. Build for the question you want answered

Because creative groups differ by job rather than by cosmetic, the results become interpretable —
but only if the metric matches the job.

> "If one ad gets more video views, that does not automatically mean people understand the product.
> And if one ad gets more engagement, that does not automatically mean it was good for the brand."

The upgrade in what you can learn:

> "You are not just asking which blue background one. You are asking better strategic questions.
> **Which hook worked? Which message worked? Which product moment worked? Which audience motivation
> seem to matter? Which objection needed answering? Which creative style helped people care?**"

### 5. Your job is never done

> "even the best ads fatigue, eventually people see them. Too many times the market changes,
> competitors react to the offer changes, seasonality changes. The platform keeps learning,... the
> thing that works beautifully in one week may start to get tired by **week four, week six, or week
> eight**."

⚠️ **Fatigue is not evidence the creative was bad.** *"It just means ads have a lifespan."* And in
an automated environment it compounds: when people stop responding, the system reads that as a
signal too, and delivery drops.

So the job is not one batch at launch. It is *"building a system that can keep feeding the campaign
with fresh, useful, meaningful, different creative"* — new hooks, product moments, creator angles,
proof points, objection handlers, seasonal versions, offer-led ads, and edits informed by the last
round.

> "You are not just launching creative, you are **managing creative momentum.** And yes, that sounds
> like a phrase someone would put on a conference slide. But you know what? It's actually"

---

### The final takeaway

> "creative variety is no longer and nice to have. It is a must have. But the keyword is not just
> variety, it is **meaningful and distinct variety.** Different creative ideas, doing different jobs
> for different audience motivations inside one clear campaign strategy."

And the argument the whole module exists to make:

> "yes, meta is definitely more automated now. Yes, Advantage Plus tools are doing more. Yes,
> Andromeda helps meta process and retrieve ads in a more advanced way, but none of that means
> creative strategy is becoming less important. It means creative strategy is becoming **more**
> important because **automation is only as good as the inputs you give it.** And if you give the
> system boring, generic, repetitive ads, you should not be shocked when the campaign struggles."

> "Meta can help find the match, but you use the creative strategist. You still need to give it
> something worth matching, and then you need to keep giving it something worth matching."

## Test and learn

> "Test and learn means testing your campaign based on a hypothesis and then learning whether that
> hypothesis is proven true or not."

Digital's advantage over every other medium is that testing is *"relatively easy, fast, and
inexpensive"*, so *"you'd be silly not to test on every single campaign."*

⭐ **It is continuous, not an event.** The clients Ashley sees performing best *"are always in a
constant state of testing and learning. No campaign goes live without something being tested, even
if it's just ads with a different colored background."*

⚠️ **The complacency trap:** *"it's critical to never get too comfortable and think, okay, we found
out exactly what works on Meta, so now let's do that forever."* Algorithms and consumer preference
both move; a fixed formula decays.

### Have a plan, and have it early

Start the testing plan **before the creative is made**, so the creative is built to answer the
question. For established brands, plan at least a quarter ahead, ideally a year — it keeps briefs
on a structured path.

**Write down everything you want to learn, then sequence it so each test builds on the last.** Her
example progression: which ad type drives more awareness → does adding creator content to those ads
help → then formats, placements, copy, visuals.

⛔ **One element per test.** *"If you test multiple factors at once, you won't be able to isolate
what caused the difference or the improvement."* Each test is a stepping stone.

### Meta's testing tools

| Tool | What it does |
|---|---|
| **A/B testing** | Two or more ad variations against each other |
| **Sales testing** | How ads drive sales or conversions |
| **Meta Experiments** | A section of Ads Manager; test across placements, audiences or creative formats |
| **Brand survey test** | Good option for brands testing awareness |
| **Brand lift / sales lift studies** | ⚠️ Require a Meta representative and a much higher budget threshold. Worth it if you have both — *"they help you measure true impact"* |

The **sales lift study** is called out as *"the gold standard for measuring the true impact of
campaigns on sales, but it's not always available."*

### The test setup, and a worked example

Four things define a test:

1. **Campaign objective** — the clear goal
2. **Hypothesis** — what you think will achieve it
3. **Metrics to measure** — how success is tracked
4. **Test parameters** — how long it runs (**typically about four weeks**) and the **holdout group
   size**

**Ashley's example, in full:**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Campaign objective | Increase brand awareness by 5% over previous campaigns |
| Hypothesis | Adding creator content to our ads will drive more brand awareness than previous campaigns |
| Metrics | A three-question brand survey comparing the exposed group to the control |
| Parameters | Four weeks, 5% holdout group |

#### ⭐ Reading the result, including the null one

If creator-plus-brand content beat brand alone, you can conclude creator content boosted
performance and keep using and testing it.

If there was little difference, **do not dismiss creator content altogether**:

> "It's possible the specific content didn't resonate or other factors influence results. In that
> case, test different content or adjust another variable."

Then form a new hypothesis and go again. *"Test, learn, iterate and optimize."*

## Measurement and attribution

### The honest position, and it is the course's

> "It's very difficult to prove that your campaign was the sole reason for growing awareness or
> selling a product. Most brands are doing other things beyond your campaign."

⭐ **Ashley's Twitter interview story**, which is the module's argument in narrative form. Final
round, needed the job, asked how she would prove to a CMO that Twitter ads drove sales. She could
have shown click-through and engagement and implied the rest.

> "But, I knew that would be a lie. I refuse to lie about attribution I'll lie about the color of my
> hair (I'm a real blond!) because I have my standards! So I gave the honest answer: **“I can’t prove it.”**"
>
> "Unless that ad on Twitter was the only one ever run and it was for a product that could only be
> bought through that ad, I cannot honestly claim that Twitter drove that sale. **However, I can
> certainly make a case that it likely contributed to the sale.**"

She got the job. The lesson to carry: the professional answer is the contribution case, not
manufactured certainty.

### Brand metrics are proxies

For awareness, reach and consideration objectives, *"most digital metrics are proxies."* A strong
view-through rate looks good on paper and does not mean anyone registered your brand.

> "You can't get inside the heads of your audience. Maybe your ad had a cute kitten, and sure,
> people noticed the kitten, but did they notice your product?"

Ashley's name for how budget gets allocated across uploaded ads: **the hunger games of ad spend** —
upload 20, and the system funds whichever seem to be hitting the objective.

⚠️ **Read as a description of how budget flows, not as an instruction to upload 20 near-identical
ads.** Module 8 is explicit that ten ads which are the same idea with tiny changes have not given
the system much more to work with. See `andromeda-update.md`.

### Meta's attribution models

Six, all customisable to the campaign and customer journey:

| Model | Credit goes to |
|---|---|
| **Last click** | 100% to the final interaction |
| **First click** | The first interaction |
| **Linear** | Split equally across all touchpoints |
| **Time decay** | More credit the closer a touchpoint is to conversion |
| **Position based** | First and last get the credit, the middle shares the rest |
| **Data driven** | Machine learning assigns credit by each touchpoint's actual impact |

Ashley notes these get complicated enough that *"you've got a lot of times that you're gonna have
to have a marketing data scientist hanging around"*, and points to Meta's business help centre for
implementation.

#### ⛔ Why not just use last click

It is the easy option and many clients want it. The analogy she borrows:

> "Think of last click attribution, like it's going out, you're having 12 beers and then finishing
> the night with a tequila shot. The next morning, you're nursing your mom, the next morning, you're
> nursing a brutal hangover and you think, "Hmm, that tequila did me in." But realistically, it's
> probably the beers that played a bigger part"

Last click blames the tequila and ignores the beers.

### Which metric proves what

#### Performance metrics — *"more straightforward and clear cut and sometimes brutal"*

| Metric | What it tells you, and the caveat |
|---|---|
| **Clicks** | Feel good, rarely the point unless traffic is the goal. Meta may find *"click happy"* people who never convert |
| **CPC** — cost per click | Useful against benchmark when traffic is the goal. Meaningless for awareness |
| **CPV** — cost per view | Video campaigns. High CPV → adjust targeting or creative |
| **VTR** — view-through rate | How much of the video is watched. Past that 1.7-second average suggests the creative is resonating, but *"we don't know for a fact that it's the most perfect metric"* |
| **ROAS** — return on ad spend | Revenue per dollar spent. *"A crucial metric for performance"* |
| **Cost per download** | Apps. Too high → revisit targeting or creative |

Ashley's Dutch phrase for click-happy non-buyers: *kijken, kijken, niet kopen* — look, look, don't
buy.

#### Brand metrics — *"trickier, but still important"*

Without a brand lift study or survey, these infer brand success:

| Metric | What it tells you, and the caveat |
|---|---|
| **CPM** — cost per thousand impressions | Below benchmark suggests the algorithm finds your ad relevant and well-targeted. ⚠️ Fluctuates seasonally — Q4 is expensive |
| **VTR** | Also a brand-recall signal, especially with brand front-loaded |
| **Engagement** | Reactions, comments, shares, clicks. Some brands use it for affinity — but *"a high engagement rate doesn't always mean you're driving sales or brand growth"* |
| **Reach** | Unique people reached. Critical for brand campaigns, especially CPG. ⭐ **Aim for a frequency of 2 to 3** so the message sticks |

### Getting closer to the truth

On Meta's own attribution: *"it's not all-encompassing."* Consider third-party measurement — Google
Analytics, TripleLift, or multi-touch attribution — to see touchpoints across platforms.

> "Measurement and attribution is an art form and there is no 100% truth. Now be careful with
> proxies and use all the available measurement tools to get as close to the truth of how"

## Watchouts

Module 7, which Ashley introduces as *"dumb things other people did so you don't do them too."*

Seven mistakes, each a real one she has watched happen.

### 1. Organic is not enough

> "If you're making an ad for a brand, you need media behind it. Otherwise, you're just spending
> cold, hard cash to create some content and hoping it magically makes its way into people's feeds."

⚠️ **Organic content reaches roughly 5% of your own followers, if you are lucky.** The viral brand
reel exists, *"but that's like winning the lottery."* Fine as a bonus, never a growth strategy.

### 2. Design for the safety zones

Meta publishes safety zones in its ad specs. Ignore them and text gets cropped.

> "I cannot tell you how many ads I still see with the subtitles or logos cut off."

The designer needs a template that accounts for them — every platform has them — and **someone has
to preview the ads across all placements.** *"If your creative is getting chopped off, no one is
going to engage with it, and it just looks sloppy."*

### 3. You need the rights to the music

Music available inside Instagram for a personal Reel is **not** licensed for a brand's ad.

Legitimate routes: Meta's own free music library for ads, or any royalty-free library. *"If you're
a big fancy brand, you can definitely try licensing some Beyonce tunes, but don't make the mistake
of thinking you can just use any track without the proper rights."*

### 4. Know Meta's ad rules before you upload

Ads get rejected or pulled for rules people did not know applied to them.

- A campaign about the environment can fall under **social issues**, which carries its own rules
- **Targeting restrictions**, especially anything involving teens

Check current guidelines before the buy, not after the rejection.

### 5. The measurement strategy must match the creative

If you are running a brand survey or lift study, the creative has to reinforce the thing being
measured.

> "Imagine a survey asking, which brand do you think of when you see kittens forever? And the ad
> that you're running doesn't even mention kittens forever. That's a big missed opportunity, and it
> makes it impossible to properly attribute success."

### 6. ⭐ The creative must match the ad objective

Called *"a classic"*, and the one most likely to land on the strategist.

> "the campaign objective is to get downloads for a mobile app, but wait for it. The ad only talks
> about the brand, but doesn't mention the app. Clients are gonna be like, why didn't I get any
> downloads?"

Her account of the blame: *"the creative strategist didn't check the creative. The creative clearly
didn't read the brief, and the client approved the wrong things. But guess who takes the fall for
this? Yep, you."*

**So the check is yours to run**, whoever else failed to.

### 7. Do not use Boost

Meta's boost-a-post button is quick and the options are thin. *"None of these objectives usually
lead to business outcomes. For instance, traffic does not equal sales."*

Use Ads Manager, or the equivalent in whatever media-buying software you are in. *"Yes, I know,
it's more complicated, but no one said life was easy."*

## The key takeaways, as Ashley closes

1. **Understand the algorithm.** It decides which ads have the highest total value.
2. **Creative is the targeting.** Do not over-rely on narrow targeting; broaden and let the
   algorithm work.
3. **Make a lot of ads.** Mix formats, placements and creative. ⚠️ See `andromeda-update.md` — the
   later module sharpens this from *a lot* to *meaningfully different*.
4. **Nail the creative.** Quality is a huge factor. Follow the best practices.
5. **Have a test-and-learn plan**, spanning campaigns rather than sitting inside one.
6. **Measure and attribute honestly.** *"There is no 100% truth. Be careful with proxies."*

⭐ **The thread to keep returning to:**

> "Stay focused on the campaign objective and how you will measure that success... Am I making
> enough ads to drive awareness? Does that ad actually communicate the brand? Am I testing correctly
> to see if I'm driving that awareness? Am I measuring it correctly?"

## Auditing an account you did not build

**⚠️ This file is this skill's assembly, not a module of the course.** Every check in it comes from
Ashley's material — the objectives table, the targeting argument, the creative best practices, the
seven watchouts, the measurement section — put into the order a diagnosis actually needs. ACA does
not ship it as a sequence.

It exists because "I've inherited this account and it's a mess" is the most common way this course
gets used, and because working the modules in teaching order is the wrong order for triage.

### ⛔ The triage order, and why it is this way

> **Objective → measurement → targeting → creative.**

Work it in that order and stop at the first thing that is broken, because everything downstream
inherits it. The usual instinct is to reach for creative first, which is the most expensive possible
place to start and often fixes nothing.

- **A wrong objective cannot be fixed by better creative.** Meta will keep pursuing the goal it was
  given. This is the single most consequential setting in the account.
- **Broken measurement means you cannot tell whether anything you change worked.** Fixing it costs
  nothing and is worth doing before spending on assets.
- **Narrow targeting raises costs in an auction and excludes buyers**, and it is a settings change,
  not a production job.
- **Creative is the biggest lever** — but only once the three above are not lying to you.

---

### 1. Objective

- [ ] **What objective is it running?** Get this before anything else.
- [ ] **Does it match the business outcome the client actually wants?** If they want sales and the
      objective is Traffic or Engagement, stop here — this is the finding.
- [ ] **Traffic running on an ecommerce account?** ⚠️ Expect exactly the symptom pattern of cheap
      clicks and no revenue. *"Clicks do not equal sales."*
- [ ] **Engagement being used as a performance objective?** *"We were never able to prove that
      there's any correlation between getting a high engagement and actual business outcomes."*
- [ ] **Leads or App promotion running alone?** Both should be paired with an awareness campaign.
- [ ] **Sales objective without the prerequisites?** Pixel, CAPI, catalog where relevant, and a
      full-funnel campaign alongside. See `the-system.md`.

⭐ **The pattern worth recognising instantly:** a good CPC with no sales is not a half-working
campaign. It is the system doing precisely what it was told, and the cost efficiency is a score in
the wrong game.

### 2. Measurement

- [ ] **Is the pixel installed, and is it actually firing on purchase?** Not "is it in the account"
      — is the event landing. On an inherited account, assume nothing.
- [ ] **Is the Conversions API set up?**
- [ ] **Is there any conversion history, and what shape is it?** Steady, or one spike?
- [ ] **Which attribution model is the reporting using?** If last click, read the tequila analogy in
      `testing-and-measurement.md` before quoting the number to anyone.
- [ ] **Does any other system claim the same conversions?** Email, affiliate, an ecommerce
      dashboard. ⚠️ **If two platforms each claim credit for the same orders, both numbers are
      inflated** — reconcile against the backend order count before presenting either.
- [ ] **Is there a test running at all? A holdout?** If not, nobody in the account currently knows
      what is working, including whoever is telling you what is wrong.

⚠️ **The course gives no minimum conversion volume for the Sales objective**, no learning-phase
threshold, and nothing on attribution windows. Those are real gaps. Do not invent numbers for them
— check Meta's own current documentation.

### 3. Targeting

- [ ] **How narrow is it?** Interest stacks and tight age bands are the common inherited problem.
- [ ] **Would broadening be affordable?** The rule is *"as broadly as your budget and logic will
      allow"*, and Advantage Plus audience where budget permits.
- [ ] **Is any narrowing genuinely product-linked**, or is it a proxy someone assumed? A cut that
      the product itself demands is reasonable; a demographic guess usually is not.
- [ ] **How many placements?** Meta's own recommendation as of the recording is at least six. More
      placements do not raise costs and usually lower them.
- [ ] **Is auto-placement doing the resizing?** Preview every placement before trusting it.

### 4. Creative

- [ ] **How many genuinely distinct ideas are running** — as opposed to how many ad IDs exist?
      ⭐ Three colourways of one video is **one** creative input. See `andromeda-update.md`.
- [ ] **Do the ads carry different jobs?** Demo, objection handling, proof, offer, lifestyle.
- [ ] **Does the creative match the objective?** Watchout 6, and the one that lands on the
      strategist.
- [ ] **Brand and message in the first two seconds?**
- [ ] **Subtitles, and readable on a phone?**
- [ ] **Does each ad stand alone**, without assuming the viewer saw the others?
- [ ] **Anything cropped by the safety zones?**
- [ ] **Music rights? Category rules?**
- [ ] **How long has it been running?** Fatigue is normal by week four to eight and is not evidence
      the creative was bad.

### 5. What to say when someone blames the creative

Frequently the agency or the previous owner will assert the creative is the problem. It may be. But:

- **A creative diagnosis made without measurement is an assertion**, not a finding.
- **New creative built on the wrong objective inherits the wrong objective.**
- **"More variations" is not the same ask as "more distinct concepts"** — see `andromeda-update.md`,
  and re-scope the quote before agreeing to it.

⭐ **If they insist the creative is fine and the client agrees, use Ashley's demo**: record your own
feed, splice the ad in, watch the room change. *"The contrast is often stark and well, a bit
depressing."*
