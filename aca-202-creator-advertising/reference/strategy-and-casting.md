# The five strategy questions

Where creator work goes wrong, in Ashley's telling:

> "A client says, hey, we want to increase sales, and we think influencers can sell our products.
> And then the team goes, okay, let's go find some creators who can sell some stuff. **Whoa whoa
> whoa.**"

Before hiring, briefing, or doing anything, answer five questions. Creator content only works when
it connects the brand's goal to something real people actually care about — *"and that is the part
brands often skip."*

> "The audience is usually not sitting around thinking about your brand strategy. They are thinking
> normal human things like, I'm hungry, I need a ride home, I want a sunscreen that doesn't feel
> disgusting, I want jeans that actually fit."

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | **What is the client objective, and how will success be measured?** |
| 2 | **What do we know about the audience's need state or context?** |
| 3 | **What does the brand need to communicate in that context?** |
| 4 | **What creator job could help?** |
| 5 | **How will paid media support and distribute the content?** |

## Worked all the way through — ACA's rideshare example

**The client:** a rideshare service. **The objective:** get new riders to try the app.

> "That is the business goal, but it is not yet a creator strategy."

**1. Objective and measurement.** Not "make creator content" — get new riders to try the service.
Success might be new rider signups, first rides completed, cost per new rider, or trial from a
first-ride offer. The exact metric follows the client's KPIs.

**2. Audience need state.** Nobody thinks *"I need to try a rideshare brand today."* They think:

- I need a reliable way to get home
- I do not want to wait forever
- I do not want to pay too much
- I'm going out tonight and I do not want to deal with parking

> "Creator content should not just say what the brand wants to say, it should connect the brand
> message to what people actually need, want, feel, or are trying to solve."

**3. What the brand needs to communicate.** It is easy to use · it is reliable · there is a
first-ride offer · it is a good option for going out.

**4. The creator jobs.** Not picked from a menu — *"You're not saying, I'll have one attention to
trust and a side of demonstration."* Each connects back to objective, context and message:

- One creator **demonstrates** how easy it is to book
- One shows the service in a **nightlife moment** (relevance)
- One handles the **price objection** with the first-ride offer
- One builds **trust** by showing a real trip experience

**5. How paid media supports it.** Same business goal, different media roles: the awareness video
might buy reach or video views; the ease-of-use demo might target people who already showed
interest; the discount-code video runs closest to the download.

⚠️ **The content job and the media objective have to make sense together.**

> "You might take an awareness style creator video and run it like a pure app download app. Then you
> wonder why did it not drive all these cheap installs? ... **That is not** necessarily **a creator
> problem. That is a creative content and media mismatch.**"

In a perfect world creative strategy and media plan are built together, each informing the other.

## ⭐ The team idea

The mistake it prevents: making every creator asset say the same direct thing.

> "yes, you could be very literal and make every single creator, as it say, download the app now and
> use my code for 20% off your first drive. Hey, and sometimes you do need that direct download
> message, but **if every creator asset says the exact same thing, you are probably not using
> creators as strategically as you could.**"

The metaphor:

> "Think of it like football. Or as we'd say in America, soccer. The team has one goal, right?
> Score. But not every player has the same position. One player creates the opening, another moves
> the ball forward, another assist, another takes the shot."

The nightlife video may not make anyone download the app. It primes them, so that when the direct
offer arrives *"the app is not just some random offer coming into your feed. It already has a place
in their life."*

### It still works on one creator

> "this does not always mean you need ten creators. Maybe you only have budget for one or even two
> creators. That is fine. **The question is still the same. What different roles can that creator
> content play?**"

One creator can make a broad awareness video, a simple booking demo, and a direct offer video.

### ⛔ The Frankenstein ad

What happens when one asset is asked to do every job:

> "It's trying to be relatable, educational, funny, trustworthy, promotional, urgent, and conversion
> focused all in six seconds. Yeah, good luck with that."

# Casting strategy

## ⛔ Do not start searching before you know what you are casting for

> "A lot of people start looking for creators way too early."

Ashley's own confession: there is a cat groomer online she loves and is *"just dying to find the
right brand fit"* for — and he does not fit the strategy for any client she has.

> "if you start searching before you know what you need, **every interesting creator starts to look
> like a possibility** and that that is dangerous."

⭐ **The metaphor that makes it stick:**

> "Looking for creators without a clear casting brief is a little bit like, you know, doing grocery
> shopping when you are starving and you do not have a shopping list. Man, I don't know about you,
> but if I do that, I somehow come home with Oreos, Fritos, some weird sour candy, and approximately
> zero of the things I was supposed to buy. **The problem is not the grocery store. The problem is
> that I**"

**The casting brief is that shopping list.** See `the-casting-brief.md` — Ashley writes one for
projects of any size and has the client sign it off before looking at a single person.

## The six casting criteria

Once the job is known, these are what to look for.

### 1. Can they create interesting content?

Followers do not mean they can make a useful piece of *advertising*. Look at the actual content:

- Do they know how to open a video?
- Do they hold attention?
- Do they understand pacing?
- Does their content feel natural on the platform?

### 2. Can they communicate clearly?

Explaining, demonstrating, simplifying, showing, comparing, making the product easier to
understand. *"If the creator cannot communicate clearly, they may not be the right person."*

### 3. Are they credible for this category?

⭐ **Credibility is not only expertise.** It might be lived experience, taste, community trust,
category knowledge, *"or simply that that they make sense in the context of the brand and message."*

### 4. Do they fit the tone and the job you need?

> "A creator can be great and still wrong for the brand... Maybe they are too polish, too chaotic,
> too serious, too funny, too young to niche, too broad to glamorous, too casual or just totally
> wrong. **Fit matters.**"

### 5. Do they bring something the brand cannot easily make itself?

Personality, craft, humanity, visual style, expertise, relationship to the category, community, or
the ability to make a product feel real.

> "A creator should bring something useful and distinctive, **not just be a human tripod for your
> client's script.**"

### 6. Are they brand appropriate?

*"The less fun part, but it matters."* Can this person represent the brand, the category and the
message without creating a problem?

⚠️ **The alcohol example, given concretely:** working on alcohol brands in the US, Ashley's teams
*"generally only hired people who were at least 25"* — above the legal drinking age. And it is not
only age: a creator whose public content shows over-consumption or irresponsible behaviour around
alcohol may be wrong for an alcohol brand *"even if they are popular, even if they make good
content, even if they are funny."*

> "Brand appropriate does not just mean do we like them."

## On follower counts

> "yes, follower account can be one thing you look at. Now, I am not saying followers are completely
> irrelevant, but **followers are not the strategy.** The goal is not to find the biggest creator.
> The goal is to find the right creator for the job."

See `myths.md`, myth 1, for the arithmetic that settles this with a client.

## One creator can do several jobs

*"Maybe one creator can make the product demo, a trust-building testimonial, and a more direct offer
video. That certainly can work, but **it should be intentional.** You are not just hiring a person
and hoping they magically solve everything — you are casting against the job or jobs the content
needs to do."*
