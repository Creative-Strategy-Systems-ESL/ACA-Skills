# Course 202: Creator Advertising — The Strategy Before the Brief

**Ashley Vinson.** 20+ years across creative agencies, digital production, creative strategy, and
client side; former Meta and Twitter. 1h 09m, 7 lessons.

This is the **zoom-out** course. Not how to source and brief creators — how to decide what creator
content is for, before anyone is hired.

> "The goal is not just to do creators. The goal is to use creators to help brands get better
> results."

## What this skill needs from you

**Required: none, to explain any of it.** Ask a creator question and get an answer.

**To produce the Creator Casting Brief, required:**

1. **The client objective**, and how success will be measured
2. **The product or service**, and what makes it worth choosing
3. **Who it is for**, and what is going on in their life that the product speaks to

**Optional, and it sharpens the brief:**

4. Budget, and how many creators it realistically covers
5. Where the content will run, and whether paid media is behind it
6. Category constraints — alcohol, finance, health, gambling, anything with claims or age limits
7. What has been tried with creators before, and what happened

⚠️ **If the objective is missing, ask for it.** Every section of the brief derives from it, and a
brief built on a guessed objective casts the wrong people convincingly.

⛔ **But if the request is one-shot and has a deadline in it, ask AND deliver.** Write your best
reading of the objective into section 1, mark it `⚠️ ASSUMED, NOT SUPPLIED`, put the question at the
top of the document, and produce the rest. A reply consisting only of "what is your objective?" is
not a deliverable to someone who said "this week", and this is the situation the skill is most
likely to meet. **Ask-and-deliver wins over ask-and-wait unless the person is clearly still in
conversation with you.**

## The through-line

> "The question is not should we use creators? The better question is **what job can creator
> content help us do better than the brand could do on its own?**"

And the corrective that runs under the whole course:

> "A bad creator ad is still a bad ad. An authentic looking video with no clear brand or message is
> still unclear. A famous creator making boring content is still the wrong choice."

## How to run the brief

**Teach first, then produce.** Someone asking for a casting brief usually has not settled the
strategy, and the brief's fields are all downstream of it.

1. **Settle the terminology** if the request used UGC, influencer or organic. Ask which meaning.
2. **Work the five strategy questions** in **Creator strategy and casting** (below). These are the brief.
3. **Name the creator job or jobs** from the eight. Not a menu — *"You're not saying, I'll have one
   attention to trust and a side of demonstration."*
4. **Produce the brief** using **The Creator Casting Brief** (below).
5. **Say what it is for**: Ashley writes one and has the client sign it off *before anyone starts
   looking*, so "right fit" is agreed rather than argued about later.

## Answering well

**Fix the words before the plan.** *"A client says we need UGC. Someone else says we need
influencers and someone else says, can't we just find someone with like a phone... suddenly
everyone thinks they were talking about the same thing, but they are not."* Organic especially:
distribution or style? *"Those are completely different strategies."*

**Push back on follower counts with the arithmetic, not with an opinion.** Myth 1 in
**The nine myths** (below) has the numbers, and they are the fastest way to win this argument.

**Do not attach creator jobs to funnel stages.** *"A testimonial is not automatically awareness. A
product demo does not automatically mean consideration."* They are creative jobs, not measurement
buckets.

**Match the metric to the job.** The most common reporting failure in the course is judging an
asset against a job it was never given.

## What creators are, and what they are for

Lessons 1 and 2.

### The definitions this course uses

**A creator** is a person who makes content a brand can use in a marketing or advertising context.
That covers a UGC creator, an influencer, an expert, a customer, a founder, an employee, or a
professional creator who makes content for brands.

**Creator content** is what that person makes for the brand. It might show the creator on camera or
only the product; it might explain, demonstrate, build trust, create relevance, or drive an action.

**"Creator content" is the umbrella term throughout**, chosen deliberately because the other words
are contested.

### People-led advertising is not new

> "Brands have always, always, always used people to make products feel more relatable, credible,
> desirable, or easier to understand. Think about spokespeople, testimonials, celebrities, brand
> ambassadors,"

What changed is **how it gets made, where it shows up, and how many jobs it can do** inside a
brand's advertising system. The same idea now appears as a TikTok-style demo, an Instagram
influencer post, a founder video, a customer testimonial.

### ⛔ Three words worth settling before anything else

The language is genuinely broken, and it costs money because two people agree while meaning
different things.

> "A client says we need UGC. Someone else says we need influencers and someone else says, can't we
> just find someone with like a phone? Someone else says, let's just post it first and see if it
> picks up. And suddenly everyone thinks they were talking about the same thing, but they are not."

#### UGC — source or style?

**Originally:** user-generated content. Real customers making content unprompted and unpaid.

**In practice today:** a *style*. Casual, self-shot, native to the platform, less polished, less
obviously brand-produced.

⭐ **So UGC can mean where the content came from, or what it looks like, and the distinction
matters.** Paid creator content can look like UGC without being user-generated in the original
sense.

#### Influencer — influence over whom?

The word smuggles in an assumption: that this person can influence an audience to buy, try or care.

> "An influencer can have a million followers and influence no one to care about your product.
> Another creator can have 10,000 followers, be deeply trusted inside a niche community and sell the
> heck out of your product."

**The better question is not how many followers.** It is *"influence over whom, in what context, and
for what purpose?"*

#### Organic — distribution or style?

⚠️ **The one that causes the most damage**, because both meanings are common:

- **Organic distribution** — posted without paid media behind it. Whatever reach it gets, it gets
  from the platform.
- **Organic style** — feels natural, human, casual, native, not overly produced. It belongs in the
  feed.

> "A video can look organic, but be built very intentionally for paid media. In fact, a lot of great
> creator advertising is exactly that. It looks native and human, but it is still planned, briefed,
> edited, contract and approved and distributed as advertising."

⭐ **When a client says "we want organic creator content", ask which they mean.** *"Do they mean
organic distribution with no paid media, or do they mean organic style? Those are completely
different strategies."*

## The eight creator jobs

The framework the rest of the course runs on. What creator content can do that a brand struggles to
do on its own.

Ashley's framing of the competition: in a feed, *"your ad is not just competing with other ads, it
is competing with someone's friends, vacation, some delicious food, a cat falling off the sofa, and
my personal favorite grown men chopping wood."*

| # | Job | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Attention** | A format, tone or opening that feels native to the platform, so people are less likely to scroll away |
| 2 | **Explanation** | Making something complicated feel simpler, clearer, less corporate |
| 3 | **Demonstration** | Showing the product used, opened, tested, tasted, styled, compared, assembled, packed, cleaned, cooked with, travelled with. *"Sometimes showing is much more powerful than just explaining"* |
| 4 | **Trust** | From expertise, experience, category knowledge, community trust, or simply that it is not the brand saying it about itself |
| 5 | **Relevance** | Showing how the product fits a specific moment, audience, culture, market, lifestyle or use case. Makes it less abstract |
| 6 | **Objection handling** | Too expensive · don't understand how it works · don't believe the claim · need proof. Addressed in a more human way |
| 7 | **Variety** | Different hooks, people, tones, formats, use cases, objections and reasons to care — instead of one polished brand film trying to do everything |
| 8 | **Learning** | Which angles, messages, formats, product moments and creator styles people respond to. Makes the next round smarter |

### ⚠️ All eight are written from an acquisition posture

Every one of these describes reaching a stranger: `so people are less likely to scroll away`,
makes the next round smarter. **There is no job here about someone who has already bought.**

That is a real blind spot, and it bites on a specific and common brief: **a product people misuse,
return, or abandon after purchase.** A return-rate problem, an onboarding problem, a
churn-after-first-renewal problem — the course has no job that names any of them, and a brief filled
straight from this list produces a clean acquisition plan that drives more traffic to the same
problem and hits every metric while making things worse.

⛔ **The jobs still work; the framing has to be redirected.** A post-purchase problem is a **second
need state** attached to a **second objective**, and the same eight jobs apply to it:

| The post-purchase problem | The job that addresses it |
|---|---|
| They use it wrong and blame the product | **Explanation** — but it has to land *before* purchase, not in the box |
| They do not believe it is working | **Demonstration** of what "working" looks like, including when it is undramatic |
| They regret the price after buying | **Objection handling**, aimed at the post-purchase moment |
| They stop before the benefit arrives | **Trust** — someone who stuck with it saying how long it took |

⭐ **The timing point is the one that matters most and the course never makes it.** If the fix is
"you are using it wrong", the content has to reach them *before checkout*. By the time they read the
leaflet they have had three bad days and the return is already decided.

### ⚠️ These jobs are not funnel stages

The most useful caveat in the lesson, and the easiest to get wrong:

> "A testimonial is not automatically awareness. A product demo does not mean automatically it's consideration. A creator handling a price objection is not now bottom of the funnel."  ⭐ **These are creative jobs, not rigid measurement buckets.**

What determines the job is the brand problem, the content, the distribution, and what you are trying
to learn or achieve.

### Why creator content is not a shortcut

> "creator content does not automatically make better. Adds a bad creator ad is still a bad ad. An
> authentic looking video with no clear brand or message is still unclear. A famous creator making
> boring content is still the wrong choice, and a creator reading a script that sounds like it was
> written by the client's legal team. Well, it will not magically be authentic because they made a
> vertical video."

**Creator content is a creative tool inside a brand's advertising system.** Not a magic wand, and
not a substitute for strategy.

## The five strategy questions

Where creator work goes wrong, in Ashley's telling:

> "A client says, hey, we want to increase sales, and we think influencers can sell our products.
> And then the team goes, okay, let's go find some creators who can sell some stuff. **Whoa whoa
> whoa.**"

Before hiring, briefing, or doing anything, answer five questions. Creator content only works when
it connects the brand's goal to something real people actually care about — *"and that is the part
brands often skip."*

> "The audience is usually not sitting around thinking about your brand strategy. They are thinking
> normal human things like, I'm hungry, I need a ride home, I want a sunscreen that doesn't feel
> disgusting, I want jeans that actually fit."

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | **What is the client objective, and how will success be measured?** |
| 2 | **What do we know about the audience's need state or context?** |
| 3 | **What does the brand need to communicate in that context?** |
| 4 | **What creator job could help?** |
| 5 | **How will paid media support and distribute the content?** |

### Worked all the way through — ACA's rideshare example

**The client:** a rideshare service. **The objective:** get new riders to try the app.

> "That is the business goal, but it is not yet a creator strategy."

**1. Objective and measurement.** Not "make creator content" — get new riders to try the service.
Success might be new rider signups, first rides completed, cost per new rider, or trial from a
first-ride offer. The exact metric follows the client's KPIs.

**2. Audience need state.** Nobody thinks *"I need to try a rideshare brand today."* They think:

- I need a reliable way to get home
- I do not want to wait forever
- I do not want to pay too much
- I'm going out tonight and I do not want to deal with parking

> "Creator content should not just say what the brand wants to say, it should connect the brand
> message to what people actually need, want, feel, or are trying to solve."

**3. What the brand needs to communicate.** It is easy to use · it is reliable · there is a
first-ride offer · it is a good option for going out.

**4. The creator jobs.** Not picked from a menu — *"You're not saying, I'll have one attention to
trust and a side of demonstration."* Each connects back to objective, context and message:

- One creator **demonstrates** how easy it is to book
- One shows the service in a **nightlife moment** (relevance)
- One handles the **price objection** with the first-ride offer
- One builds **trust** by showing a real trip experience

**5. How paid media supports it.** Same business goal, different media roles: the awareness video
might buy reach or video views; the ease-of-use demo might target people who already showed
interest; the discount-code video runs closest to the download.

⚠️ **The content job and the media objective have to make sense together.**

> "You might take an awareness style creator video and run it like a pure app download app. Then you
> wonder why did it not drive all these cheap installs? ... **That is not** necessarily **a creator
> problem. That is a creative content and media mismatch.**"

In a perfect world creative strategy and media plan are built together, each informing the other.

### ⭐ The team idea

The mistake it prevents: making every creator asset say the same direct thing.

> "yes, you could be very literal and make every single creator, as it say, download the app now and
> use my code for 20% off your first drive. Hey, and sometimes you do need that direct download
> message, but **if every creator asset says the exact same thing, you are probably not using
> creators as strategically as you could.**"

The metaphor:

> "Think of it like football. Or as we'd say in America, soccer. The team has one goal, right?
> Score. But not every player has the same position. One player creates the opening, another moves
> the ball forward, another assist, another takes the shot."

The nightlife video may not make anyone download the app. It primes them, so that when the direct
offer arrives *"the app is not just some random offer coming into your feed. It already has a place
in their life."*

#### It still works on one creator

> "this does not always mean you need ten creators. Maybe you only have budget for one or even two
> creators. That is fine. **The question is still the same. What different roles can that creator
> content play?**"

One creator can make a broad awareness video, a simple booking demo, and a direct offer video.

#### ⛔ The Frankenstein ad

What happens when one asset is asked to do every job:

> "It's trying to be relatable, educational, funny, trustworthy, promotional, urgent, and conversion
> focused all in six seconds. Yeah, good luck with that."

## Casting strategy

### ⛔ Do not start searching before you know what you are casting for

> "A lot of people start looking for creators way too early."

Ashley's own confession: there is a cat groomer online she loves and is *"just dying to find the
right brand fit"* for — and he does not fit the strategy for any client she has.

> "if you start searching before you know what you need, **every interesting creator starts to look
> like a possibility** and that that is dangerous."

⭐ **The metaphor that makes it stick:**

> "Looking for creators without a clear casting brief is a little bit like, you know, doing grocery
> shopping when you are starving and you do not have a shopping list. Man, I don't know about you,
> but if I do that, I somehow come home with Oreos, Fritos, some weird sour candy, and approximately
> zero of the things I was supposed to buy. **The problem is not the grocery store. The problem is
> that I**"

**The casting brief is that shopping list.** See `the-casting-brief.md` — Ashley writes one for
projects of any size and has the client sign it off before looking at a single person.

### The six casting criteria

Once the job is known, these are what to look for.

#### 1. Can they create interesting content?

Followers do not mean they can make a useful piece of *advertising*. Look at the actual content:

- Do they know how to open a video?
- Do they hold attention?
- Do they understand pacing?
- Does their content feel natural on the platform?

#### 2. Can they communicate clearly?

Explaining, demonstrating, simplifying, showing, comparing, making the product easier to
understand. *"If the creator cannot communicate clearly, they may not be the right person."*

#### 3. Are they credible for this category?

⭐ **Credibility is not only expertise.** It might be lived experience, taste, community trust,
category knowledge, *"or simply that that they make sense in the context of the brand and message."*

#### 4. Do they fit the tone and the job you need?

> "A creator can be great and still wrong for the brand... Maybe they are too polish, too chaotic,
> too serious, too funny, too young to niche, too broad to glamorous, too casual or just totally
> wrong. **Fit matters.**"

#### 5. Do they bring something the brand cannot easily make itself?

Personality, craft, humanity, visual style, expertise, relationship to the category, community, or
the ability to make a product feel real.

> "A creator should bring something useful and distinctive, **not just be a human tripod for your
> client's script.**"

#### 6. Are they brand appropriate?

*"The less fun part, but it matters."* Can this person represent the brand, the category and the
message without creating a problem?

⚠️ **The alcohol example, given concretely:** working on alcohol brands in the US, Ashley's teams
*"generally only hired people who were at least 25"* — above the legal drinking age. And it is not
only age: a creator whose public content shows over-consumption or irresponsible behaviour around
alcohol may be wrong for an alcohol brand *"even if they are popular, even if they make good
content, even if they are funny."*

> "Brand appropriate does not just mean do we like them."

### On follower counts

> "yes, follower account can be one thing you look at. Now, I am not saying followers are completely
> irrelevant, but **followers are not the strategy.** The goal is not to find the biggest creator.
> The goal is to find the right creator for the job."

See `myths.md`, myth 1, for the arithmetic that settles this with a client.

### One creator can do several jobs

*"Maybe one creator can make the product demo, a trust-building testimonial, and a more direct offer
video. That certainly can work, but **it should be intentional.** You are not just hiring a person
and hoping they magically solve everything — you are casting against the job or jobs the content
needs to do."*

## The Creator Casting Brief

**The artifact this skill produces.**

⚠️ **Read this before producing one.** ACA ships no worksheet with Course 202. But the casting
brief is **not invented here** — Ashley names it, says she writes one for every project, and lists
the questions it must answer. **The fields below are hers. The layout is this skill's.** Say so
when you hand one over; see `provenance.md`.

> "For big and small projects, I will write a creator casting brief and have the client sign off on
> it before we even start looking for even just one person. This way, everyone is aligned on what
> kind of creators we need, what jobs they need to do, what the brand is trying to communicate, and
> **what right fit actually means.**"

### What it is for

Two jobs, and the second is the one that saves arguments:

1. **A shopping list** — so searching is filtered rather than open-ended, and every interesting
   creator does not start to look like a possibility.
2. **A sign-off** — so "right fit" is defined *before* anyone has fallen in love with a candidate,
   rather than relitigated when a name is on the table.

Produce it **before sourcing**, not after a shortlist exists.

### ⛔ Mark provenance inside the artifact, section by section

**The single most important instruction in this file.**

A filled brief leaves the building carrying a course code, an instructor's name and a derivation
line. That authority attaches to **the whole document** — including the parts nobody had a source
for. A client reads one document; they cannot tell that the need state came from twenty years of a
practitioner's experience and the budget came from a guess in the same typeface.

So every section carries one of these, and the fill prompts below say which applies:

```
SOURCE: ACA Course 202 — Ashley Vinson's question, our answer for this brand
⚠️ NO ACA SOURCE: our judgement. [what it rests on, and what would firm it up]
⚠️ ASSUMED, NOT SUPPLIED: [what was inferred, and what would confirm it]
```

**Where each applies:**

| Section | Marker |
|---|---|
| 1 · 2 · 3 · 4 · 6 (the question) | `SOURCE: ACA Course 202` |
| 5b, the six-criteria table | `SOURCE` for the criteria, `NO ACA SOURCE` for the weighting |
| 6, the specific disqualifiers | ⚠️ `NO ACA SOURCE` — patterns below are starting points, not a list |
| 7, the practical frame | ⚠️ `NO ACA SOURCE` in full |
| **Any budget, rate, timeline or contract term** | ⚠️ `NO ACA SOURCE`, always |

⛔ **Never let a number, a duration or a legal term leave this document unmarked.** The course
supplies no rate benchmarks, no timelines and no contract language, and says so. A brief that
presents those as though it did is the failure this whole file exists to prevent.

### 1. Campaign objective, and how success will be measured

> [The business outcome, not "make creator content". Then the actual metric.]

⛔ **The most common failure in the whole brief.** *"The objective is not to make creator content.
The objective is to get new riders to try the service."* If what is written here is an activity
rather than an outcome, stop and fix it — everything below inherits the error.

Name the metric too: signups, first purchases, cost per new customer, trial from an offer. Whatever
the client's KPI actually is.

### 2. Audience context and need state

> [What is going on in their life that this product speaks to, in their terms.]

Not a demographic. The human context — *"I need a reliable way to get home"*, *"I don't want to deal
with parking"*, *"I want a sunscreen that doesn't feel disgusting."*

✅ **Good:** a need someone would recognise as their own thought.
⛔ **Not good:** `Women 25-40 interested in wellness.` That is targeting, not a need state, and it
will not tell you who to cast.

### 3. What the brand needs to communicate in that context

> [The message, joined to the need above.]

The join is the point. Not what the brand wants to say, but what it needs to say *given* what that
person is trying to solve.

### 4. The creator job or jobs

> [From the eight: attention · explanation · demonstration · trust · relevance · objection handling ·
> variety · learning.]

Name them, and say which asset does which. If several jobs are needed, this is where the **team**
gets planned — different positions, one goal.

⚠️ **Not a menu.** *"You're not saying, I'll have one attention to trust and a side of
demonstration."* Each job has to connect back to sections 1 to 3.

⚠️ **And not a funnel stage.** A testimonial is not automatically awareness.

### 5a. What credibility, style, skill or point of view would help that job get done well

`SOURCE: ACA Course 202`

> [Two or three sentences. The shape of the person, written against the job.]

Written against the job. *"Do we need someone who can earn attention? Explain clearly? Demonstrate
the product? Build trust? Make the brand feel relevant to a specific audience or moment? Handle an
objection?"* Those need different people.

**Keep this short — it is the summary.** The operational detail goes in 5b, and writing the same
thing twice is the most common way this brief gets bloated.

### 5b. The six criteria, scored against this campaign

`SOURCE: the six criteria are ACA's` · `⚠️ NO ACA SOURCE: the weighting is yours`

| Criterion | What it means here | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Can they create interesting content? | | |
| Can they communicate clearly? | | |
| Are they credible for this category? | | |
| Do they fit the tone and the job? | | |
| Do they bring something the brand cannot make itself? | | |
| Are they brand appropriate? | | |

⭐ **The weight column matters and ACA does not supply it.** The course's whole thesis is that the
job determines everything — so the job has to re-rank these, and presenting six equal criteria
quietly contradicts the course it came from.

Work it out from section 4:

| If the job is | The criterion that carries most weight |
|---|---|
| **Explanation** — a counterintuitive message | *Can they communicate clearly* — by a distance |
| **Trust** in a regulated or technical category | *Are they credible*, and *brand appropriate* becomes a gate rather than a criterion |
| **Attention** at the top of a funnel | *Can they create interesting content* |
| **Objection handling** on price | *Credible* — but read it as **lived** credibility. Someone who had the same doubt and paid anyway usually beats an expert here |
| **Demonstration** | *Can they create interesting content*, specifically the physical craft of showing a thing |

⚠️ **Brand appropriate is never a low weight.** In a regulated category it stops being a criterion
and becomes a pass/fail gate applied before the others are scored.

### 6. ⭐ What would make someone wrong for this campaign, even if they are popular or good at content

`SOURCE: the question is ACA's` · `⚠️ NO ACA SOURCE: the specific disqualifiers are yours`

> [The disqualifiers.]

**The question that does the most work, and the one people leave out.** It is what stops a
well-liked candidate being argued into the campaign later.

⚠️ **ACA gives this question and one worked example, from alcohol in the US.** That example does not
transfer to most categories. The patterns below are starting points to think against, **not a list
to copy** — every real disqualifier has to come from this campaign's own job and category.

**Patterns worth checking, whatever the brand:**

| Pattern | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Cannot physically do the asset** | If a job needs the product demonstrated on camera, can they, and will they? |
| **Wrong register for the category** | An aesthetics-led voice on a clinical message; a comedy voice on a bereavement product |
| **Their own content contradicts the message** | Especially where the campaign is correcting a behaviour |
| **Will not accept paid usage or edits** | ⭐ The course treats this as an immediate remove — it signals they do not understand creator content inside a campaign |
| **Script-only, no room for their voice** | You are paying for the voice; a read-out gives you the authority and none of the credibility |
| **Wrong community, not merely wrong size** | A large following in the wrong country, or of peers rather than buyers |
| **Cannot say the awkward thing** | If a job is the price objection, they have to be willing to name the price and argue with it |

⚠️ **Category constraints belong here too.** Minimum age where the category demands it — Ashley's
teams used 25+ for alcohol in the US, above the legal drinking age. Public content that conflicts
with the brand's position. Anything that would be a problem *because of the category*, not because
of taste.

⛔ **In a regulated category, name what you cannot answer.** Health, finance, gambling, alcohol and
anything making claims carry rules this course does not contain — it says claims may need
substantiation and stops there, and it carries nothing jurisdiction-specific. **Write the open
questions into the brief as items for the client's own regulatory adviser.** Do not answer them.

### 6b. How paid media will support and distribute this

`SOURCE: ACA Course 202 — this is strategy question 5`

> [Which assets run where, behind what media, at roughly what weight.]

⭐ **This is the fifth of Ashley's five strategy questions and it belongs here, not in the annex.**
The course is emphatic — *"plan the media from the beginning"*, *"post and boost later is not a
strategy"*, and the content job and the media objective *"need to make sense together."* A casting
brief that files media next to timings has already lost that argument.

At casting stage this does not need a media plan. It needs three things settled:

1. **Is paid media behind this at all?** If not, say so — it changes who is worth casting, and the
   arithmetic in `myths.md` is the argument for changing the answer.
2. **Which asset does which media job?** An awareness asset and a direct-offer asset want different
   buys, and the mismatch between them is *"not a creator problem, that is a creative content and
   media mismatch."*
3. **Does the split of budget between content and media exist yet?** If the whole budget is going on
   creators, that is a decision someone should make deliberately rather than by default.

### 7. Practical frame

`⚠️ NO ACA SOURCE — this whole section is yours`

> [Deliverables · formats · rights to secure · rough budget · timings · what has been tried with
> creators before · anything a creator must know before saying yes.]

Not part of Ashley's six questions. Included because the production flow shows what goes wrong when
these are settled late — usage rights, edit rights, market rights and disclosure all depend on being
known up front.

⛔ **Everything in this section is unsourced, and it is also everything the client will negotiate
over.** Mark it. The course refuses rate benchmarks and contract language by design, and supplies no
timeline guidance at all — not a range, not a sequence. If you put a number or a date here, it is
yours to defend.

**What the course does give you for this section**, and it is only this:

- **Budget four things, not one** — the creator fee, the team hours to manage the process, the
  rights to use the content, and the paid media behind it. Myth 8.
- **Secure edit rights** even on the smallest budget, because they are what turns two assets into a
  testable set. Myth 7.
- ⚠️ **The course's own two pieces of budget advice do not agree.** Myth 8 says fund four buckets;
  Myth 1's worked split is 20% creator / 80% media and funds only two of them. Reconcile it
  deliberately rather than quoting whichever suits — and say which you did.

**Also record here: what has been tried with creators before, and what happened.** It has no home in
sections 1 to 6 and it changes casting more than most things in them.

## Producing a good one

**Every section derives from the one above it.** If section 4 does not follow from 1 to 3, the
brief has not done its job and no amount of good casting will rescue it.

**Fill section 6 properly or the brief is decorative.** Sections 1 to 5 describe who you want.
Section 6 is what lets you say no to someone charming.

**Say what is assumed.** If the objective, the need state or the budget was inferred rather than
given, mark it in the brief. A casting brief signed off on an assumed objective casts the wrong
people, convincingly, and nobody downstream can see the assumption.

**Keep it to what casting decisions need.** This is not the production brief — that comes after the
creator is vetted, interested and contracted, and it is covered in
`production-and-measurement.md`. Two different documents at two different moments.

**One creator can carry several jobs**, and the brief should say so explicitly when budget is tight,
rather than implying several hires.

## The seven-step production flow

### 1. Find potential creators

Sources: creator agencies · influencer platforms · talent managers · social search · referrals ·
existing customers · employees · category experts · creators already making relevant content.

TikTok and Meta both run creator marketplace tools. ⚠️ *"I would not treat any one marketplace or
tool as the magic answer. These tools can help you find people, but it does not decide who is
strategically right for your campaign."*

### 2. ⛔ Vet them properly — "the part people usually skip"

Review before you recommend, hire, or put anyone in front of a client:

- The quality of their **non-paid** content
- Tone · past brand work · audience context · category fit · credibility
- **Their comments** · reputation
- Whether anything in their public presence creates risk

> "Instagram may tell you someone is available or eligible to work through that system, but that
> does not mean they are the right person for your brand. It does not mean they are credible. It
> does not mean they are brand safe. It does not mean they have never done something your client
> would very much not like to discover later."

> "**Do the boring checks up front**, because the last thing you want is to explain to your client
> why the person you hired to promote their resort is now in the news for running a property scam."

### 3. Confirm interest, budget and basic fit

Between "we like this creator" and the final brief. Not the full production brief yet — enough that
they understand the opportunity:

- What is the brand, and the general campaign?
- What kind of content, and what job does it need to do?
- Where might it run? **Will it be used in paid media?**
- Rough budget range · expected deliverables
- Category requirements, compliance issues, or deal breakers

> "there is no point writing a beautifully detailed brief for someone who does not want to do paid
> usage, will not allow edits, cannot meet the timeline, is outside the budget, does not want to
> work in the category or something goes wait, this is an ad."

### 4. ⚠️ Negotiate the terms — where creator work gets messy

*"The non-sexy part."* Agree: final deliverables · timelines · revision rounds · **legal rights for
paid media** · partnership ad permissions · disclosure approvals · compliance · music rights ·
localisation · any releases needed.

**The failures Ashley has watched happen:**

| What went wrong | The consequence |
|---|---|
| Creator thought it was organic only | The brand cannot legally run it in paid media |
| Agreement covered the content "as is" | The brand wants ten cuts and has no edit rights |
| Rights limited by market | The client wants to run it in several markets and cannot |
| Someone appears in frame with no release | Unusable |
| Creator used music the brand has no rights to | *"Do not get me started on the time I got a creator video with a Beyonce song. Are you trying to get us sued?"* |

> "That is how creator content goes from fun and scrappy to why is there a lawyer yelling at me?"

#### Regulated categories

Alcohol · finance · health · gambling · anything making claims · anything age-restricted.

> "you do not get to say, oh, it's just a casual creator. Video **bring your friends know it is**"

- If alcohol is shown you may need category compliance and proof of age, and **the age requirement
  may be higher than the legal drinking age** depending on market, brand or platform rules
- If claims are made, they may need substantiation
- If it runs in paid media, you need the rights

⭐ **Disclosure covers any value received, not just cash.** *"Whether that's cash or free product,
snacks, access to a concert, whatever — it almost always needs to be clearly disclosed as
advertising."*

### 5. Brief them based on the job

A good creator brief is clear about: the campaign objective · the creator job · the key message ·
**the product truth** · the audience context · the must-say points · the do-not-say points · the
usage context · the format · where it will run.

⚠️ **But not a script cage:**

> "the brief should not turn the creator into a brand hand puppet. The best briefs give direction
> without strangling the creators ability to make content that actually feels like them. If you're
> hiring someone for their style and then force them to read a script that sounds like it came from
> a procurement document, then please do not be surprised when the video feels dead inside"

### 6. Produce and finalise

If casting, terms and brief were right, *"the first version should be pretty close to what you
need... the review should be a final quality and usability check, not a complete rescue mission."*

**The review checklist — as advertising, not as content:**

- [ ] Is the brand or product clear enough?
- [ ] Is the opening strong enough?
- [ ] Does it show the product clearly?
- [ ] Does it deliver the key message?
- [ ] Does it fit the format you need?
- [ ] Does it work without sound, if that matters for the placement?
- [ ] Does it stay within the platform safety zones?
- [ ] Any claims, music, legal rights, disclosure or compliance issues?
- [ ] Anything confusing, distracting, or likely to cause a problem once it goes live?

> "You are reviewing it as advertising, you know, not like vibes."

### 7. Put the content into the campaign

It should run as part of a content suite or broader paid campaign, *"not as some random standalone
creator floating around by itself. Like most ads in an algorithmic world, creator assets usually
work better when they are part of a team of ads."*

## Measurement and reporting

> "**You cannot decide whether something worked unless you first define what worked was supposed to
> mean.**"

### Measurement starts at strategy, not after the campaign

The failure it replaces:

> "The team runs creator content. They open the dashboard, they find a nice looking number and says,
> oh look, it worked... The question is not can we find a number that looks good? The question is,
> **did the content help achieve the thing we said it was supposed to help achieve?**"

The correct order: business goal → strategy → **how we will measure** → creator job(s) → media plan
→ make the content → look at the data and ask whether it did its job.

⚠️ *"Us advertising people, we're very good at finding the good looking numbers."*

**Creator content made for a brand is advertising**, so measure it as advertising — not because
every asset must drive the final sale alone, but because it should have a clear purpose, a clear
job, and a clear way of being evaluated.

### The two kinds of evidence

#### Proxy metrics — clues, not proof

Signals suggesting something might be happening: watch time, view-through rate, comments, search
behaviour, landing-page visits.

⭐ **The watch-time story, which is the whole lesson in one example.** A client used average watch
time as proof a video worked:

> "A lot of the videos did not mention or show the brand until pretty late. So if people watched an
> average of, let's say, six seconds, but the brand did not even show up until second nine, did that
> video really create brand impact? And maybe the dashboard says, hey, great watch time. But **if
> people never even got to the brand, what exactly did we achieve?**"

⚠️ **Read proxies through the media buy.** *"It is very easy to get more video views if the media
was bought for video views. It is easier to get reach if the media was bought for reach."* Do not
compare an asset in a reach buy with one in a video-view buy and pretend the numbers mean the same
thing.

#### Matching the metric to the job

| If the job was | Look at |
|---|---|
| **Awareness / relevance** | Reach, frequency, video starts, early watch time, recall signals |
| **Explanation** | Hold rate, average watch time, landing-page visits, search behaviour, comment quality |
| **Action** | Clicks, downloads, store visits, purchases, cost per new customer, discount-code redemptions |

#### Business outcome data — stronger, still needs interpretation

Downloads, first purchases, cost per new customer, new-customer data, conversion lift, brand lift,
incrementality testing, CRM data, actual sales.

⭐ **The credit problem, worked through.** Three creators: a nightlife awareness video, a
testimonial, a discount-code video. After the campaign the discount-code creator has the clearest
result, because people used the code.

> "That is useful information, but it does not automatically mean that the discount code creator was
> the only creator who mattered. Maybe that nightlife video helped prime the audience. Maybe the
> testimonial helped build trust. Maybe the direct offer worked better because people had already
> seen that other content first. **Or maybe not.**"

> "**The clearest number is not always the full story, and the biggest number is not always the most
> important number.**"

### What else moves the result

Even hard business numbers are affected by the offer, the price, the season, the website or App
Store page, the targeting, the media budget, the platform's optimisation, and other media running
at the same time.

> "So it performance is good. Do not just assume creator content deserves all the credit or all the
> blame and it performance is bad. Do not assume creator content deserves all the blame or all the
> credit."

**Platform data is one lens.** Meta, TikTok and Google each have their own attribution rules,
tracking limits, optimisation choices *"and their own incentives."* Third-party measurement helps —
for ecommerce, tools of the Triple Whale or Northbeam kind; elsewhere brand lift, conversion lift,
incrementality testing, surveys, CRM data or custom reporting. *"The specific tool matters less than
the principle: use the best evidence you can get and understand the limits of that evidence."*

### ⭐ Ask about the team, not the winner

> "a lot of clients want to isolate one creator and say, hey, did this one add work? But advertising
> usually does not work like one little lonely asset carrying the entire business on its back."

Better questions: How did the creator mix perform **as a team**? Did the creator assets help the
campaign find new pockets of response? Did they improve traffic quality? Did they help retargeting?
Did they make the direct offer more effective? Did the overall mix beat what we had before?

> "Sometimes the right answer is not this ad worked and this ad failed. Sometimes the right answer
> is **this asset played a different role.**"

### The reporting sequence

Go back to the goal → the strategy → the creator job → the media plan → then ask what evidence you
have, and whether it answers the question you set out to answer.

> "the goal of measurement is not just to crown a winner. The goal is to understand what happened,
> what role the creator content played, and what the evidence actually tells us and what we should
> do next."

⚠️ **Align on all of it before launch.** *"If you do not define these things up front, every number
becomes a potential argument. And trust me, there will be arguments."*

## The nine myths

Lesson 7. *"A lot of bad creator decisions come from a handful of repeated beliefs that sound
logical on the surface, but fall apart the second you look at how advertising actually works."*

Each of these is something a client or a colleague will say out loud. The counter-argument is the
useful part.

---

### Myth 1 — More followers means better results

> "No, no and no."

The assumption is that followers equal distribution: 100,000 followers means reaching 100,000
people. Organic reach is limited; without paid support only a small percentage of followers see it,
and seeing it does not mean watching it, remembering the brand, understanding the message, or
caring.

⭐ **The arithmetic, which is the fastest way to win this argument with a client:**

| Approach | Spend | Result |
|---|---|---|
| One large creator, 1M followers, at a **generous** 10% organic reach | $10,000 all-in | **~100,000 impressions** |
| A smaller creator, plus paid media at ~$5 CPM | $2,000 creator + $8,000 media | **~1,600,000 impressions** |

**Same budget. Roughly sixteen times the impressions.**

> "Most of the time I would rather have a mix of creators with less followers making strategically
> different content pull ups, paid media support, then spend the entire budget on one creator with a
> lot of followers and hope that algorithm is in a generous mood."

⚠️ These are ACA's illustrative figures at an assumed CPM. Rerun them with the client's real CPM
rather than quoting the numbers as fact; the shape of the result is the point.

### Myth 2 — Fame beats fit

A famous person gets attention, *"but attention is not the same thing as persuasion."* If they are
wrong for the audience, category, tone or job, *"fame does not save you. It just makes that mismatch
more expensive."*

> "**A creator that is a good fit will beat a creator that is famous every single time.**"

Not an argument against famous people — an argument against buying fame instead of fit. *"If they
also happen to be famous and you have the budget for both content and media, sure, why not."*

### Myth 3 — If we put paid media behind it, it won't feel real

Heard from clients *and* from creators. The version Ashley most objects to: *"then people will know
it's an ad."*

> "Come here, just a little closer. **They know it's an ad.**"

Two things follow:

1. **Disclosure is usually legally required anyway** when a creator has received value from a brand
   — and value is not only money. Free product, travel, access, gifted services all count. She points
   at the Kim Kardashian SEC case for anyone who thinks this is theoretical.
2. ⭐ **A creator who refuses paid promotion is a casting signal.** *"If a creator says I only do
   organic posts, I will not let a brand promote my content — I would remove them from the list of
   people to hire immediately, because that tells me they do not understand how creator content
   works inside an advertising campaign."*

> "Paid media does not ruin creator content. **Bad creator content ruins greater content.**" What
> makes it feel fake is bad fit, over-control, forced messaging, or a script no normal person would
> say out loud.

### Myth 4 — Authentic means unplanned

> "authentic does not mean random. It does not mean hey cater, just go out there and shoot some
> stuff. **Some of the best creator content is very planned.**"

The creator understands the message, the product, the audience, where it will run, what they can and
cannot say, and what job the content does. It still feels natural because they have room for their
own voice, style, pacing and judgement.

> "Authentic brand creator content means well briefed, well matched and well executed **in the
> authentic style and voice of the creator you hired.**"

Roaming, capture-what-happens content at a sponsored event can be great — *"but I would treat that
as bonus content or one part of the mix. I would not rely on let's see what they capture on the day
as the entire strategy. **Because hope is not a strategy.**"

### Myth 5 — If content looks organic, it should only run organically

The style-versus-distribution confusion again. A piece of creator content *"can absolutely look
organic and still be built for paid media. In fact, that is often the whole point."*

Strong creator advertising frequently works **because** it feels natural in the feed while being
part of a paid strategy.

### Myth 6 — Let's post it first and boost it later

The instinct is understandable: see how it does in the wild before spending.

⚠️ **But organic performance is not a clean test.** The algorithm changes, the audience changes, the
news changes, competitors post, other creators post, people are in different moods.

> "A post that gets attention one week might not get attention the next week, and even if the
> content is basically the same. So if a post gets a lot of likes, that does not mean automatically
> it's a strong ad, and if a post gets almost no response, that does not automatically mean it is a
> bad ad. It just means it was posted into one organic context at one specific moment. **That is
> information. It is not a full media test.**"

And organic-only gives you organic metrics: no controlled delivery, no clear audience testing, no
campaign-level comparison, no conversion signals, no retargeting impact, no proper ad performance
data.

⚠️ **This includes Instagram collab posts.** Useful, can appear on both handles, may help visibility
— *"but it is still relying on organic reach."*

> "You already spent money, time and effort to make the content. Give it enough media support to be
> fairly tested, fairly distributed and fairly measured. **Post and boost later is not a strategy.
> Plan the media from the beginning.**"

### Myth 7 — One creator video is enough

> "One version means one hook, one tone, one structure, one creator perspective, and **one chance.**"

Serious use of creator content in paid media usually wants multiple versions: different openings,
message angles, product moments, lengths, often different creator types. Variation gives the
platform more to work with, the audience more chances to connect, and the team more to learn from.

⭐ **Budget for it, or at least buy the edit rights.** Ashley's own fallback when budget covers only
one creator making two pieces: *"I would make sure that we had the editing rights, so I could cut
that content into shorter snippets and edit it together with other creator content or ads for the
brand."*

### Myth 8 — Creator content is cheap content

It can be more efficient than traditional production: faster, and more content variety per pound.

> "**But 'can be efficient' is not the same as cheap.**"

Still required: strategy, sourcing, briefing, feedback, editing, approvals, usage rights, legal
review, compliance, and media support.

> "the Germans have a saying and no, I will not try to attempt to say it in German, but basically it
> means **by cheap by twice.** And that is exactly what happens when brands try to save money on
> creator content, and then realize they cannot use the content properly, that it just doesn't
> perform, that the rights are wrong, the quality is weak, or the whole thing has to be"

**Budget four things**, not one: paying the creator · the team hours to manage the process · the
rights to actually use the content · the paid media to give it a real chance.

### Myth 9 — Creators can save a weak brand strategy

> "Absolutely not."

If the product benefit is unclear, the audience need vague, the message generic, the media plan weak
and the measurement plan fuzzy:

> "Creators will not magically save the work. They will just make **the same strategic problems show
> up in a more human looking format.**"

---

## The wrap-up

> "The goal is to use creators to make content that helps brands get better results from their
> advertising efforts and campaigns."

Creators can earn attention, explain the product, demonstrate how it works, build trust, create
relevance, handle objections, add variety, and bring a human point of view. But:

> "**it is not magic, it is advertising** and it works best when strategy, creative, media,
> measurement and learning are all working together. And that starts before anyone picks up a phone
> and starts, you"
