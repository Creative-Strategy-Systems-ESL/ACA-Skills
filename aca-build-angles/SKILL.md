# Build angles across all ten types

Ad Creative Academy's Course 103 artifact, produced for a specific offer. This skill generates the
angles. It does not teach the framework beyond what is needed to write each one well.

**Work all ten, including the ones that look wrong.** That is the exercise, and it is where the
value is: the forced attempt at an unlikely angle regularly surfaces a claim the obvious angles
miss. An angle you reject with a reason is a useful output. An angle you skip is a gap.

## What this skill needs from you

**Required:**

1. **The offer** — what it is, what it does, and what makes it different
2. **Who it is for**

**Optional, and several of the ten angles cannot be written honestly without them:**

3. **The price and the current promotion, if any** — Promotion angle
4. **Real customer numbers, ratings or review counts** — Social Proof angle
5. **Actual endorsements, and by whom** — Influencer angle
6. **What competitors offer and where you genuinely beat them** — Comparison angle
7. **Whether anything is actually new** — New Release angle
8. **Research on the audience** — sharpens all ten, and Emotional Benefit most
9. **What has already been run**, so the plan targets untested ground

### ⛔ Nine of the ten angles are an invitation to invent something

This is the most claim-generative artifact in the set. Ten angles × three example lines is thirty
pieces of copy, and the framework itself asks for exactly the claims that are easiest to fabricate:

| Angle | What it asks for | What gets invented |
|---|---|---|
| **Promotion** | A deal | A discount that does not exist |
| **Social Proof** | Numbers | "Join 250,000 customers" |
| **Influencer** | An endorser | A named expert who has never heard of them |
| **Comparison** | An advantage | A competitor limitation nobody checked |
| **Education** | A mechanism | A process the product does not use |
| **New Release** | Novelty | "First ever", "revolutionary" |

**Only Emotional Benefit, Functional Benefit, Gifting and Seasonality can be written from the offer
alone**, and even those go wrong if the benefit named is not one the product delivers.

So: **produce the angle for every type, but produce it as a structure with the missing fact
marked**, rather than filling the gap with something plausible. ACA's own worksheet does this — its
examples are written with `[PRODUCT]`, `[celebrity/expert]`, `[competitor]` brackets left in, and
those brackets are the design, not laziness.

✅ *"Social Proof — `[N]` customers have `[specific outcome]`. **Needs: your actual review count and
rating.** The angle works here because the audience is choosing between options and wants to know
what people like them did."*

⛔ `Social Proof — Join over 50,000 happy customers!`

The second one is a finished ad line that looks ready to ship and is a fabrication. Somebody will
ship it.

**Where the fact exists, use it and say where it came from.** Where it does not, the bracket plus
the "needs" note is the deliverable, and it doubles as a shopping list of what to go and find.

### The three angles with a sharper edge than the rest

- **Comparison** makes a claim about somebody else's product. That is the one a competitor's lawyer
  reads. Never name a competitor limitation that was not supplied, and never generate the
  comparison itself.
- **Influencer** attaches a real person's name and reputation to a product. Never invent an
  endorsement, not even as a placeholder with a real name in it.
- **Education** describes a mechanism. It must be true of the actual product, and it is the angle
  most likely to be quietly fabricated because a plausible mechanism is easy to write and hard to
  disprove from the outside.

## How to run it

1. **Confirm the offer and the audience.** Ask if either is missing; everything below depends on
   them. Name which of the optional inputs you have, because that decides which angles arrive
   finished and which arrive as structures.
2. **Write all ten.** For each: the angle applied to this offer, why it suits (or does not), and
   any facts it needs. **The 10 angle types** (below) has each type's core message, when-to-use
   conditions and psychological triggers.
3. **Say plainly which ones do not fit.** An angle that is wrong for this offer gets a sentence
   explaining why, not a forced line. Gifting for a B2B compliance tool is a reject, and saying so
   is more useful than a strained attempt.
4. **Then build the Multi-Angle Strategy** — the top three with reasons, audience-angle matching,
   combinations, and a testing plan. **The Multi-Angle Strategy Worksheet** (below) covers all four.
5. **Close with the facts needed**, gathered from step 2 into one list, so the gaps are actionable
   rather than scattered through thirty lines.

## Getting the output right

**One angle per ad.** An ad attempting three angles communicates none of them. The ten are options
to test against each other, not ingredients to combine inside one execution. The one exception is a
deliberate combination where one angle leads and the other supports, which **The Multi-Angle Strategy Worksheet** (below)
covers.

**The "why" is the output, not the line.** A line without a reason is a suggestion; a line with the
conditions under which it works is a decision someone can argue with. Every one of the ten types
carries when-to-use conditions — say which of them this offer actually meets.

**Check the angle against the awareness stage.** Education and Emotional Benefit reach earlier
stages; Promotion and Comparison assume the person already knows what the product is. An angle
aimed at the wrong stage is a good line that lands on nobody.

**Angle exhaustion is real and it is usually the actual problem.** If every ad for six months has
been Functional Benefit, the untested angles are where the upside is regardless of which performs
best in theory. Ask what has already run; the answer often reframes the whole exercise.

## The 10 angle types

The angle is the **why**: the reason someone should buy right now. The same offer aimed at the same
audience performs completely differently depending on the angle chosen, which is what makes this
the highest-leverage lever in creative.

Each type below carries ACA's core message, the conditions under which it works, the psychological
triggers it pulls, and its example applications. The worksheet's fill field for each is the same
question: **how could you apply this angle to your offer?**

⚠️ **The `[BRACKETS]` in the examples are the design.** ACA writes them with the specific fact left
out — `[celebrity/expert]`, `[competitor limitation]`, `[N] customers` — because that fact has to
come from the brand. Filling a bracket with something plausible turns a template into a
fabrication, and the result reads as ready to ship.

**Which angles need a supplied fact before they can be finished:**

| Needs a fact you must be given | Writable from the offer alone |
|---|---|
| Promotion · Social Proof · Influencer · Comparison · New Release | Emotional Benefit · Functional Benefit · Gifting · Seasonality |
| Education needs the real mechanism | |

---

### 1. Promotion
> **"Because it's on sale"**

Emphasises limited-time offers, discounts, special deals, or exclusive pricing. Creates urgency and
appeals to value-conscious customers.

**When to use:** during sales events or seasonal promotions · to clear inventory or increase
short-term revenue · when targeting price-sensitive audiences · to reactivate dormant customers

**Psychological triggers:** scarcity · loss aversion · value perception

**Examples:**
- `[PRODUCT]`: "Flash Sale: 48 hours only! Save 30% on all [PRODUCT] bundles"
- `[SERVICE]`: "Join today and get your first month free - limited spots available"
- `[APP]`: "Unlock all premium features at 50% off - this weekend only!"

---

### 2. Education
> **"Because it does this"**

Focuses on product functionality, features, and unique mechanisms. Appeals to rational
decision-making and works well for complex or innovative products.

**When to use:** for products with unique technical features · when targeting logical,
information-driven buyers · for higher-priced items that require justification · when your solution
has a unique mechanism

**Psychological triggers:** curiosity · desire for mastery and knowledge · rational problem-solving

**Examples:**
- `[TECH PRODUCT]`: "How our proprietary algorithm analyzes 50+ sleep factors to improve your rest quality"
- `[SKINCARE]`: "The science behind our patent-pending delivery system that reaches 7 skin layers"
- `[SOFTWARE]`: "See how our platform automates your workflow in 3 simple steps"

---

### 3. Social Proof
> **"Because everyone loves it"**

Leverages the wisdom of crowds and demonstrates widespread adoption. Shows that others have already
made the purchase decision and are satisfied.

**When to use:** when you have impressive customer numbers · for products with strong reviews and
ratings · in competitive markets where trust is crucial · for status-oriented products

**Psychological triggers:** conformity bias · fear of missing out · desire for belonging

**Examples:**
- `[PRODUCT]`: "Join the 250,000+ customers who've transformed their [problem area] with [product]"
- `[SERVICE]`: "Why 98% of customers renew their subscription after the first year"
- `[APP]`: "The #1 rated solution in the App Store with over 10,000 five-star reviews"

---

### 4. Influencer
> **"Because this person loves it"**

Uses trusted personalities, experts, or celebrities to build credibility. Leverages transferred
authority and trust from a **specific individual** rather than the crowd.

**When to use:** when endorsed by relevant experts or celebrities · for products where expertise
matters in the decision · when targeting communities that follow influencers · to establish
authority in new markets

**Psychological triggers:** authority bias · aspirational identity · credibility through association

**Examples:**
- `[PRODUCT]`: "The exact [product] that [celebrity/expert] uses daily for [benefit]"
- `[FITNESS]`: "Developed with Olympic trainer [name] to maximize your workout results"
- `[BEAUTY]`: "The secret weapon in [celebrity]'s skincare routine for the past 5 years"

⚠️ Distinct from Social Proof: the crowd versus one trusted person. They fail differently too, since
an influencer angle inherits that individual's reputation, good and bad.

---

### 5. Gifting
> **"Because someone you love needs it"**

Positions the product as a perfect gift for someone else. Leverages the buyer's desire to please
others and be seen as thoughtful.

**When to use:** during holiday seasons or special occasions · for products with gifting potential ·
**when targeting gift-givers rather than end-users** · for premium products that make impressive
gifts

**Psychological triggers:** altruism · social signaling · relationship reinforcement

**Examples:**
- `[PRODUCT]`: "The gift they'll actually use every day (unlike last year's present)"
- `[SUBSCRIPTION]`: "Give the gift of [benefit] that keeps giving all year round"
- `[EXPERIENCE]`: "Create unforgettable memories for someone special with our [product]"

⚠️ The buyer and the user are different people here, so the pain point that converts belongs to the
giver, not the recipient.

---

### 6. New Release
> **"Because it's new"**

Emphasises novelty, innovation, or being first. Appeals to early adopters and those who want to
stay ahead of trends.

**When to use:** for product launches or feature updates · when targeting innovators and early
adopters · when your product represents a genuine innovation · to create renewed interest

**Psychological triggers:** novelty seeking · status from being first · curiosity about new
solutions

**Examples:**
- `[PRODUCT]`: "Introducing the first-ever [product] that [unique benefit]"
- `[TECH]`: "Just released: The next generation [product] with [new feature]"
- `[SERVICE]`: "Be among the first to experience our revolutionary approach to [problem]"

---

### 7. Emotional Benefit
> **"Because it'll make you feel better"**

Focuses on the emotional transformation or relief the product provides. Speaks directly to feelings
rather than function.

**When to use:** for products that address emotional needs · when targeting emotionally-driven
buyers · for lifestyle and personal development offerings

**Psychological triggers:** desire for emotional states (joy, relief, confidence) · pain avoidance ·
identity reinforcement

**Examples:**
- `[FASHION]`: "Walk into any room with the confidence that comes from knowing you look your best"
- `[WELLNESS]`: "Finally experience the calm and peace you've been searching for"
- `[HOME]`: "The joy of coming home to a space that feels truly yours"

---

### 8. Functional Benefit
> **"Because it will make this easier"**

Emphasises practical value, efficiency, and problem-solving. Focuses on how the product makes life
easier.

**When to use:** for utility-focused products that save time or effort · when targeting practical,
solution-oriented buyers · for products with clear functional advantages

**Psychological triggers:** desire for ease and convenience · problem-solution alignment ·
practical value perception

**Examples:**
- `[TOOL]`: "Complete your [task] in half the time with none of the frustration"
- `[SOFTWARE]`: "Automate the 3 most time-consuming parts of your [process]"
- `[APPLIANCE]`: "The smart solution that handles [task] while you focus on what matters"

⚠️ Emotional and Functional Benefit are the pair most often confused. The test: does the promise
name a **feeling** or a **task**? Most products can run both, and running both as separate ads is
usually more informative than trying to blend them.

---

### 9. Comparison
> **"Because it's better than our competitors"**

Directly or indirectly contrasts your offering with the alternatives. Highlights competitive
advantages and unique selling points.

**When to use:** in competitive markets with clear alternatives · when you have specific advantages
over competitors · when targeting customers with competing options · for differentiation in
saturated markets

**Psychological triggers:** contrast effect · loss aversion · maximization tendency

**Examples:**
- `[PRODUCT]`: "Why our [product] outperforms leading brands in all 5 key performance tests"
- `[SERVICE]`: "The only [service] that guarantees [benefit] without [common competitor limitation]"
- `[SOFTWARE]`: "What our customers say after switching from [competitor]"

---

### 10. Seasonality
> **"Because now is the time of year you need this"**

Connects to seasonal needs, timely relevance, or calendar-based urgency. Creates time-specific
relevance.

**When to use:** for products with seasonal utility · during specific times of year (holidays,
seasons, events) · when targeting seasonal pain points or desires · for creating calendar-based
urgency

**Psychological triggers:** timeliness · preparedness · cultural and seasonal associations

**Examples:**
- `[SUMMER PRODUCT]`: "Be ready when the temperatures rise with the only [product] that [summer benefit]"
- `[HOLIDAY]`: "Make this [holiday] season memorable with our exclusive [offering]"
- `[SEASONAL CHALLENGE]`: "The solution to [seasonal problem] that works even in the harshest [season]"

⚠️ Distinct from Promotion, though they often ship together. Seasonality says the *need* is now;
Promotion says the *deal* is now. A seasonal angle works without a discount.
---

### Choosing between them

- **What does the evidence support?** Social Proof without numbers, Influencer without an endorser
  and Comparison without a real advantage are claims the audience can check. Write them as
  structures with the fact marked; do not fill the gap.
- **What is the awareness stage?** Education and Emotional Benefit reach earlier stages; Promotion
  and Comparison assume the person already knows what the product is.
- **What has the account already run?** Angle exhaustion is real. If every ad for six months has
  been Functional Benefit, the untested angles are where the upside is, regardless of which
  performs best in theory.
- **Does the offer actually meet the when-to-use conditions?** Each type lists them. An angle whose
  conditions are unmet is a reject with a reason, which is a better output than a strained line.

### The pairs people confuse

- **Social Proof vs Influencer** — the crowd against one trusted person. They fail differently:
  an influencer angle inherits that individual's reputation, good and bad.
- **Emotional vs Functional Benefit** — does the promise name a **feeling** or a **task**? Most
  products can run both, and running them as separate ads is more informative than blending them.
- **Promotion vs Seasonality** — Seasonality says the *need* is now; Promotion says the *deal* is
  now. A seasonal angle works without a discount.
- **Education vs Comparison** — Education explains your mechanism; Comparison asserts something
  about somebody else's. The second carries risk the first does not.

## The Multi-Angle Strategy Worksheet

The second half of ACA's sheet, and the half that turns ten filled-in options into something
someone can act on. Four parts, plus a closing notes field.

**Do not stop at the ten.** A list of ten angles is a menu. The output someone can use is: these
three first, aimed at these segments, tested this way.

---

### Your top 3 most promising angles

> Based on your product, audience and objectives, which 3 angles seem most promising?

| Angle | Why? |
|---|---|
| | |
| | |
| | |

**The "why" column is the section.** Three angles named without reasons is a shortlist; three with
reasons is an argument someone can disagree with, which is what makes it useful in a review.

A good reason cites something specific — a when-to-use condition the offer meets, a fact that
exists, an awareness stage, or a gap in what has already been run:

✅ *"Comparison. The audience is explicitly choosing between us and two named alternatives, we have
a documented advantage on delivery time, and no ad in the account has ever made a direct
comparison."*

⛔ `Comparison. It's a competitive market.`

⚠️ **Do not pick the three that produced the best-sounding lines.** The line quality of a
fabricated Social Proof angle is not evidence; it is a symptom. Rank on fit and on whether the
supporting fact exists.

---

### Audience-angle matching

> Different audience segments may respond better to different angles.

| Audience segment | Primary angle | Secondary angle |
|---|---|---|
| | | |
| | | |

⭐ **The most skipped part and where the leverage usually is.** The same offer aimed at two segments
frequently wants two different angles, and running one angle at everyone is a common reason an
account plateaus with no obvious cause.

The clearest case is **Gifting**, where the buyer and the user are different people: the pain point
that converts belongs to the giver. Any offer with a gift-giving segment needs a separate row, and
its angle is rarely the one that works on the end user.

Only fill this if segments were actually given or are genuinely evident. Inventing audience
segments to fill the table produces a plan aimed at people who may not exist.

---

### Angle combinations

> Sometimes combining angles creates powerful messaging. List 2-3 potential combinations:

> `1. [angle] + [angle] = `
> `2. [angle] + [angle] = `
> `3. [angle] + [angle] = `

⚠️ **This sits in tension with "one angle per ad", and both are right.**

A combination is **one angle carrying a second as support**, not two arguments sharing a stage.
Social Proof + Promotion is a promotion made credible — the promotion leads, the proof makes it
believable. If both angles want to be the headline, it is not a combination, it is a cluttered ad
that communicates neither.

Write each as *lead + support*, and say which is which:

✅ *"Education (lead) + Social Proof (support) = the mechanism explained, closing on the number of
people it has worked for. The proof is there to make the mechanism credible, not to compete with
it."*

⛔ `Education + Social Proof = talk about the science and the reviews.`

Combinations that tend to work, and why:

| Combination | Why |
|---|---|
| Education + Social Proof | Proof makes an unfamiliar mechanism believable |
| Comparison + Functional Benefit | The advantage is stated, then made concrete |
| Emotional Benefit + Influencer | A person embodying the feeling beats describing it |
| Seasonality + Promotion | The need is now, and so is the deal. The standard Q4 pairing |
| Promotion + Comparison | Two hard-edged claims fighting each other. Usually reads as desperate |

---

### Angle testing plan

> How will you test different angles to identify the most effective options?

A list of angles is not a plan. A plan has three things:

1. **A sequence** — which first, and why. Usually the highest-fit angle against the most untested
   ground, since a winner against an exhausted angle tells you less.
2. **A way to read the result** — what is being held constant, and what metric decides it. Angle
   tests are message tests, so they want the same offer, format and audience underneath.
3. **A rule for what happens next** — what a win means (build variations inside that angle) and
   what a loss means (was the angle wrong, or the execution?).

⚠️ **Change one thing.** An angle test where the format, the audience and the offer also moved has
not tested the angle, and the result will be read as though it did.

⚠️ **Do not specify sample sizes or durations from nothing.** Those depend on the account's traffic
and baseline conversion rate. Say what needs to be calculated, or ask for the numbers.

---

### Final notes & insights

> Any additional observations or ideas about angle strategy for this specific product.

The place for what did not fit the structure: an angle that is unavailable now but would be strong
in three months, a segment worth investigating, a claim worth substantiating because it would
unlock a whole angle.

⭐ **The most useful thing that usually goes here is the list of facts to go and find.** Every angle
marked "needs your actual review count" or "needs a documented competitor advantage" is a small,
concrete errand, and gathering them converts several structures into finished angles at once.

### What is reproduced and what is this skill's

**Reproduced as written:** all ten angle types, their core messages (*"Because it's on sale"*,
*"Because it does this"*, and the rest), every when-to-use condition, every psychological trigger,
all thirty example applications with their `[BRACKETS]` intact, and the Multi-Angle Strategy
section's four parts and closing notes field.

**This skill's, drawn from the course rather than stated by it:**

- **The claim-risk table** — which angles cannot be written without a supplied fact. Nine of the
  ten invite a specific fabrication, and this is the most claim-generative artifact in the set: ten
  angles times three example lines is thirty pieces of copy, each a potential invented number,
  endorsement, competitor limitation or mechanism. The course does not warn about this, and the
  worksheet's brackets are the only structural defence it has.
- **The instruction to keep the brackets and mark the missing fact** rather than filling it. This
  reads as ACA's intent — their examples are all written that way — but it is nowhere stated as a
  rule, and a plausible filled bracket is a finished ad line that nobody downstream can identify
  as invented.
- **The sharper edge on three angles**: Comparison makes a claim about someone else's product,
  Influencer attaches a real person's reputation, and Education describes a mechanism that must be
  true. These fail differently from the others and worse.
- **The resolution of the combinations tension.** The course says one angle per ad in the lesson
  and then asks for angle combinations in the worksheet, without reconciling them. Resolved here as
  lead-plus-support: a combination is one angle carrying another, and if both want the headline it
  is a cluttered ad rather than a combination.
- **The confusable pairs** (Social Proof vs Influencer, Emotional vs Functional Benefit, Promotion
  vs Seasonality, Education vs Comparison), the ✅/⛔ answer-quality examples, the three-part
  definition of a testing plan, and the note that the facts-to-find list is the most useful thing
  in the closing notes field.
- **The warning against inventing audience segments** to fill the matching table.

**No worked example ships with this skill.** The course provides none for this worksheet, and
inventing a fully-filled ten-angle set for an invented product would model exactly the behaviour
the skill warns against — thirty fabricated lines presented as an exemplar. The ✅/⛔ pairs
illustrate answer quality instead and are marked as such.

The split is by job:
