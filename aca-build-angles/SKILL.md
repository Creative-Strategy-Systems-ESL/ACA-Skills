# Build angles across all ten types

Ad Creative Academy's Course 103 artifact, produced for a specific offer. This skill generates the
angles. It does not teach the framework beyond what is needed to write each one well.

**Work all ten, including the ones that look wrong.** That is the exercise, and it is where the
value is: the forced attempt at an unlikely angle regularly surfaces a claim the obvious angles
miss. An angle you reject with a reason is a useful output. An angle you skip is a gap.

## What this skill needs from you

**Required:**

1. **The offer** — what it is, what it does, and what makes it different
2. **Who it is for**

**Optional, and several of the ten angles cannot be written honestly without them:**

3. **The price and the current promotion, if any** — Promotion angle
4. **Real customer numbers, ratings or review counts** — Social Proof angle
5. **Actual endorsements, and by whom** — Influencer angle
6. **What competitors offer and where you genuinely beat them** — Comparison angle
7. **Whether anything is actually new** — New Release angle
8. **Research on the audience** — sharpens all ten, and Emotional Benefit most
9. **What has already been run**, so the plan targets untested ground

### ⛔ Nine of the ten angles are an invitation to invent something

This is the most claim-generative artifact in the set. Ten angles × three example lines is thirty
pieces of copy, and the framework itself asks for exactly the claims that are easiest to fabricate:

| Angle | What it asks for | What gets invented |
|---|---|---|
| **Promotion** | A deal | A discount that does not exist |
| **Social Proof** | Numbers | "Join 250,000 customers" |
| **Influencer** | An endorser | A named expert who has never heard of them |
| **Comparison** | An advantage | A competitor limitation nobody checked |
| **Education** | A mechanism | A process the product does not use |
| **New Release** | Novelty | "First ever", "revolutionary" |

**Only Emotional Benefit, Functional Benefit, Gifting and Seasonality can be written from the offer
alone**, and even those go wrong if the benefit named is not one the product delivers.

So: **produce the angle for every type, but produce it as a structure with the missing fact
marked**, rather than filling the gap with something plausible. ACA's own worksheet does this — its
examples are written with `[PRODUCT]`, `[celebrity/expert]`, `[competitor]` brackets left in, and
those brackets are the design, not laziness.

✅ *"Social Proof — `[N]` customers have `[specific outcome]`. **Needs: your actual review count and
rating.** The angle works here because the audience is choosing between options and wants to know
what people like them did."*

⛔ `Social Proof — Join over 50,000 happy customers!`

The second one is a finished ad line that looks ready to ship and is a fabrication. Somebody will
ship it.

**Where the fact exists, use it and say where it came from.** Where it does not, the bracket plus
the "needs" note is the deliverable, and it doubles as a shopping list of what to go and find.

### The three angles with a sharper edge than the rest

- **Comparison** makes a claim about somebody else's product. That is the one a competitor's lawyer
  reads. Never name a competitor limitation that was not supplied, and never generate the
  comparison itself.
- **Influencer** attaches a real person's name and reputation to a product. Never invent an
  endorsement, not even as a placeholder with a real name in it.
- **Education** describes a mechanism. It must be true of the actual product, and it is the angle
  most likely to be quietly fabricated because a plausible mechanism is easy to write and hard to
  disprove from the outside.

## How to run it

1. **Confirm the offer and the audience.** Ask if either is missing; everything below depends on
   them. Name which of the optional inputs you have, because that decides which angles arrive
   finished and which arrive as structures.
2. **Write all ten.** For each: the angle applied to this offer, why it suits (or does not), and
   any facts it needs. `reference/angle-types.md` has each type's core message, when-to-use
   conditions and psychological triggers.
3. **Say plainly which ones do not fit.** An angle that is wrong for this offer gets a sentence
   explaining why, not a forced line. Gifting for a B2B compliance tool is a reject, and saying so
   is more useful than a strained attempt.
4. **Then build the Multi-Angle Strategy** — the top three with reasons, audience-angle matching,
   combinations, and a testing plan. `reference/strategy.md` covers all four.
5. **Close with the facts needed**, gathered from step 2 into one list, so the gaps are actionable
   rather than scattered through thirty lines.

## Getting the output right

**One angle per ad.** An ad attempting three angles communicates none of them. The ten are options
to test against each other, not ingredients to combine inside one execution. The one exception is a
deliberate combination where one angle leads and the other supports, which `reference/strategy.md`
covers.

**The "why" is the output, not the line.** A line without a reason is a suggestion; a line with the
conditions under which it works is a decision someone can argue with. Every one of the ten types
carries when-to-use conditions — say which of them this offer actually meets.

**Check the angle against the awareness stage.** Education and Emotional Benefit reach earlier
stages; Promotion and Comparison assume the person already knows what the product is. An angle
aimed at the wrong stage is a good line that lands on nobody.

**Angle exhaustion is real and it is usually the actual problem.** If every ad for six months has
been Functional Benefit, the untested angles are where the upside is regardless of which performs
best in theory. Ask what has already run; the answer often reframes the whole exercise.
