# Course 105: The AI-Powered Creative Strategist

**Reza Khadjavi**, CEO of Motion, interviewed by **Evan Lee**, Motion's head of partnerships.
27m 49s, 1 module.

This is the course about **the role itself** rather than the craft. Where it came from, why brands
need it, what AI does and does not change about it, and how to stay valuable while the ground
moves.

## What this skill needs from you

**Required: none.** This is a conversation about the shape of the job. It is worth hearing before
you have a specific question.

**Optional, and it sharpens the answer:**

1. **Where you sit** — in-house, agency, freelance, founder wearing the hat
2. **What you are actually worried about** — your job, your team's structure, where to start with
   AI, whether to hire for this
3. **What you have already tried with AI**, and what happened

Career questions are personal, and a generic answer to "will AI take my job" is worth very little.
Ask what their situation is if they have not said, but **answer the question first**. Someone
asking this is usually anxious, and a question in reply reads as evasion.

## The through-line

Reza's argument in one shape:

> **The role was born when the constraint moved, and AI is the constraint moving again.**

Pre-2020, targeting was the biggest revenue lever. *"If I'm a yoga brand and I can figure out how
to get in front of yoga enthusiasts, that was probably the biggest revenue unlock."* Then iOS 14
happened, and, more importantly, the distribution algorithms got very good at targeting on their
own. That opened a question: **where is the revenue impact now?**

> "And then everyone very immediately understood that creative was where the big revenue unlock
> was."

The creative strategist emerged to bridge two disciplines that did not talk: media buyers with *"a
million spreadsheets"* who were *"huge data nerds"*, and creative teams *"just making stuff... not
rooted in actual decisions from the data."*

> "These were the types of people that were like almost unicorns... they had a bit of both traits."

**The AI parallel is the point of the course.** The same thing is happening again: a discipline
being added to an already-hybrid role.

> "To imagine that role without AI at this point is now pretty crazy."

## Two ideas worth leading with

**1. Get the process right, then apply AI to parts of it.** The most actionable thing in the
course, and the failure it prevents is extremely common:

> "I can spend 20 minutes with AI and I have like 2000 ideas and maybe a bunch of like ads that are
> generated, but **are those going to produce more winners**"

Volume was the old constraint and AI solves it. That does not help if what gets produced is not
rooted in anything. The measure of the role is unchanged: *reliably and predictably produce more
winning ads.*

**2. The role does not require the title.**

> "At least someone at the team has to wear the hat of creative strategist. REZA It could be the
> media buyer, could be the founder, could be the head of marketing. Actually, it doesn't really
> matter who does it, **but the work does need to be done,**"

Useful for a small brand deciding whether to hire, and for someone arguing their existing job
already contains this work.

## Answering well

**Career questions deserve a direct answer, not a hedge.** Reza's is genuinely nuanced: yes, some
jobs go; the bigger change is team structure; here are the two things that keep you valuable. That
is more useful than reassurance and more useful than doom. Give the actual position.

**Be concrete about the first step.** "Start using AI" is not advice. Reza's version is: take one
repetitive weekly task that is basically mindless, and automate as much of it as you can with the
best tool available now. That is a Tuesday afternoon, and it is deliberately small because the
point is the confidence it builds.

⚠️ **Sequence this correctly for someone who is not technical.** The two career skills include
"I've built an AI agent, I've hired a couple of AI agents", and read cold by someone whose AI
experience is ChatGPT that is not a next step, it is a reason to give up. **Lead with the one
tangible move, then explain the two skills as where it leads.** The agent line is a description of
where this ends up, not an entry requirement.

For a creative strategist, the repetitive weekly tasks worth automating first are usually the
research-gathering ones, because that is the 90% and it is genuinely mechanical: pulling and
clustering reviews, summarising comment threads, transcribing competitor ads, assembling the weekly
performance readout. All of those are ChatGPT-level work. None require building anything.

⚠️ **Do not let the reassurance drift.** The source states the hard part once (some jobs go) and
then spends most of its length on agency and control, which reads as comfort even though it is not
framed as comfort. Someone genuinely at risk deserves the risk stated plainly and once more at the
end, not buried under things they can do. Say what is uncertain, say who can actually answer it
(their own leadership, not this course), then give them the moves.

**Do not oversell the AI half.** Reza spends much of the interview on what has *not* changed, and
the foundations material is where the durable value is. Someone who takes only the AI content away
has taken the half with the shortest shelf life.

## Jobs, teams and what keeps you valuable

The part people actually came for. Reza's answer is neither reassurance nor doom, and the nuance is
the useful bit.

### "Will AI take my job?"

His answer, unhedged:

> "**I think it'll take some jobs,** but I think it's more a question of all of our jobs just
> evolving and changing. If you if you even just think about the job titles that exist today, if you
> were to go back 15, 20 years, how many of those job titles would even exist then? Right? So I
> think **I think it will take some jobs, it'll create some new**"

But that is not where he puts the emphasis:

> "**the biggest change is just going to be in the structure of teams.** I don't know how long it
> takes for this to play out. What's very likely to happen is that you won't need to hire as many
> people as you as you had in the past. So like a much leaner, smaller team can get a lot more"

The concrete shape of it:

> "what's more likely is that **a 20 person company could probably stay 20 people and do the work of
> 40** or a 40 person company. You could probably stay 40 and do the do the work of 80."

⚠️ **Read what that implies.** The risk is less "you are fired" and more "the role above you is
never opened, and the team you expected to build does not get hired." The threat lands on growth
and progression before it lands on employment, which changes what you do about it.

### ⭐ The two skills

> "If you want to be valuable to your organization, it's probably two really core important skill
> sets."

**1. Rising to a higher level of abstraction.**

> "if you think about a junior role, if you want to move up in your career, how do you rise to a
> higher level of abstraction? So you're not just staying at like junior execution roles?"

**2. Scaling that.**

Historically, scaling meant people:

> "if you go to a company and you're doing really well and you're creating a lot of value,
> oftentimes what happens is you can create more value by saying, now if I grow my team to ten
> people... I've risen to this level of abstraction. REZA And then now I've scaled my team."

Now the second half can be done without headcount:

> "instead of hiring all of these people, what you can do instead is you've risen up to this level
> of abstraction. You're able to add strategic, high leverage value. You're able to help the
> leadership of your team get more done with less people. So you say that, **hey, instead of hiring
> two more people, I've built an AI agent, I've hired a couple of AI agents, and I'm doing a bunch
> of stuff myself.** REZA **And guess what? We don't need those five other people that** that **we
> thought we might**"

His summary:

> "**If you can learn how to help your organization stay lean and scale without necessarily adding
> headcount, then you'll be very, very valued at that organization.**"

He is also optimistic about the aggregate effect:

> "Hopefully what ends up happening is that a lot more companies get created. **So if you're ever at
> all thinking about starting a company, this is probably a good time to do that.** But my guess of
> what happens is that there's a lot more companies, but those companies are smaller."

### ⭐ How to actually rise a level

The concrete method, and it is refreshingly unmystical:

> "having an understanding of **the 1 or 2 levels above you, what do they care about? What are they
> thinking about? What are their priorities** becomes really important. Often when we're at an
> organization, we're kind of just thinking about our level and down what is in front of me, what do
> I need to get done?"

Why it works:

> "**If you as an individual in an organization can contribute to one of the most important
> problems of the organization, then you will have created value for that organization, and they
> will want to make sure to keep you there.**"

> "if you know it, then in your day to day work, you can think about how to contribute to that.
> Often the big issue is that we just don't know, like we're not paying attention to that."

And the failure it prevents:

> "that's how you can wake up one day being surprised and be like, oh my God, what? REZA Everything
> is so different now and my job is at stake... and then we can get surprised when priorities shift,
> and we're not adding as much value as we thought we were."

**Note how this connects to the CEO whisperer idea in `the-foundations.md`.** A creative strategist
doing real market research already holds information leadership wants. Routing it upward is
simultaneously the research practice and the career move; they are the same action.

### Use AI in your personal life

His first piece of closing advice, and it is not filler:

> "everybody in their own personal life should be using AI a ton, right? And I think actually
> getting into the habit of outside of work, just starting to get immersed in it as a way of just
> how you operate, I think is really important because then it's not just related to work."

> "**if we are the type of individuals that are just using AI in so many different aspects of our
> day to day, then it'll start to come very naturally.** So I'd say one of the, one of the key areas
> is just if you find yourself using AI more and more, just generally in your life, and that's
> probably a good indication that you're in, you're"

The bar he sets, via analogy:

> "imagine, for example, somebody asks in the modern era, if you're a knowledge worker, **how do you
> use Google?** REZA It's like, what do you mean? What kind of question is that? It's just it's so,
> so much a part of what I do. **I think that is the right feeling for AI.**"

That is a genuinely useful self-test. If using AI still feels like a decision, it has not become
infrastructure yet.

### ⭐ The one tangible starting move

If someone wants a single action, this is it:

> "a very tangible idea is **take one part of your work that is pretty repetitive, that you seem to
> be going back to every single week. That is kind of a mindless thing for you,** and it's just it's
> just repetitive and try to take just that one thing. And with the best tools that you can find at
> the time that you're trying to do this, what's the best way that you can automate as much of that
> thing as possible?"

And why it is deliberately small:

> "**Once you do that, then it becomes pretty addictive** and you're like, oh, now what else can I
> do? And I think that basically starts the trend of, how do I look at everything that I do and find
> more and more leverage with AI. So solving that one specific use case now gives you the confidence
> to say, okay, I can start to use AI more and more in my in my day to day and **find ways to
> automate the things that are really repetitive so that I can rise up and do things that are more
> strategic and** more **higher value.**"

Note the loop closing: automating repetitive work is not the goal, it is how you buy the time to
operate at the higher level of abstraction that the two skills call for.

### How he closes

> "Over the next several months and years, it's getting to the point where **all of humanity
> basically has superintelligence available on tap**... And what's interesting about it is that
> **everybody has access to it in the exact same way.** REZA So it's it's just like great equalizer.
> So it's not like some individuals or teams are going to have access to this advanced technology
> that the rest of us won't."

> "And then the question becomes, what do you do with it? Right. And I think, that is a real
> differentiator **in a world where everybody has access to the same capability.** REZA **It's a
> really great opportunity for people to differentiate and create a ton of value by being really,
> really good at creating value using the super**"

> "It's a really chaotic time right now. So much is happening, so many things are changing really
> fast, but it's also a time of tremendous opportunity."

⚠️ This is the most speculative passage in the interview and reads as a founder's closing note.
Quote it as his outlook rather than as a forecast. The argument underneath it, that equal access
makes application the differentiator, stands on its own regardless of what one thinks of
"superintelligence on tap".

## Applying AI, with discipline

The operational half of the interview. Reza's position is not that AI changes the job, it is that
AI has to be pointed at a job that already works.

### The measure has not moved

Start here, because everything else is judged against it:

> "if you think about defining success for the role, in some ways **all that matters is to reliably
> and predictably produce more winning ads,** right? That's like that's the goal."

Efficiency counts only in service of that:

> "there's an element of this. That is, if we can work more efficiently, then that's helpful. REZA
> But it needs to be working more efficiently towards **shipping more winning ads.**"

### ⭐ The volume trap

The most useful warning in the course, because it describes what most teams are currently doing.

Volume used to be the binding constraint:

> "oftentimes people who are getting started with all of this volume was their first big challenge.
> They're like, I need to produce 50 ads a week or something, and how am I going to do that? And
> yeah, creative AI is going to help accelerate that. **But then there's still the big question of
> what are those going to be?**"

And with AI, the trap arrives fast:

> "it's now easier than ever to like, come up with new ideas, use AI to generate a bunch of ads
> also, and like it can cut quite a bit into the efficiency and allow us to now. Okay, **I can spend
> 20 minutes with AI and I have like 2000 ideas** and maybe a bunch of like ads that are generated,
> **but are those going to produce more winners** I think becomes the question."

The older version of the same problem, which predates AI:

> "Even in the early days of creative strategy, the focus point for everyone was like, yes, we can
> just go and produce 100 ads this month, **but if they're not rooted in something, then we're just
> guessing.**"

⚠️ **This is the diagnosis for a team producing large amounts of AI creative with nothing winning.**
The output is not the problem. AI solved a constraint that was never the real one, and removing it
made the underlying problem more visible rather than worse.

### ⭐ Process first, then automate

The rule that follows, and the most actionable thing in the interview:

> "rooting it back to like if we've dialed our process and we're like, okay, forget AI doesn't
> exist, but we have a team. And here's our process for the way we do research, the way we analyze
> ads. So we we brainstorm, we... And those are done in a way where as we do them, they are taking
> us closer towards predictably producing more winners, winners, documenting our learnings, coming
> up with hypotheses that are based on data,... **that process needs to be there.** And then one by
> one, start to think about, how can I help with this? How can I help with different parts of this,"

> "**you want to have the fundamental process in place and then find ways that I can make it more
> efficient.**"

And the temptation it guards against:

> "it's very tempting to just open up an AI tool or something. REZA And just start riffing. But the
> right way for it to be in the service of producing more winning ads, it needs to be chipping away
> and helping us get more efficient at **things that we're already doing that we know are helpful**
> towards towards producing more winners."

**The practical test for any AI addition:** which step of an existing process does this make
faster or better? If the answer is "it makes new things", it is exploration rather than
improvement, which is fine as long as it is labelled honestly.

The process elements he names, worth using as a checklist for what should exist before automating:

- How we do **research**
- How we **analyse ads**
- How we **brainstorm**
- How we **prioritise**
- How we **document learnings**
- How we form **hypotheses based on data**

### Tinkering, and the trap inside it

Reza is strongly in favour of tinkering:

> "one of the things to do is to, is to make sure that we're spending time at the frontier, trying
> out different tools, trying out to automate different workflows, and like just generally tinkering
> a lot with AI. And I think that's very"

And then names its failure mode precisely:

> "one thing that often happens when you start to do that is you get kind of lost a little bit. It's
> like, wait, I'm now deep in the weeds and I've got these like eight workflows that I'm trying to
> automate... **But then there's this question of what am I even trying to automate?** REZA Like,
> **what's all this workflow meant to even accomplish?**"

The test:

> "**if you're not able to answer the question of what is it exactly that I'm trying to accomplish,
> and is that thing useful, then just know that,** you know, that's **this is just exploration.**
> We're just kind of having"

Exploration is allowed. Mislabelling it as work is what costs weeks:

> "if we spend weeks and weeks and weeks trying to automate this workflow, only at the end to
> realize is that even the right way to do it wasn't even useful..."

### ⭐ The wait-for-the-tech heuristic

An unusually practical filter, and one most AI advice omits:

> "There are lots of people building stuff, lots of companies building things that are going to work
> a little bit better out of the box... **sometimes just waiting a little bit for the tech to get
> better solves a lot of problems too.** And so keeping that in mind is that is this something that
> is likely going to get solved by one of the different tools that I'm using? REZA **If it is
> something that's going to get solved by somebody else, then maybe this is not the right thing for
> me to go deep into and try to solve.**"

Before building an elaborate custom workflow, ask whether it will be a checkbox in a product within
six months. If so, the effort is better spent on something that will not be commoditised.

### Where this ends up: agents as contractors

> "I think starting to get into the phase where a bunch of things with AI are just going to work
> well out of the box. ... And once they do, then it becomes, **how do I then use these different
> things almost as the way that you would use like contractors, right.** If you have these different AI agents
> that start to become effective and useful, then your job becomes, how do I leverage different AI
> agents to get things done for me?"

The general form he lands on:

> "The core premise is there's a bunch of things available that do stuff, and **how do I put that
> together to create the outcome that I want.** And as long as the thing that you're really focused
> on is what is the outcome that I'm driving towards? What's the thing that creates a lot of value?
> REZA Then you can scan for whether it's different AI tools or agents or apps or softwares or
> contractors or team members, like **what are the different resources that I can put together to
> accomplish the thing that I**"

Note that agents sit in the same list as contractors and team members. The skill being described is
resourcing, not prompting, and it is the same skill a manager already has.

> "But if we lose sight on the thing that we're trying to accomplish, then we get really lost."

## The role: where it came from and why brands need it

### The origin

Reza dates the role precisely, and the reason it appeared is a shift in where the money was.

> "if you go back just a few years, maybe like to 2020 and 2021, **I don't think anyone even knew
> what a creative strategist was,** right? Like it was a brand new role."

Before that, for most of digital marketing's history, targeting was the lever:

> "Probably the thing that was the most important when it came to revenue impact was around audience
> targeting. So if I'm if I'm a yoga brand and I can figure out how to get in front of yoga
> enthusiasts, that was probably the biggest revenue unlock."

Then two things happened at once. iOS 14 removed a lot of the data, **and** the algorithms got good
enough that targeting stopped being a competitive edge anyway:

> "the even bigger shift that happened was that the distribution algorithms just got really good at
> targeting. Right? So up until that point, if so much time and energy went towards targeting the
> right person, and now all of a sudden the algorithms are just really good at doing that. They
> basically opened up this big question of, okay, well **where's the big revenue impact?**"

> "And then everyone very immediately understood that **creative was where the big revenue unlock
> was**."

⚠️ Note the second half of that. The usual telling is "privacy changes broke targeting". Reza's
version is that the algorithms also got good enough to do it themselves, which matters because it
means the shift is not reversible by better data. Targeting stopped being where the advantage lives
for two independent reasons.

### The bridge

The role appeared to solve a specific organisational problem: two groups who both mattered and did
not speak the same language.

> "if you take the media average media buyer at that time, they had like a million spreadsheets and
> they were huge data nerds. And then you had the creative team all the way on the other side. That
> was just making stuff.... **that was not rooted in actual decisions from the data.** And so
> creative strategists emerges as the bridge between these two worlds."

Who filled it:

> "these are the individuals who, like, naturally, were pretty good at being part analytical, part
> creative. We all we all know people like that, whether they started out on the media buyer side or
> on the creative side. These were the types of people that were like **almost unicorns,** a little
> bit in the sense that they had a bit of both"

**This is the frame that makes the AI argument work.** The role was always a hybrid, formed by
someone pulling two disciplines together. Adding a third is a continuation, not a mutation.

> "In some ways, it's just a continuation of that and saying, okay, these, these almost like
> **polymath type individuals** need to figure out, okay, now how do I work AI into the mix as
> well?"

### Why a brand needs it

Paid advertising is not optional for an online business:

> "if you're selling anything online, paid advertising is going to be like absolute critical channel
> to get right. And so from that standpoint, getting paid advertising as a channel is basically a
> non-negotiable for an online brand."

And the biggest component of that channel is the creative:

> "The biggest component to that is your **creative flywheel,** right. Because at this point, if
> you're spending anything north of like even 20, 30, 40 or $50,000 a month in media spend, the
> amount of creative that you need to run that kind of a budget is pretty intense."

### In-house or agency

Reza is not anti-agency, and the distinction he draws is the useful part:

> "in the early days, like, you might just go and hire an agency, and I think you can still do that
> today. And I think that's fine. There are a lot of terrific agencies, but there are some things
> that feel like they need to be core competency. You know, they **they say that** you **you want to
> outsource execution, but strategy and direction**"

And having it in-house makes external partners better, rather than redundant:

> "even when you work with content creators or, performance agencies, you will set them up to do
> even better work, right? REZA Because they, they would love to work with a brand that has that
> kind of expertise"

### ⭐ The title does not matter, the work does

The most practically useful thing in this section, especially for a small brand deciding whether to
hire:

> "You don't have to have the role of creative strategists. I think about at a certain organization
> size, it makes sense to hire that full time. **At least someone at the team has to wear the hat of
> creative strategist.** REZA **It could be the media buyer, could be the founder, could be the head
> of marketing. Actually, it doesn't really matter who does it, but the work does need to be done,**
> and it should be done in-house at the brand,"

Two ways this gets used:

- **A brand too small to hire** does not get a pass. Someone still has to do the work, and naming
  who is the decision that matters.
- **Someone whose job title is something else** can make the case that they are already doing this
  work, which is often the honest description of a media buyer or marketing lead at a growing
  brand.

### Why the bar moved

Context for anyone who remembers an easier era:

> "those early days of like, I'm going to open up an online store, run some ads, do some
> dropshipping, and make a ton of money, like **those days are obviously gone.** REZA And the reason
> is that the market has matured. There's a lot of there's a lot of people doing that. **The bar has
> gone up.**"

> "You're up against a ton of really good performance marketers."

This is the commercial argument for the whole discipline: the work is now hard enough that doing it
well is a genuine advantage, which is precisely why it justifies a dedicated person.

## The foundations, which have not changed

The half of the interview with the longest shelf life. Reza's argument is that the modern job has
circled back to the oldest question in advertising.

### The loop back to Ogilvy

> "if you think about what happened in the era before digital marketing, David Ogilvy, my Mad Men
> era, you would have these advertising slots. It could be a billboard, it could be a TV spot. You
> would hope that your audience is going to hear this. REZA **And the question is, what do you want
> to say to them?** That's like the core of the principle of advertising is around messaging, let's
> say of like, what am I going to say to this audience? Who is my audience? And what am I going to
> say to them?"

Then digital inverted it for two decades:

> "the first couple of decades of digital marketing kind of flipped that on its head a little bit,
> because that didn't matter as much. REZA It was more like, if I have a yoga brand, if I can get in
> front of a yoga enthusiast, that's going to do so much of the heavy lifting, that maybe
> **messaging kind of comes secondary.**"

And now it has inverted back:

> "because the algorithm is taking care of the distribution in some ways it's very similar. In some
> ways it's like, just don't worry too much about how this gets in front of the right person. **Just
> think about what is it you want to say to them.**"

So the fundamental question is the pre-digital one:

> "**Who is my customer, what am I selling, and what do I want to say with regards to what I'm
> selling?**"

He immediately notes why that simple-sounding question is hard:

> "when you have dozens of competitors who are selling the same thing, then you can't all say the
> same things and you have to differentiate. REZA And then within your customer base there's
> different, use cases or there's different value props or different like audience segments that
> might resonate to your product in a different"

### ⭐ The chef metaphor: 90% of the work is collecting inputs

The single most quotable idea in the interview, and the one that reframes what the job actually
consists of.

> "you can think about **90% of the work being around collecting inputs, almost like a chef that
> wants to create a meal** is that you're going and collecting a lot of different ingredients,
> inputs."

That collection phase is deliberately unglamorous and methodical:

> "that's let's say it's like the science part of it where it's methodical. It's like, I need to
> understand x, y, z about my customer. REZA I need to understand how my competitors are positioning
> and what our advantages are against them. So that's like a very scientific information gathering"

What goes into it, in his description:

- Deep research on **who the customers are**
- **What else is going on in the market**
- Competitor **positioning**, and where your advantages sit against it
- Immersion in the ecosystem's own language. He credits Mirella's approach here: her creative
  strategists *"consume a ton of organic content just to understand what people in that ecosystem
  are saying, how they think, how they speak"*

Only then does the creative part start:

> "There's a lot of creativity... it's like, okay, we know the points that we need to hit. We got
> that pretty nailed. **What are the thousand different ways that we can engage with this
> message?**"

And he is honest that this half resists systematising:

> "The fundamentals of how to be creative are probably a little bit more difficult to pin down. But
> that's why you need individuals who are naturally creative, that **when they have the right
> ingredients**, then it's kind of just about brainstorming."

⚠️ **The 90/10 split is the practical claim.** If most of the work is gathering ingredients, then a
team that is stuck on ideas usually has an inputs problem rather than a creativity problem, and
more brainstorming will not fix it. It also explains why AI-generated volume disappoints: it
accelerates the 10% while leaving the 90% untouched.

### ⭐ The CEO whisperer

Credited to James, Motion's head of content. The argument for why research should travel upward
rather than only into ad briefs.

> "If you learn a ton about the market and you can go back to the company's leadership and say the
> things that we're learning here can **inform our product strategy and product direction**."

Reza frames this as a return to how it worked historically:

> "Hopefully there's a discussion internally around the things that we're learning in market
> research or are informing our product strategy even. Right. So having a situation where because
> that's how it would work in those olden days too, right? Where they would learn things about the
> market."

**Why this matters for anyone trying to make the case for the role.** A creative strategist doing
serious research accumulates the most current, least filtered picture of the market that exists
inside the company. Most of that never leaves the ad account. Routing it to leadership converts a
marketing function into a strategic one, and it is the single strongest argument for the role's
seniority.

It also connects directly to the career advice in `career-moves.md`: contributing to what
leadership actually cares about is exactly what "rising to a higher level of abstraction" means in
practice.

### Connecting the two halves

> "we have these products that we're selling. REZA **Understanding how to connect the dots between
> those two things.**"

That connective step is the job. Not the research on its own, which is a report, and not the
creativity on its own, which is guessing.

Then the execution principles he gestures at:

> "some of it becomes around like humor and getting attention and structuring your creative in a way
> that follows the the attention span that people have that you need to, like, hook them with
> something and then drive them to an offer."

He does not go deeper than that, and says so: there are principles, but they belong to other parts
of the curriculum.
