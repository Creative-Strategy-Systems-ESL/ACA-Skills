# Why nothing is original, and why that is good news

Module 1: *The Pattern Recognition Playbook* (11m 23s).

## The premise

> "I need to tell you something that might sting a little. You were not original. That concept
> you're so proud of, someone did it before you. That hook you wrote last week that absolutely
> crushed? There was a version of it from 2019 that you saw, and you probably forgot about that big
> creative idea that you pitched in your last brainstorm. It was floating around in your
> subconscious because you scroll past something similar at 11 p.m. three days ago."

> "And here's the thing. That's not a problem. That's actually how creativity works. Every great
> idea in the history of advertising was built up the back of something that came before it. Every
> single one. and the people that figured out first they won."

**Where this came from.** Before Foreplay, Zach ran a DTC apparel brand. The weekly ritual was the
whole team on a call trying to share competitor ads they had seen, *"screenshots in Slack, broken
links and half-remembered TikToks. It was absolutely chaos."*

> "But here's what we noticed. The weeks where we did the most research, where we actually studied
> what was working in the market, those were the weeks we produced our best creative. Every time.
> Not because we were copying anyone. Because we were pattern matching. We were giving our brains
> better raw material to work with."

⛔ **This is the causal claim the whole course rests on, and it is an anecdote.** More research
weeks produced better creative at one brand, observed by the person who then built and sells a
research tool. It is plausible and it is not evidence. Carry it as his experience.

## Act 1: The greatest ads were stolen

### Ogilvy and Rolls-Royce, 1958

David Ogilvy lands the Rolls-Royce account. *"This is the assignment of a lifetime. And what does he
do? Does he lock himself in a room and wait for divine inspiration? No. He spends three weeks
reading."* Every car magazine, every technical review, every piece of documentation about
Rolls-Royce. He studies the competition. He studies engineering.

Buried in a review in the British magazine **The Motor**, from a technical editor:

> "At 60 miles an hour, the loudest noise comes from the electric clock."

> "Ogilvy didn't write that line. He found it. He took it. He polished it into a headline that
> became the most famous ad in automotive history."

**Sales went up 50% that year**, and it launched the wave of clients that built his agency.

⭐ **And then the twist, which is the actual point of the story.** An ad from 25 years earlier, for a
car called **Pierce-Arrow**, used an almost identical line: *"the only sound one can hear in a
Pierce-Arrow is the ticking of the electric clock."* When the original agency's creative director
wrote to Ogilvy about it, Ogilvy said his version came from a magazine review.

> "The father of advertising's greatest headline was borrowed from a magazine editor, and it might
> have even been borrowed twice."

**Ogilvy's own instruction**, which Zach quotes as the origin of the whole method:

> "Study the precedents. Look at the campaigns for similar products in recent years. Find out which
> worked and which didn't, and then go do it better."

> "That's not copying. That's craft."

### "Just Do It"

Dan Wieden, co-founder of Wieden+Kennedy, the night before Nike's first major TV campaign. He had
five spots from five different creative teams and no unifying thread.

His source: the last words of **Gary Gilmore**, a convicted murderer facing a firing squad in Utah
in 1977. Gilmore said *"Let's do it."*

⚠️ **Wieden is often reported** as joking that he did not like the *let's* part, and would otherwise have given them credit. **Widely repeated, and not in ACA's material** — do not present it as a quotation.

**Both audiences he showed it to rejected it.** His own agency said, quoting Zach, *"we don't need
that."* Phil Knight at Nike had the same reaction. Wieden pushed for it anyway.

**Within ten years Nike went from 18% market share to 43%.**

> "The most valuable tagline in sports marketing history came from a death row inmate championed by
> one stubborn copywriter who was smart enough to recognize pattern when he saw one"

⚠️ **The second half of this story is the part people drop.** Recognising the pattern was necessary
and not sufficient; the line only survived because someone defended it against two rejections. A
research process that surfaces good patterns into an organisation that kills them produces nothing.

## Act 2: The direct-response brands you think are original

Zach anticipates the objection: *"Okay Zach, but that's old advertising, legacy stuff. The brands I
work with, the brands I admire, they're actually doing original things."*

### Liquid Death

Mike Cessario was at Warped Tour in 2008 watching punk bands. He noticed energy drink companies were
sponsoring every band, and the musicians were **contractually obliged to drink from the branded cans
on stage** — but a lot of them were just drinking water out of those cans.

> "And that was the insight. Energy drinks had figured out how to make a beverage feel like a
> lifestyle. They cracked the code on edgy, rebellious, attention-grabbing marketing. Nobody had
> applied that playbook to water."

Punk-rock aesthetic, skull logos, *"Murder Your Thirst"*, a tallboy can.

> "They took the energy drink marketing formula and applied it to the most boring product on earth.
> They didn't invent anything. They took something that was working and remixed it, and built a
> company valued at over $1 billion."

### Dr. Squatch

The DTC soap brand that scaled on YouTube with a charismatic spokesperson breaking the fourth wall,
cracking jokes, doing product demos in ridiculous settings.

> "Sound familiar? Because this is the Old Spice, the man your man could smell like. Format. Direct
> to camera spokesperson, absurdist humor, making a boring product feel exciting. Adapted for a DTC
> audience on YouTube and Facebook instead of TV."

**Over 120 million views and $100 million in revenue.**

> "It's the same playbook, different product, on a different problem. The result is a massive brand
> with its own identity."

### Duolingo

Not DTC, not e-commerce. A language-learning app that became one of the most followed brands on
TikTok with a giant green owl doing unhinged skits, threatening users, crushing on celebrities.

> "And everybody called it genius. Completely original. But if you were paying attention to social
> media in 2019 and 2020, you know exactly where that playbook came from. Wendy's. Twitter."

Wendy's proved a brand could drop the corporate voice and act like a real person online. Duolingo
took the same model — **unhinged brand voice, parasocial relationship with an audience, meme-native
content** — and moved it from Twitter to TikTok, with a mascot instead of a logo.

> "Fast food chain to language app. Different vertical, different platform, same pattern."

### The conclusion

> "The pattern is always the same, whether you're DTC, SaaS, food, beverage, travel, education it
> doesn't matter. The brands we admire aren't inventing new formats. They're not inventing anything.
> They're identifying proven patterns and applying them with **better taste, better targeting, and
> better timing.** And the ones who do this systematically with real tools and real process, instead
> of just vibing and hoping, those are the ones who win consistently."

⚠️ **All five cases are told after the fact, and the framing is Zach's.** Each is a real, checkable
brand story, but "X took the pattern from Y" is an interpretation, not something the founders said.
Liquid Death is the strongest because Cessario's Warped Tour origin is his own account; Duolingo
naming Wendy's as the model is Zach's reading. Present them as illustrations of the argument rather
than as documented lineage.

⚠️ **They are also all survivors.** Five brands that copied a pattern and won. The course does not
show the brands that copied a pattern and lost, which is the group that would tell you when this
approach fails.

## Act 3: The creative cyborg

Everything above was **analog pattern recognition**: Ogilvy reading for three weeks, Wieden mining
culture, Cessario happening to be at a music festival.

> "Slow. Manual. Dependent on one person happening to be at the right place at the right time."

**Austin Kleon**, *Steal Like an Artist*, is named as the prior statement of the argument:

> "nothing is original. Every creative idea is a remix of ideas that came before it. in. Your job
> isn't to create from nothing. It's to collect the right inputs and combine them in a way that
> people like."

**What has changed is the speed.** Where Ogilvy needed three weeks, a searchable ad library returns
the same class of input in minutes: what is running right now, what has been running for six months,
which hooks are working, which formats are scaling.

Then the second half:

> "take what you found, the patterns, the structures, the angles, and then bring them into AI
> ChatGPT. Claude, whatever your tool is and say, here's what I'm seeing work. Here's my brand,
> here's my audience. Give me ten variations that apply this pattern to my product."

**That combination is the "creative cyborg":**

> "systematic creative research plus AI as your brainstorming partner is what makes you a creative
> cyborg. Not someone who copies, not someone who uses AI to churn out generic garbage. Someone who
> has better inputs than everyone else, and then uses AI to multiply those inputs into creative that
> actually"

> "You plus AI is limitless — but only if you're feeding it the right stuff."

⭐ **That conditional is the whole course in one clause.** The AI half is unremarkable and everyone
has it. The research half is what the course is actually selling, and the claim is that the output
quality is set by the input quality, not by the model.
