# Course 301: Reverse Engineering Winning Ads — The Creative Research System

**Zach Murray**, founder of **Foreplay**. 25m 51s, 3 modules.

The course argues one thing hard, and everything else follows from it:

> "You were not original. That concept you're so proud of, someone did it before you... And here's
> the thing. That's not a problem. That's actually how creativity works."

Creativity is **pattern recognition**, not divine inspiration. The job is not to invent from nothing;
it is to collect better inputs than everyone else and recombine them with taste.

> "Don't waste time trying to be original. Use your time to do something better."

## What this skill needs from you

**Required: none, to explain any of it.** Ask why a competitor's ad works, or whether studying
competitors counts as copying, and get an answer.

**To produce the Pattern Map and Remix brief, required:**

1. **Three ads** you think are genuinely good, described or linked. Zach's instruction is *"pick
   three ads you genuinely think are great"* — admiration is the selection criterion, not
   performance data.
2. **The brand you are remixing for** — what it sells, roughly what it costs, who buys it.

**Optional, and it sharpens the output:**

3. What the brand's current creative looks like, so the gap analysis is real
4. The platform the remix is destined for
5. Price point relative to the competitors you named, which is what makes the audit useful

⚠️ **Two ads still work. One does not, and this is a hard floor.** The Remix draws one dimension
from each of several ads, so with two it degrades honestly and you say the map is partial. **With a
single ad every dimension comes from that ad, which is not a remix, it is a copy of it** — the
failure **The Pattern Map and the Remix** (below) calls legally and creatively bad. On one ad, produce the
pattern map, which is useful on its own, and say plainly that the Remix needs a second source.

⛔ **If the brand is missing, ask for it, but deliver the pattern maps anyway.** Breaking down the
three ads does not require knowing the destination. Only the Remix does. Produce the maps, mark the
Remix `⚠️ AWAITING BRAND`, and ask.

## ⚠️ Triaging a swipe file, which the course does not cover

ACA's selection rule is *"pick three ads you genuinely think are great."* **That does not
discriminate at the volume real swipe files reach** — everything in the file is already there
because someone thought it was great. At thirty ads the rule returns thirty.

Three cuts that do discriminate, in order:

1. **Drop anything from your own category first.** The Remix takes one dimension from each of three
   ads, and three direct competitors produce a fourth version of the same ad. ⛔ **If the whole file
   is direct competitors, that is your first finding** — the swipe file is the mechanism producing
   the sameness, and it should be said out loud before anything is mapped.
2. **Keep the ones you cannot immediately explain.** An ad whose trick is obvious has already been
   absorbed. The ones worth mapping are the ones that worked on you without your noticing why.
3. **Take three from three different categories** if the file allows it. Diversity of source is what
   makes the Remix produce something new rather than a blend of neighbours.

## How to run the artifact

**Teach the frame first, then produce.** Someone asking for an ad teardown usually has not accepted
the premise, and the premise is what stops the output being a copy.

1. **Settle whether they are remixing or copying.** The distinction is in
   **The competitor audit** (below) and it is the difference between the course working and
   producing a lawsuit.
2. **Map each ad across the six dimensions** using **The Pattern Map and the Remix** (below). Each dimension
   gets three entries: what they did, what the pattern is, and where else it has been seen.
3. **Do the Remix.** Pick one pattern per dimension, from any of the three ads, and combine.
4. **Close with the one-sentence form**, which is ACA's own template and the test that the remix is
   coherent rather than a pile of parts.

## Answering well

**The pattern is the abstraction, not the execution.** This is the single most common failure. *"You're
not stealing the price point. You're stealing the structure."* If someone writes "they used a
green background", that is what they did, not the pattern. Push for the level that transfers.

**"Seen this in" is the proof the pattern is real.** A pattern observed once is a coincidence. The
column exists to force a second sighting, and an honest "nowhere else yet" is a useful answer.

**Do not confuse this course with Course 302's competitor work.** 301 reverse-engineers individual
winning ads into reusable structures. 302 maps the competitive landscape as a whole. If the question
is "who are we up against and how are they positioned", that is 302's job, not this one's.

⛔ **Do not present the tooling as neutral.** The walkthrough runs inside Foreplay, which Zach
founded, and Foreplay features are named as the method throughout. **Provenance** (below) sets
out exactly which steps depend on the product and
**The competitor audit** (below) gives the version that runs without it. **Say which you are
giving.**

## ⚠️ Scope

Three modules and about 25 minutes. It is a **research and ideation** course.

It does not cover: how to write or design the ad once the pattern is chosen, media buying, budgets,
testing methodology, or measurement beyond naming that feedback loops matter. It names no benchmark
numbers at all, which is unusual for this programme and worth knowing before someone asks it for
one.

## The Pattern Map and the Remix

ACA's artifact for Course 301, shipped as the **Pattern Recognition Playbook** workbook and again as
the graded **Ads Pattern Recognition** assignment. Both carry the same framework.

> "Every ad you admire is built on a pattern someone else proved first. This playbook teaches you to
> see those patterns. Pick three ads ones you genuinely think are great and welll break them apart."

**The selection rule is admiration, not performance.** Zach says *"pick three ads you genuinely
think are great"*. Not the ones that scaled, not the ones with data attached. Taste is the filter,
because the exercise is training the eye.

---

### The six dimensions

Every ad gets read across all six. The option lists are ACA's, given as prompts rather than as a
closed taxonomy.

| Dimension | The question it answers | Options ACA names |
|---|---|---|
| **Hook type** | How does it grab attention in the first 3 seconds? | Pattern interrupt · bold claim · question · controversial take · native/UGC open · visual disruption |
| **Format** | What is the production structure? | Talking head · UGC testimonial · product demo · lifestyle · mashup/montage · founder story |
| **Narrative arc** | What is the story structure? | Problem > agitate > solve · before/after transformation · "I discovered something" story · listicle · myth-busting |
| **Proof mechanism** | How does it build belief? | Social proof · demonstration · authority/credentials · results/data · aspirational identity |
| **CTA style** | How does it close? | Direct offer · curiosity gap · urgency/scarcity · risk reversal · "just try it" |
| **Platform DNA** | What makes it feel native? | Aspect ratio · pacing · sound on/off design · caption style · lo-fi versus polished |

⚠️ **ACA's two documents disagree on the sixth dimension's name.** The Playbook says **Platform DNA**
throughout. The graded assignment's framework table says **Performance DNA**, but its own pattern-map
rows then say **PLATFORM DNA**. This skill uses **Platform DNA**, because two of the three
appearances say it and because the question it asks (*"what makes it feel native?"*) is about the
platform, not about performance. Flagged rather than silently reconciled.

---

### The Pattern Map, one per ad

Header: **Brand · Platform · Ad link.**

Then, for each of the six dimensions, **three fields**:

| Field | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| **THEY DID** | The specific, concrete execution. What is literally on screen |
| **THE PATTERN IS** | The abstraction. The transferable form underneath the execution |
| **SEEN THIS IN** | Where else this pattern appears. Another brand, another category, another era |

⛔ **The gap between the first two fields is the entire exercise.** Getting this wrong is the failure
mode, and it is the common one:

| ❌ Not a pattern (that is THEY DID) | ✅ The pattern |
|---|---|
| "Green screen behind a woman talking" | "Reaction-to-artefact: the creator responds to something on screen rather than addressing camera cold" |
| "Shows the price crossed out" | "Value anchoring against a stated reference price" |
| "Founder in a warehouse" | "Proof by access: showing the part of the business customers never see" |
| "Text says '3 things I wish I knew'" | "Numbered scarcity of insight, promising a finite, completable payoff" |

**The test:** could a brand in a completely different category use this? If not, you have written
down the execution.

⭐ **"SEEN THIS IN" is not decoration, it is the validation step.** A pattern observed once is a
coincidence. The column forces a second sighting, and that is what separates a pattern from a nice
ad. **"Nowhere else yet" is an honest and useful answer** — it means either the pattern is genuinely
novel, which is rare and worth noting, or it has not been abstracted far enough. Check which.

#### Filling it well

- **Timestamp the hook** if it is video. "First three seconds" is the definition, so say what happens
  in them rather than describing the ad generally.
- **One dimension can be absent.** An ad with no proof mechanism is a finding, not a gap in the map.
  Write "none" and note it, because a missing dimension is often exactly what makes an ad cheap to
  produce.
- **Do not force an option from the list.** The lists are prompts. If the hook is something ACA does
  not name, name it yourself.

---

### The Remix

The second half, and the reason the first half exists.

> "Pick one pattern from any ad for each category. Combine."

For each of the six dimensions, two fields:

| Field | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| **MY VERSION** | The pattern, rewritten for this brand, this product, this audience |
| **STEALING FROM AD #** | Which of the three it came from. Kept visible on purpose |

**Then ACA's closing form**, which is the coherence test:

> "A **{FORMAT}** ad that opens with **{HOOK}**, builds belief through **{PROOF}**, and closes with
> **{CTA}**."

⚠️ **The one-sentence form only names four of the six dimensions.** Narrative arc and Platform DNA
are mapped and remixed but do not appear in the summary. That is ACA's template as written. If the
sentence reads well but the ad still feels wrong, the missing two are usually where the problem is,
so check them before rewriting.

#### What makes a remix rather than a copy

**Take one dimension from each ad, not all six from one.** Six dimensions from a single ad is that
ad. The design of the exercise, drawing across three sources, is what makes the output new.

**If every dimension came from the same ad, say so and push back.** That is the most likely way this
produces something legally and creatively bad.

**The version must survive translation.** *"You're not stealing the price point. You're stealing the
structure."* If the pattern only works at the source brand's price, category or audience, it did not
transfer, and MY VERSION should say what replaced it.

---

### Producing it

Deliver as a document with:

1. **A header block** — brand, date, who it is for.
2. **Three pattern maps**, one per ad, each with the six dimensions and their three fields.
3. **The Remix**, six dimensions with MY VERSION and STEALING FROM AD #.
4. **The one-sentence form**, filled.
5. **A "what I could not verify" line.** Anything read from a description rather than from the ad
   itself, any "SEEN THIS IN" recalled rather than checked, marked as such.

⛔ **Mark inferences inside the document, not only in the covering message.** A pattern map gets
pasted into a deck, forwarded, and read a month later by someone who never saw the conversation. If
a "SEEN THIS IN" is from memory rather than from a checked example, it has to say so **in the cell**,
because that is the only place it will still be visible when it matters. Use `⚠️ from memory, not
verified`.

⚠️ **If the ads were described rather than supplied, say so at the top.** A pattern map built from
someone's description of an ad is a map of the description. Useful, but not the same artifact, and
the difference should be visible to whoever reads it next.

#### ACA's own submission requirement

The assignment version is graded:

> "Export this document as a single PDF and submit it via the submission screen so your work can be
> marked."

Worth mentioning to anyone working through the certification, because the deliverable format is
specified and a filled map in some other form will not submit.

## Why nothing is original, and why that is good news

Module 1: *The Pattern Recognition Playbook* (11m 23s).

### The premise

> "I need to tell you something that might sting a little. You were not original. That concept
> you're so proud of, someone did it before you. That hook you wrote last week that absolutely
> crushed? There was a version of it from 2019 that you saw, and you probably forgot about that big
> creative idea that you pitched in your last brainstorm. It was floating around in your
> subconscious because you scroll past something similar at 11 p.m. three days ago."

> "And here's the thing. That's not a problem. That's actually how creativity works. Every great
> idea in the history of advertising was built up the back of something that came before it. Every
> single one. and the people that figured out first they won."

**Where this came from.** Before Foreplay, Zach ran a DTC apparel brand. The weekly ritual was the
whole team on a call trying to share competitor ads they had seen, *"screenshots in Slack, broken
links and half-remembered TikToks. It was absolutely chaos."*

> "But here's what we noticed. The weeks where we did the most research, where we actually studied
> what was working in the market, those were the weeks we produced our best creative. Every time.
> Not because we were copying anyone. Because we were pattern matching. We were giving our brains
> better raw material to work with."

⛔ **This is the causal claim the whole course rests on, and it is an anecdote.** More research
weeks produced better creative at one brand, observed by the person who then built and sells a
research tool. It is plausible and it is not evidence. Carry it as his experience.

---

### Act 1: The greatest ads were stolen

#### Ogilvy and Rolls-Royce, 1958

David Ogilvy lands the Rolls-Royce account. *"This is the assignment of a lifetime. And what does he
do? Does he lock himself in a room and wait for divine inspiration? No. He spends three weeks
reading."* Every car magazine, every technical review, every piece of documentation about
Rolls-Royce. He studies the competition. He studies engineering.

Buried in a review in the British magazine **The Motor**, from a technical editor:

> "At 60 miles an hour, the loudest noise comes from the electric clock."

> "Ogilvy didn't write that line. He found it. He took it. He polished it into a headline that
> became the most famous ad in automotive history."

**Sales went up 50% that year**, and it launched the wave of clients that built his agency.

⭐ **And then the twist, which is the actual point of the story.** An ad from 25 years earlier, for a
car called **Pierce-Arrow**, used an almost identical line: *"the only sound one can hear in a
Pierce-Arrow is the ticking of the electric clock."* When the original agency's creative director
wrote to Ogilvy about it, Ogilvy said his version came from a magazine review.

> "The father of advertising's greatest headline was borrowed from a magazine editor, and it might
> have even been borrowed twice."

**Ogilvy's own instruction**, which Zach quotes as the origin of the whole method:

> "Study the precedents. Look at the campaigns for similar products in recent years. Find out which
> worked and which didn't, and then go do it better."

> "That's not copying. That's craft."

#### "Just Do It"

Dan Wieden, co-founder of Wieden+Kennedy, the night before Nike's first major TV campaign. He had
five spots from five different creative teams and no unifying thread.

His source: the last words of **Gary Gilmore**, a convicted murderer facing a firing squad in Utah
in 1977. Gilmore said *"Let's do it."*

⚠️ **Wieden is often reported** as joking that he did not like the *let's* part, and would otherwise have given them credit. **Widely repeated, and not in ACA's material** — do not present it as a quotation.

**Both audiences he showed it to rejected it.** His own agency said, quoting Zach, *"we don't need
that."* Phil Knight at Nike had the same reaction. Wieden pushed for it anyway.

**Within ten years Nike went from 18% market share to 43%.**

> "The most valuable tagline in sports marketing history came from a death row inmate championed by
> one stubborn copywriter who was smart enough to recognize pattern when he saw one"

⚠️ **The second half of this story is the part people drop.** Recognising the pattern was necessary
and not sufficient; the line only survived because someone defended it against two rejections. A
research process that surfaces good patterns into an organisation that kills them produces nothing.

---

### Act 2: The direct-response brands you think are original

Zach anticipates the objection: *"Okay Zach, but that's old advertising, legacy stuff. The brands I
work with, the brands I admire, they're actually doing original things."*

#### Liquid Death

Mike Cessario was at Warped Tour in 2008 watching punk bands. He noticed energy drink companies were
sponsoring every band, and the musicians were **contractually obliged to drink from the branded cans
on stage** — but a lot of them were just drinking water out of those cans.

> "And that was the insight. Energy drinks had figured out how to make a beverage feel like a
> lifestyle. They cracked the code on edgy, rebellious, attention-grabbing marketing. Nobody had
> applied that playbook to water."

Punk-rock aesthetic, skull logos, *"Murder Your Thirst"*, a tallboy can.

> "They took the energy drink marketing formula and applied it to the most boring product on earth.
> They didn't invent anything. They took something that was working and remixed it, and built a
> company valued at over $1 billion."

#### Dr. Squatch

The DTC soap brand that scaled on YouTube with a charismatic spokesperson breaking the fourth wall,
cracking jokes, doing product demos in ridiculous settings.

> "Sound familiar? Because this is the Old Spice, the man your man could smell like. Format. Direct
> to camera spokesperson, absurdist humor, making a boring product feel exciting. Adapted for a DTC
> audience on YouTube and Facebook instead of TV."

**Over 120 million views and $100 million in revenue.**

> "It's the same playbook, different product, on a different problem. The result is a massive brand
> with its own identity."

#### Duolingo

Not DTC, not e-commerce. A language-learning app that became one of the most followed brands on
TikTok with a giant green owl doing unhinged skits, threatening users, crushing on celebrities.

> "And everybody called it genius. Completely original. But if you were paying attention to social
> media in 2019 and 2020, you know exactly where that playbook came from. Wendy's. Twitter."

Wendy's proved a brand could drop the corporate voice and act like a real person online. Duolingo
took the same model — **unhinged brand voice, parasocial relationship with an audience, meme-native
content** — and moved it from Twitter to TikTok, with a mascot instead of a logo.

> "Fast food chain to language app. Different vertical, different platform, same pattern."

#### The conclusion

> "The pattern is always the same, whether you're DTC, SaaS, food, beverage, travel, education it
> doesn't matter. The brands we admire aren't inventing new formats. They're not inventing anything.
> They're identifying proven patterns and applying them with **better taste, better targeting, and
> better timing.** And the ones who do this systematically with real tools and real process, instead
> of just vibing and hoping, those are the ones who win consistently."

⚠️ **All five cases are told after the fact, and the framing is Zach's.** Each is a real, checkable
brand story, but "X took the pattern from Y" is an interpretation, not something the founders said.
Liquid Death is the strongest because Cessario's Warped Tour origin is his own account; Duolingo
naming Wendy's as the model is Zach's reading. Present them as illustrations of the argument rather
than as documented lineage.

⚠️ **They are also all survivors.** Five brands that copied a pattern and won. The course does not
show the brands that copied a pattern and lost, which is the group that would tell you when this
approach fails.

---

### Act 3: The creative cyborg

Everything above was **analog pattern recognition**: Ogilvy reading for three weeks, Wieden mining
culture, Cessario happening to be at a music festival.

> "Slow. Manual. Dependent on one person happening to be at the right place at the right time."

**Austin Kleon**, *Steal Like an Artist*, is named as the prior statement of the argument:

> "nothing is original. Every creative idea is a remix of ideas that came before it. in. Your job
> isn't to create from nothing. It's to collect the right inputs and combine them in a way that
> people like."

**What has changed is the speed.** Where Ogilvy needed three weeks, a searchable ad library returns
the same class of input in minutes: what is running right now, what has been running for six months,
which hooks are working, which formats are scaling.

Then the second half:

> "take what you found, the patterns, the structures, the angles, and then bring them into AI
> ChatGPT. Claude, whatever your tool is and say, here's what I'm seeing work. Here's my brand,
> here's my audience. Give me ten variations that apply this pattern to my product."

**That combination is the "creative cyborg":**

> "systematic creative research plus AI as your brainstorming partner is what makes you a creative
> cyborg. Not someone who copies, not someone who uses AI to churn out generic garbage. Someone who
> has better inputs than everyone else, and then uses AI to multiply those inputs into creative that
> actually"

> "You plus AI is limitless — but only if you're feeding it the right stuff."

⭐ **That conditional is the whole course in one clause.** The AI half is unremarkable and everyone
has it. The research half is what the course is actually selling, and the claim is that the output
quality is set by the input quality, not by the model.

### What to do with this

Zach's own homework at the end of module 1 is the artifact this skill produces:

> "Take three ads you admire and break them down into their component parts. What's the format?
> Where did it come from? What pattern is it building on? And how could you apply that to your own
> content? And how"

Go to **The Pattern Map and the Remix** (below).

## The competitor audit

Module 2: *The Competitor Audit — A Systematic Research Workflow* (8m 22s). The course's operational
core, taught as a live walkthrough on a real brand.

### The warning it opens with

> "you can't just scroll through your competitors Facebook pages and go, cool at and expect to get
> any better. That's how you end up a copycat or worse, a copycat with no strategy."

> "Looking at competitors for inspiration can be your greatest unlock. Or you could fall into the
> dark pit of being a meta brand that looks exactly like everyone else in your space."

**Both outcomes come from the same activity.** What separates them is whether there is a system
underneath. That is what this module supplies.

---

### The five steps

1. **Audit your own brand first and name the content gaps.** You cannot see what to borrow until you
   know what is missing.
2. **Study 5 to 6 competitors across price points**, not only direct ones.
3. **Check historical and seasonal campaigns** to find proven winners rather than current guesses.
4. **Remix the patterns with AI** into brand-specific variations.
5. **Find non-obvious inspiration from adjacent brands** that sell to the same person in a different
   category.

---

### Step 1: The worked example, and what a gap analysis looks like

The brand is **James Perse**, a luxury Los Angeles apparel label. *"Beautiful products, incredible
quality. The kind of brand where people pay $250 for a t-shirt because the fit and the fabric are
genuinely that good. They built a real lifestyle brand."*

> "But here's the honest truth. I had creative is not that great. And I say that with love."

The diagnosis, read straight off their ad history:

| What the audit found | What it means |
|---|---|
| Static images, no video testing | No format experimentation at all |
| No active hooks | Nothing engineered for the first three seconds |
| Videos are purely brand films | Made for brand, not for performance |
| Low creative volume | Too few shots on goal to learn anything |
| Zero UGC | The cheapest, highest-volume format is unused |

> "This is a brand with a ton of equity and an audience that already loves the product. But they're
> leaving so much performance on the table because their creative strategy is in brand mode."

⭐ **"In brand mode" is the diagnosis worth carrying.** It names a specific, common and fixable
condition: a brand whose creative is competent and beautiful and is not built to be tested. The
audit does not say the work is bad. It says the work is doing a different job from the one the
budget needs.

⚠️ **The transcript gives two different prices for the same t-shirt.** *"$250 for a t-shirt"* early
on, then *"True Classic Tees at $30 has a completely different creative engine than James Perse at
125"* and *"someone who spends $125 on a t-shirt"* later. Both are carried where they appear; the
$125 figure is the one used twice and the one the competitor comparison rests on.

⛔ **The discrepancy propagates, which is why it matters.** The competitor set below is built as a
price ladder around this brand: True Classic below it, Everlane and Theory near or above. Those
relationships only hold at **$125**. At $250 the ladder inverts and the worked example stops
demonstrating what it claims to. Use $125 and know that the source is not clean.

---

### Step 2: Competitors across price points

> "Here's something that most people get wrong about competitor analysis. They only look at direct
> competitors, brands that are at the same price point selling the same product. That's useful, but
> it's incomplete."

> "You should be studying competitors based on **every price point**, because a brand like True
> Classic Tees at $30 has a completely different creative engine than James Perse at $125. And
> that's exactly where interesting patterns live."

⭐ **This is the most transferable idea in the module.** The reason is mechanical rather than
aesthetic: **price point determines creative engine.** A $30 brand must convert on volume and
therefore tests aggressively, uses bundles, leans on urgency, and produces a lot. A $125 brand
converts on desire and can afford restraint. So the cheap brand is running the experiments the
expensive brand never will, and the results are free to read.

The set Zach builds for James Perse:

| Brand | Why it is in the set |
|---|---|
| **True Classic Tees** (~$30) | *"an absolute crusher, you know, aggressive performance, creative, great bundle ads with high volume of testing."* |
| **Everlane?** ⚠️ **NAME INFERRED, NOT STATED** (higher price point) | *"Transparency-driven creative with strong editorial approaches"* |
| **Outerknown** | *"Really aligned with the James Perse outdoor lifestyle"* |
| A premium basics brand | The direct competitor, *"to see how luxury does a performance."* |
| **Theory** | *"similar pricing tier. But you know enterprise level creative team"* |

⚠️ **One brand name in the transcript is garbled.** The second entry is rendered *"everything a
little bit higher, drug price point, transparency-driven creative"*. **Everlane** is the reading used
here: it is a higher-priced basics brand whose entire market position is transparency, which is the
only part of the phrase that survives intact. Treat the name as inferred, not as stated.

**What to read on each competitor:** every ad they have launched, how long each one ran, what formats
they use, and what hooks they are testing.

---

### Step 3: History is where the proof is

> "I can go back and look at what worked for these brands in Black Friday of last year, what they
> tested, what they scaled. If you're planning a Q4 campaign, this is intel you literally can't get
> anywhere else."

⭐ **Two signals do most of the work, and both come from timing rather than from the creative:**

| Signal | How to read it |
|---|---|
| **Ads launched on the same day** | A creative test. The brand was putting these against each other |
| **An ad that ran a long time** | The winner. *"That's the pattern to study"* |

**Run length is the closest thing to performance data you can see from outside.** A brand does not
keep paying for an ad that is not working. Nothing else visible from the outside carries that
information, which is why it is worth more than how good the ad looks.

⚠️ **Four months is the worked example's number, not a threshold.** Zach reads one ad that had run
four months as the winner in that account. The course sets no minimum anywhere, and what counts as
long depends entirely on the category's testing tempo. Read run length **relative to the other ads
in the same set**, not against a fixed figure.

⚠️ **Nor is there a defined window for "same day".** Ad libraries show start dates by day and brands
stagger launches, so treat a cluster within a few days as a probable test cohort and say that is what
you are doing.

⚠️ **Run length is a proxy and it lies in specific ways.** An ad can run long because it is an
evergreen brand asset nobody reviews, because it is contractually committed, or because the account
is badly managed. Long-running plus part of a same-day test cohort is a much stronger signal than
long-running alone.

### Copying versus remixing

The distinction the whole course turns on, stated on True Classic's bundle ads:

> "James Perse would never price that way, but the format showing a curated bundle style together
> with a clear value anchor, that's something James first could absolutely adapt to the price
> point."

> "**That's the difference between copying and remixing. You're not stealing the price point. You're
> stealing the structure.**"

| Copying | Remixing |
|---|---|
| Takes the execution | Takes the structure |
| Transfers the offer, the price, the claim | Transfers the mechanism and re-fits it |
| Works only if you are the same kind of brand | Works across price points and categories |
| Produces a brand that looks like the category | Produces something that feels new from proven parts |

**The test:** name the thing you are taking without naming the source brand's product, price or
audience. If you cannot, you are copying.

---

### Step 4: What to actually make

Two categories, and the distinction is about effort, not quality:

| | **Single base hits** | **Home runs** |
|---|---|---|
| What | Take one winning ad pattern and adapt it quickly | Take a deeper insight and build a full creative concept around it |
| Effort | Low, fast turnaround | High |
| Use for | Volume, filling a test slate | The concept the quarter is built on |

**For base hits**, Zach's flow is: put the reference ad into ChatGPT or Claude, **refine the
messaging first**, then either brief a designer or generate the asset with an image tool. He names
**Nano Banana, Higgsfield and Artlist** as the generation options and says he personally uses
Higgsfield or Artlist.

⭐ **"Refine the messaging first" is the step that gets skipped**, and skipping it is how this
becomes a machine for producing polished versions of someone else's ad. The image is the last step,
not the first.

**For home runs**, the worked example: Outerknown's longest-running video is an employee store tour
with product-specific highlights. Broken down, it works because it

- builds trust by showing a physical, brick-and-mortar store
- focuses on performance and function
- touches sustainable messaging

> "The best part is the James Perse stores literally belong in architectural magazines. They could
> heavily lean into this format."

**That is the reasoning to imitate:** the pattern was chosen because the target brand has an
unusually strong version of the asset the pattern needs. A store tour is a weak idea for a brand with
ugly stores.

---

### Step 5: Adjacent brands, the step most people skip

> "here's where it gets really interesting. And it's the part that most people skipped your best
> creative inspiration often doesn't come from your direct competitors. It comes from **adjacent
> brands,** companies that sell to the same person as you, but doing a completely different
> category."

The method is to describe the customer rather than the product. For James Perse: *"someone who
spends $125 on a t-shirt, cares about what they put in their body, what their home looks like, and
where they travel."*

That description produces the adjacent set: **luxury wellness, premium home goods, high-end
fitness.**

> "These are the brands to sell to the exact same avatar with completely different creative
> strategies. That is where original feeling ideas come from."

> "This is how to stop being a meta copycat and start building creative that feels genuinely fresh,
> even though every element of it came from proven patterns."

**How to generate the list yourself:** write one sentence describing the customer that mentions no
product category, then ask what else that person spends money on. The sentence is the work; the
brands follow from it.

⚠️ **Course 230's Cross-Vertical Creative Inspiration Finder does exactly this job in more depth**,
with a verification discipline this course does not have. Where that skill is installed alongside
this one, route the adjacent-brand question there and keep this one for the audit.

The walkthrough runs inside **Foreplay**, and several steps are named as product features: **Spyder**
(competitor tracking), **boards** (saving), **Creative Target Expansion** (finding brands that share
an audience), automatic video transcription, and historical ad data.

⛔ **Zach founded Foreplay.** See **Provenance** (below). The method is separable from the

| Step | Without a paid tool |
|---|---|
| Audit your own creative | Free. Your own ad account, plus the public ad library |

| **Historical and seasonal** | ⛔ **This is the real loss.** Public libraries generally show active ads. Last Black Friday is largely gone unless you saved it at the time |

## Building a creative engine for your whole team

Module 3 (6m 06s). The module about the organisation rather than the work.

### The problem

> "When was the last time you had your best creative idea? I bet it wasn't in a brainstorm. And it
> wasn't in a meeting. And it wasn't sitting at your desk staring at a brief. It. It was in the
> shower or on the train, or scrolling your phone at 10 p.m. when your brain was half off."

> "Most creative teams funnel every single ad concept through one person, the creative strategist,
> the creative director, or whoever holds the title. Everyone else just waits for the brief. That's a
> massive waste of creative potential."

### The insight underneath it

The origin story, from the weekly all-hands call at Zach's DTC brand where everyone had to bring ads
they had seen that week — *"not just the marketing team, everyone: our operations manager, our
customer service person, our freelance designer."*

> "And here's what I discovered. The more we tested the better a performance was. That sounds
> obvious, but the insight underneath it isn't. It wasn't just that we tested more ads, it's that
> **we had more diverse inputs because everyone's algorithm is completely different.**"

> "Your social feed shows you different ads than mine, and your accounts feed shows them different
> ads and your creative directors, your ops managers. TikTok for you page is in a completely
> different universe than what your performance marketer sees. **That's not noise, that's signal.**"

⭐ **This is the strongest idea in the course and it is not really about ads.** Personalised feeds
mean every employee is running a free, continuously updating sample of a different slice of the
market. A company with 30 people has 30 uncorrelated research feeds and typically uses one. The
asymmetry is that the marketing team's feed is the *least* representative of the customer, because
the algorithm has learned they are a marketer.

**The bottleneck was never generation. It was capture:**

> "The problem was capturing it. We had screenshots and slack and links and email threads and
> bookmarks that nobody could find. Half the ads were already taken down by the time someone went
> back to look at them."

⚠️ **"Half the ads were already taken down" is the constraint that makes this urgent.** Ad creative
is ephemeral. A reference you did not save is usually unrecoverable, which is why a capture habit
beats a research sprint.

---

### Strategy 1: Get everyone contributing

> "Get your entire team looking for ways you can communicate the product or service that you sell.
> And I don't just mean your creative team. I mean everyone who sees ads, which is everyone."

What each function is uniquely positioned to see:

| Function | What their feed and their job surfaces |
|---|---|
| **Operations** | Competitors' shipping and unboxing experiences |
| **Customer service** | ⭐ *"The exact language customers use to describe your product"* |
| **Finance** | *"a completely different algorithm showing them completely different apps."* |
| **Sales** | B2B and pitch-style creative *"that you would never encounter"* |

⭐ **Customer service is the highest-value entry in this table and the least like the others.** The
rest contribute ads. Customer service contributes **the customer's own words**, which is the raw
material for hooks rather than a reference for them. Anyone building this should route that channel
separately.

**What makes it work is friction.** The whole strategy fails on capture, not on willingness:

> "The For Play mobile app plus Instagram integration makes it dead simple. Anyone on your team can
> save an ad they see on Instagram or TikTok, or anywhere else with one tap, It goes straight to
> your shared swipe file, ready to head into production."

> "Honestly, I say 80 to 90% of my best ad ideas went on a walk or on the toilet."

> "That's how you **multiply creative surface area** without adding a single person to your team."

⛔ **The tool is Zach's own product.** See **Provenance** (below). The mechanism that matters is
**one-tap capture from the phone into a shared place**, and any tool that clears that bar works. The
test is whether saving takes longer than the impulse lasts: a shared album, a channel with a
bookmark emoji, a note. **A process that requires opening a laptop will not survive contact with a
person on a walk**, which is where the material actually appears.

---

### Strategy 2: Make performance data a commodity

> "Here's a pattern I constantly see in creative teams. The strategist creates the brief, the
> designer makes the ad, the media buyer launches it, and then silence. Nobody tells the designer
> whether their ad worked or not. Nobody closes the loop. And that's insane."

> "Imagine if a basketball player took a shot but never saw if the ball went in. How would they get
> any better?"

**Feedback loops have to be fast, visible, and tied to the actual work people are doing.**

What the analytics layer is for, in his description: which hooks are converting, which formats are
driving the best CPA, and when creative is starting to fatigue — **shared with designers, creators
and the CEO, not held by the media buyer.** He names four capabilities: comparison reports, creative
fatigue alerts, gamified goal tracking, and industry benchmarks.

> "Here's the part that really matters. When people can see their impact of their work, they get
> better and they get more motivated. It turns creative production from a black box into a game with
> real scores. Your designer who never knew how their ads performed. Now they can see that their
> last concept outperformed the industry benchmark by 40%. That's not just data. That's a positive
> feedback loop that makes them want to push harder."

> "That is how you build a creative engine. Not by hiring more people, but by making the people you
> have more connected to the outcomes of their work."

⛔ **The tool here is the instructor's own product, and unlike strategy 1 the course gives no
generic version.** The mechanism that matters is **whoever made the thing finds out what it did**,
on a cadence short enough to change the next one. What that actually needs:

| The capability, as pitched | What it is generically | Cheapest honest version |
|---|---|---|
| Comparison reports | Same-metric comparison across creatives | A spreadsheet the media buyer already exports |
| Creative fatigue alerts | Noticing decay before it costs money | The 7-day versus 30-day check, run monthly |
| Gamified goal tracking | Making outcomes visible to whoever made them | ⚠️ See the caution below before building this |
| Industry benchmarks | Knowing whether good is good | ⛔ Not available free, and the course names none |

**The first two are the ones that close the loop**, and neither needs a product. A standing item in
a weekly creative review, naming which concepts won and which decayed, does the job.

⚠️ **Two cautions the course does not give:**

1. **"Gamified goal tracking" cuts both ways.** Making individual creative performance visible to
   the whole team is a motivator when results are good and a public leaderboard of failure when they
   are not. Most ads lose. A team culture that has not absorbed that will learn to make safe ads,
   which is the opposite of what the loop is for.
2. **The 40% figure is illustrative, not a result.** It is an example of what a designer might see,
   not a reported outcome. Do not repeat it as a benchmark.

⛔ **This strategy needs attribution you can defend before it is worth building.** Telling a designer
their concept won, on numbers that cannot survive scrutiny, is worse than telling them nothing.
Course 201 covers what the attribution models can and cannot prove; where that skill is installed
alongside this one, settle measurement there first.

---

### The close

> "The best creative teams don't depend on one person. They build systems that capture inspiration
> from everywhere, and they use data to create feedback loops that make everyone better."

> "That is what it means to be a creative cyborg **team**. Not replacing humans with AI, not copying
> competitors, but building a system where human creativity is multiplied by better tools, better
> inputs, and better feedback."

> "You plus AI is limitless. Now go build."

### Standing this up

The course describes the destination and not the transition. What it implies, in order:

1. **Pick the capture surface first, and optimise it for one-handed use on a phone.** Everything else
   depends on this and nothing else is hard.
2. **Name the contribution as part of the job**, not a favour. The all-hands call worked because it
   was a standing rule with a slot in the week.
3. **Route customer-service language separately** from ad references. Different material, different
   use.
4. **Close the loop on the way back out.** Whoever saved it should hear what happened to it,
   otherwise contribution decays within a month.
5. **Only then build the reporting layer**, and only once the numbers behind it can be defended.
