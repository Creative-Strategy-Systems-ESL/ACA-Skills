# The 14 blocks

Operational lookup for filling the worksheet. What each block is, the execution styles the
worksheet lists, what to look for when scoring it, and **what its absence usually means** — that
last column is what Section 4's missing-blocks question runs on.

An ad is an assembly of interchangeable parts, not a monolith. The point of naming the parts is
that you can then diagnose which one is failing and swap it without rebuilding the ad.

## Structure blocks — the backbone

Every direct response ad has all three. They are not optional, so for these the question is never
present-or-absent, it is where the boundaries fall and how well each works.

### 1. Hook Block (0-3 seconds)

The scroll-stopping opener. Roughly three seconds to signal relevance and generate curiosity.

Effective hooks do one or more of:

- **Identify a specific pain point** — *"Tired of struggling with patchy beard growth?"*
- **Challenge a common belief** — *"The one skincare ingredient dermatologists say you should NEVER use"*
- **Make a bold promise** — *"How we generated 427 qualified leads in 24 hours without paid ads"*
- **Create a pattern interrupt** — something unexpected that breaks the scroll rhythm

**Scoring it:** does it earn the next three seconds? A hook can be well made and still generic. The
sharper test is whether it could open a competitor's ad unchanged; if it could, it is a category
hook, not this ad's hook.

**Worksheet fields:** start and end timestamp · hook type · key visual elements · key message or
text · pattern interrupt technique.

### 2. Body Block

The main persuasive content: where the argument is made, the product is shown, objections are
handled.

**Scoring it:** does it follow from the hook? The most common structural failure is a hook that
promises one thing and a body that argues another, and it shows up as a good hook rate with a bad
hold rate.

**Worksheet fields:** start and end timestamp · body approach · key points covered · transition
technique to CTA.

The **transition to CTA** field is the one people skip, and it is diagnostic. An ad that cuts
abruptly to an offer loses everyone who was persuaded but not yet ready.

### 3. CTA Block

The action-driving closer.

**Scoring it:** clarity beats cleverness. The worksheet asks for action clarity 1-10 separately from
the rest for that reason.

**Worksheet fields:** start and end timestamp · CTA phrasing · urgency / scarcity elements · value
reinforcement · action clarity (1-10).

## Product blocks — they showcase the offering

These argue. For each: present (Y/N) · timestamp · style · effectiveness (1-10) · notes.

### 4. Product Introduction

How the product is first revealed.

**Execution styles:** direct product shot · problem-then-product · benefit-first approach · other

**What to look for:** the timestamp matters more than the style. A product introduced at 0:01 to a
problem-aware audience is arriving before the problem has been named.

**If absent:** the ad is running on brand or entertainment and expecting the landing page to sell.
Sometimes deliberate at the top of funnel, usually an oversight.

### 5. Demonstration

Showing the product in action.

**Execution styles:** how-to usage · before/after results · real-time results · comparison to
alternative · user demonstration · other

**What to look for:** whether the demonstration proves the specific claim the hook made, or just
shows the product existing. Most weak demos are the latter.

**If absent:** normal for products whose benefit is not visual. A real gap for anything where
seeing it work is the argument, and the single most common missing block in underperforming
physical-product ads.

### 6. Features / Benefits Presentation

Highlighting key selling points.

**Execution styles:** feature → advantage → benefit · problem → solution → outcome · pain → remedy →
relief · list / bullet points · animated graphics / text · other

**What to look for:** whether it reaches the third step. Most feature copy stops at the second —
what it is and why that matters — and never says how it improves their life.

**If absent:** fine in an emotionally-driven ad. A problem when the audience is product-aware and
comparing, because that is exactly when they want specifics.

### 7. Buying Experience

Making the purchase process clear.

**Elements included:** price reveal · shipping information · purchase process explanation ·
guarantee / risk reversal · limited time offer · other

**What to look for:** risk reversal is the highest-leverage element here and the most often left
out.

**If absent:** costs least at the top of funnel and most at the bottom. For a most-aware audience,
an ad with no buying experience block is asking someone ready to buy to go and find the terms
themselves.

### 8. Unboxing (if applicable)

Creating anticipation and trust through packaging reveals.

**Execution styles:** customer unboxing · professional unboxing · packaging highlights · initial
reaction · other

**What to look for:** whether it is doing a job or filling time. Unboxing works for gifting,
premium positioning and categories where the physical object is part of the value.

**If absent:** usually nothing. The worksheet marks this one "if applicable" for a reason, and a
teardown that lists it as a missing opportunity for a software product has misread the block.

## Person blocks — they create emotional connection

These connect. Same fields: present (Y/N) · timestamp · approach · effectiveness (1-10) · notes.

### 9. Problem Statement

Articulating the pain point.

**Approaches:** personal story / experience · common pain point · question-based · other

**What to look for:** whether it is the audience's problem in the audience's words, or the
marketer's description of the problem. `I'm tired of waking up tired` against `combats fatigue`.

**If absent:** the ad is aimed at a solution-aware or later audience and assumes the problem is
understood. Correct for retargeting, fatal for cold traffic.

### 10. Failed Alternatives

What did not work before this solution.

**Types:** prior solutions tried · competing product comparison · common misconceptions · *"I've
tried everything"* approach · other

**What to look for:** this block is how an ad earns the right to make its claim in a crowded
category. It is the most under-used block in the framework.

**If absent:** in a saturated market this is usually the biggest single opportunity, because the
audience has tried the alternatives whether or not the ad acknowledges it.

### 11. Desired Outcome

The transformation promised.

**Outcome presentation:** emotional transformation · physical / tangible results · lifestyle
improvement · status enhancement · problem elimination · other

**What to look for:** an outcome, not a feature restated. "You get 21g of protein" is a feature;
"you stop thinking about lunch" is an outcome.

**If absent:** the ad has described a product without saying what changes. Rare and serious.

### 12. Before & After

Visual proof of change.

**Execution approaches:** side-by-side comparison · sequential reveal · testimonial transformation ·
results timeline · other

**What to look for:** whether the "before" is credible. An implausible before state destroys the
after.

**If absent:** often a compliance constraint rather than a creative choice, particularly in health,
beauty and finance. Worth noting as a constraint rather than a miss when the category explains it.

### 13. Social Proof

Testimonials and validation.

**Types:** customer testimonials · reviews / ratings · user count / statistics · expert endorsements
· awards / certifications · other

**What to look for:** whether the proof is from people recognisably like the target. Social proof
works on similarity, so a celebrity endorsement and a review from someone in the same situation are
doing different jobs.

**If absent:** almost always a gap, and one of the cheapest to fill.

### 14. Storytelling

Narrative that ties everything together.

**Execution approaches:** personal journey · customer success story · origin / founding story ·
problem-solution narrative · day-in-the-life scenario · other

**What to look for:** whether the story goes anywhere. A founder talking about their motivation is
not yet a story; a story has a change in it.

**If absent:** common and often fine. Storytelling is a way of delivering the other blocks, not an
extra block to add.

⚠️ **Storytelling and Before & After are the two blocks whose execution is least assessable from a
written description.** Whether a story lands depends on delivery, pacing and face. If you have a
description rather than the ad, mark these `PRESENT, EXECUTION NOT ASSESSABLE` rather than scoring
them.

## Reading the categories against each other

**Product blocks argue. Person blocks connect.** The split is a fast diagnostic for an ad that
feels flat:

- **Only Product blocks** → a specification sheet. Accurate, unwatchable.
- **Only Person blocks** → an emotional film that never explains what is being sold.
- **Neither category thin, but the ad still flat** → the problem is usually structural rather than
  a missing block: the hook and body are arguing different things, or the blocks are present in an
  order that fights the awareness stage.

The category check takes ten seconds and catches a real proportion of underperformers, so run it
before hunting for a subtler explanation.
