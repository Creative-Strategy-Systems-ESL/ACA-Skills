# Fill the Consumer Psychology Worksheet

Ad Creative Academy's Course 102 artifact, produced for a specific brand. This skill runs the
worksheet. It does not teach the psychology behind it, beyond what is needed to fill each section
correctly.

**The output is a document someone else can use.** It gets handed to a writer, argued with in a
review, and referred back to when creative drifts. Produce it as filled sections with headings, not
as prose.

## What this skill needs from you

**Required:**

1. **The product or service**, and what it actually does
2. **Who it is for**, as far as you know
3. **Research on those people** — reviews, support tickets, sales-call notes, survey responses,
   social comments, or your own notes from talking to them

**Optional, and it sharpens the artifact:**

4. Current messaging or existing ads
5. Price point and the offer
6. What competitors are saying
7. Whether this is for one ad or a multi-touch campaign (decides whether Section 6 matters)

### If something is missing, ask. Do not invent it.

The worksheet is only worth what it is grounded in. A fabricated pain point produces a worksheet
that reads exactly like a real one and sends a writer in the wrong direction, and nobody downstream
can tell.

Three situations, and the middle one is the trap:

**1. Research supplied.** Work from it. Quote it.

**2. ⚠️ A source NAMED but not supplied** — "we have 400 reviews", "sales talks to them daily",
"there's loads of comments". **This is not the same as having the research.** The sentence sounds
like evidence arrived; nothing did. Ask for a bounded slice rather than the whole corpus:

> Paste 20 to 30 review lines, weighted towards the critical ones and anything that repeats, plus
> any themes you have already spotted. That is enough to ground Sections 1 and 2.

Asking for all 400 gets refused. Asking for a specific, achievable slice gets an answer. Skew the
request to negative and repeated reviews deliberately: complaints carry the pain point in the
customer's own words, which is exactly what Section 2 needs.

**Then produce the sheet anyway**, on what you have, flagged. Asking and delivering nothing is not
a service to someone with a deadline. Send the ask **and** the draft, and say which sections the
answer would upgrade.

**3. No research at all.** Say what would be needed, then offer to produce a draft on assumptions
**marked as assumptions inside the worksheet itself**.

### ⚠️ A handful of quotes is not the corpus

The common real situation is not one of the three above. It is a corpus **named** plus a few lines
**hand-picked by the person selling the product**. Two rules:

- **Below roughly ten customer lines, Section 1's stage placement is an assumption**, however
  confident the lines sound. A small sample can carry Section 2's *language* — the words are real
  whatever else is true — but it cannot establish where a population sits. State the sample size in
  the section header and flag the placement accordingly.
- **Say who selected them.** Lines chosen by the brand skew positive and skew towards what the brand
  already believes. That is not dishonesty, it is what happens when someone picks their favourite
  four. Note it, and it costs nothing.

### ⚠️ Reviews are written by people who have finished shopping

A review is customer voice, not prospect voice, and Section 1 asks about prospects. Someone who
bought and came back to write about it is by definition past the awareness stage you are trying to
place.

Sort what you are given before using it:

- **Pre-purchase language** — describing the problem, what they tried, why they were sceptical.
  This can carry Section 1.
- **Post-purchase language** — what surprised them, what they now do differently, whether it was
  worth it. Gold for Sections 3 and 4, and **evidence about buyers, not about the people you are
  trying to reach.** Using it to place the awareness stage produces a confident, wrong answer.

### What may be reasoned, what must be supplied, and what "supplied" does not mean

| Fair to reason towards, flagged | Must come from you |
|---|---|
| How this product category generally behaves | What **this** brand's customers actually say |
| Which awareness stage a category typically sits at | Any specific number, claim, timeframe or result |
| Which emotional drivers a category usually touches | Testimonial content, review quotes, named benefits |
| Structural advice: which angle suits which stage | Anything a customer could disprove |

General category reasoning is a legitimate draft. **Brand-specific invention is not**, even
flagged, because a fabricated quote outlives the flag attached to it.

⛔ **But "the client said it" is not the same as "it is true", and this is where sheets get people
into trouble.** Two very different things arrive labelled as supplied:

| A supplied **fact** | A supplied **assertion** |
|---|---|
| They know it because it is theirs: price, ingredients, process, how long it simmers, what is in the box | An estimate, a comparison, or a superlative about the outside world |
| *"£6.50 a jar", "18-hour simmer", "10-jar subscription"* | *"our nutritionist reckons about 3x the collagen", "we're the only one doing this", "competitors all say X"* |
| Use it | **Quarantine it.** Record it, do not build a line on it |

The tell is whether the claim is checkable by someone outside the company — a competitor, a
customer, a regulator. Anything hedged (*"reckons"*, *"about"*, *"I think"*) is an internal
estimate wearing the clothes of a fact, and it is usually the most quotable sentence in the brief,
which is exactly why it ends up in the ad.

## How to run it

**Work the sections in order.** Later ones depend on earlier ones: the hooks in Section 5 are
supposed to follow from the awareness stage in Section 1 and the emotional driver in Section 2.
Filling 5 first produces hooks that sound fine and are aimed at nobody.

1. **Confirm the inputs.** Name what you have and what is missing. Ask for the missing required
   ones before starting.
2. **Fill Sections 1 to 3** — awareness, emotional drivers, persuasion principles. These are the
   grounded ones and they carry the rest.
3. **Fill Sections 4 and 5** — the framing matrix and the hooks. These are derived from 1 to 3, not
   invented alongside them.
4. **Fill Section 6** only if this is a multi-touch campaign. Say so and skip it if it is one ad.
5. **Deliver Section 7 as a checklist to run later**, against finished creative. It is a gate, not
   a summary, and ticking it against the worksheet proves nothing.
6. **Flag every section's provenance.** Open each with one of the three markers below.
7. **Carry Section 8 through unchanged.** Four ready-made formulas, nothing to fill. They are what
   the writer receiving this reaches for, along with the line the sheet closes on: the goal is not
   manipulation, it is connecting people who have real problems with something that helps.
8. **Deliver a Claims Register.** Required, not optional. See below — it is the part that stops a
   flagged worksheet turning into an unflagged ad.

### The three provenance markers

Open every filled section with whichever applies:

```
GROUNDED IN: [what was supplied — e.g. 34 Amazon review quotes, Oct 2025]
⚠️ ASSUMPTION, NOT VERIFIED: [what was reasoned about the category, and what would confirm it]
⚠️ CLIENT-ASSERTED, UNVERIFIED: [what they told you that nobody has checked]
```

The third one matters as much as the other two. Without it, a hedged internal estimate arrives
inside the same header as a hard fact and is silently promoted.

### ⛔ The Claims Register, and why the section flags are not enough

**Nobody copies a section. They copy a line.**

A flag on line one of Section 4 does not travel with line four of Section 4. The copywriter opens a
document and pastes `eighteen hours in the pot` and `3x the collagen` — seven-word fragments,
which are the unit that actually moves through a business. Both arrive at the ad with no history,
and three weeks later nobody can reconstruct where the number came from.

So the worksheet ends with a register, and it is a required output field:

| Claim | Where it appears | Status | What it needs before it ships |
|---|---|---|---|
| 18-hour simmer | S4 mechanism, S5 hook 3 | Client-supplied process fact | Confirm it is always 18h, not "up to" |
| "3x the collagen" | **Nowhere — quarantined** | ⚠️ Client-asserted, hedged | Lab data, named comparator, claims review |
| "The only 18-hour simmer" | **Nowhere — quarantined** | ⚠️ Unverified superlative | A documented competitor audit |
| ~900 reviews | S3 social proof | Count not confirmed | Exact figure and rating on the day of use |

**Every line in Sections 4, 5 and 6 that carries a number, a superlative, or a claim about what the
product does to a body gets a row.** Tag it inline where it appears (`[C2]`) so the fragment carries
its own pointer when it is copied.

**Write the quarantined ones down too, and say where they are not used.** A claim that is missing
from the register looks overlooked; a claim recorded as deliberately excluded is a decision, and it
stops someone else quietly reinstating it.

### On claims about what the product does to a body

The frameworks here are equally good at selling something that works and something that does not,
which is why this matters in health, supplements, food, beauty, finance and anything with a
regulator.

Three different things, and they are not interchangeable:

- **A mechanism claim** — how it is made, what is in it, what the process is. Usually the brand's
  own knowledge and the safest ground. It is also the sophistication escape, so this is a happy
  alignment rather than a compromise.
- **An outcome claim** — what it does to the person. Needs substantiation the brand actually holds,
  and often a regulatory check. Do not generate these from research; research tells you what
  customers *say*, which is not the same as what is *demonstrated*.
- **An attributed customer quote** — what one named person reported. Different from a brand claim
  and must stay visibly different in the artifact, because the moment it loses its quotation marks
  it has become one.

You are not the compliance check and should not pretend to be. **Sort claims into those three
buckets, put the outcome claims in the register, and say plainly that they need substantiation
before use.** That is the strategist's job and it is genuinely useful; guessing at what is legal is
neither.

## Getting the output right

**Quote the customer's own language wherever the research has it.** Section 2 asks what customers
want to stop feeling and start feeling. Their words beat your paraphrase of their words every time,
because they were produced by someone in the state you are trying to name. *"I'm tired of waking up
tired"* is worth more than `combats fatigue`.

**Narrow, do not hedge.** Section 2 invites checking every applicable driver and then asks for the
primary transformation. The second step is the one that matters. A worksheet naming four emotional
drivers has not made a decision, and the writer downstream will make it for you, arbitrarily.

**Say why, not just what.** "Problem aware" is a guess wearing a label. *"Problem aware, because
these lines describe the symptom repeatedly and almost never name a product category"* is an
assessment someone can disagree with, which is what makes it useful.

**Where the research runs out, stop and mark it.** A worksheet with four grounded sections and
three flagged as assumptions is genuinely useful. One where the reader cannot tell which is which
is worse than useless.

## What this worksheet is not, and what to send with it

This is the **message** decided. It is not a production brief, and handing it to a writer on its own
leaves them with real questions it has no field for.

Say so when you deliver it, and name what still has to travel alongside:

- **Channel, format and count** — statics, UGC scripts, emails, a landing page? "£54 for ten jars"
  behaves completely differently in a six-second hook and in a subject line, and Section 6's four
  touches are four touches of *something*.
- **Brand voice** — existing copy, or examples of what they like. Section 3 can specify a register
  (warm and self-deprecating, or credentialed and precise) without knowing whether it is theirs.
- **Product specifics a writer needs and research will not contain** — what it tastes like, how it
  is used, what is in the box, the guarantee and cancellation terms. Lines get written out of these.

None of that is a gap in the worksheet; ACA's sheet is about what to say and it does that. It is a
gap in the **hand-off**, and naming it takes one sentence and saves a round trip.

### Section 1 — Audience Awareness Assessment

Place the audience on the spectrum: Unaware · Problem Aware · Solution Aware · Product Aware ·
Most Aware. Table of what each means and its message focus is in `frameworks.md`.

**Fill:** `Based on this assessment, my primary messaging approach should be: ______`

✅ **Good:** *"Problem aware. Of 28 supplied lines, 19 describe the symptom without naming any
product category ('I just assumed I was tired because of work'), and only 3 mention having tried
anything. They have not started shopping for a solution. Messaging should validate the problem
before differentiating anything."*

⛔ **Not good:** *"Problem aware."* That is a guess wearing a label. State the evidence, so someone
can disagree with it.

⚠️ **Use pre-purchase language for this section, not post-purchase.** A review is written by
someone who already bought; they are past the stage you are placing. *"I've tried every collagen
powder going"* is a prospect describing their history and can carry Section 1. *"I bought it for
the collagen but the sleep was the surprise"* is a customer describing an outcome and cannot — it
tells you about buyers, not about the people you are trying to reach. Sort before you use, and say
how many lines the placement actually rests on.

Note a significant secondary stage if there is one, but commit to a primary.

---

### Section 2 — Emotional Drivers Analysis

**Maslow's Hierarchy** — check all that apply:
- [ ] Physiological (basic survival needs)
- [ ] Safety (security, stability, protection)
- [ ] Belonging (connection, relationships, community)
- [ ] Esteem (recognition, status, respect, achievement)
- [ ] Self-actualization (growth, creativity, purpose)

**Life-Force 8** — check all that apply:
- [ ] Survival, enjoyment of life, life extension
- [ ] Enjoyment of food and beverages
- [ ] Freedom from fear, pain, and danger
- [ ] Sexual companionship
- [ ] Comfortable living conditions
- [ ] To be superior; winning
- [ ] Care and protection of loved ones
- [ ] Social approval

**Then narrow to one transformation:**

> **FROM:** [current negative emotion] → **TO:** [desired positive emotion]

> **Helps customers avoid feeling:** [list]
> **Helps customers feel:** [list]

**The narrowing is the point.** Checking four drivers and stopping is not a decision; the writer
downstream will make it for you, arbitrarily.

⚠️ **Emotions, not transactions.** "From tired to energised" is an emotional transformation. "From
not owning it to owning it" is a purchase, and it will not generate a hook. If the FROM state reads
like a description of not having bought yet, redo it.

Use the customer's own words. `I'm tired of waking up tired` beats `combats fatigue`.

### Section 3 — Persuasion Principles Selection

Pick **2 to 3** and say specifically how each will be used. A decision with the execution named,
not a shortlist.

| Principle | Is | How I'll use it |
|---|---|---|
| Reciprocity | Giving something valuable first | |
| Unity | Creating tribal identity | |
| Commitment | Small yeses leading to bigger ones | |
| Loss aversion | What they'll lose by not acting | |
| Social proof | Others like them are using it | |
| Anchoring | Setting value reference points | |
| Authority | Expert endorsement or credentials | |
| Pratfall effect | Strategic vulnerability for credibility | |
| Liking | Rapport and relatability | |
| Scarcity | Limited availability or time | |

⚠️ The course teaches four more this grid omits: **MAYA, distinctiveness, self-centeredness,
primacy and recency**. Consider them; `frameworks.md` has all fourteen.

✅ **Good:** *"Social proof — lead with the 4,200 five-star reviews specifically from parents,
since the research shows buyers checking whether other parents rated it."*
⛔ **Not good:** `Social proof — use testimonials.`

> **CORE MESSAGE:** ______

- **Benefit angle:**
- **Pain avoidance angle:**
- **Status/identity angle:**
- **Process/mechanism angle:**

### Section 5 — Hook Development

- **For unaware / problem aware:** [creates curiosity or names the problem]
- **For solution / product aware:** [differentiates your approach]
- **For most aware:** [urgency or offer]

Each hook must be traceable back to Sections 1 and 2. A hook that does not follow from the
awareness stage and the emotional driver may be a good line, but it is not the output of this
process and should not be presented as though the research produced it.

Both hook menus are in `frameworks.md` if nothing comes.

### Section 7 — Creative Evaluation Checklist

**Deliver this as a gate to run later, against finished creative.** Ticking it against the
worksheet proves nothing; it exists to catch the drift between what was planned and what got made.

- [ ] Does it clearly address the right awareness level?
- [ ] Does it tap into at least one powerful emotional driver?
- [ ] Does it emphasise transformation, not just features?
- [ ] Does it leverage 2 to 3 persuasion principles effectively?
- [ ] Is the hook strong enough to stop the scroll?
- [ ] Is the primary benefit immediately clear?
- [ ] Would I be excited to see this ad if I were the target customer?
- [ ] Have I created pattern interrupts or distinctive elements?
- [ ] Does it address objections the audience might have?
- [ ] Is the call-to-action clear and compelling?

**Areas for improvement:** ______

Question 7 is the one that catches what the others miss. The other nine can all pass on an ad that
is technically correct and lifeless. Answer it as the customer, not as the person who made it.

---

### Section 8 — Winning Creative Formulas

**Nothing to fill.** Four ready-made structures printed at the bottom of the sheet, in ACA's words
*"quick reference formulas that have proven effective across countless campaigns"*, there for when
a section stalls.

| Formula | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Problem → Agitation → Solution** | Identify a painful problem · Intensify the emotion around it · Present your solution as the relief |
| **Feature → Advantage → Benefit** | What it is · Why that matters · How it improves their life |
| **Before → After → Bridge** | Show the painful "before" state · Show the desirable "after" state · Position your product as the bridge |
| **Objection → Counter → Reframe** | Acknowledge their concern · Counter with new information · Reframe the perspective |

Use them to unstick a section, not to replace one:

- **Section 4's benefit frame reads as a spec sheet** → *Feature → Advantage → Benefit*, and take
  the third step. Most feature-frame copy stops at the second.
- **Section 4's pain-avoidance frame will not come** → *Problem → Agitation → Solution*.
- **Section 2's transformation needs to become a script** → *Before → After → Bridge*. It is the
  same FROM/TO, staged.
- **Checklist item 9, objections** → *Objection → Counter → Reframe*.

**Carry them into the delivered worksheet.** They are what the writer receiving it reaches for, and
the artifact is less useful without them.

ACA closes the sheet on this:

### The Claims Register

**Not part of ACA's sheet. Added here, and required.** The section flags above are per-section;
copy is written per-line, and a line is what gets pasted into the ad.

Every line in Sections 4, 5 and 6 carrying a **number**, a **superlative**, or a **claim about what
the product does to a body** gets a row. Tag it inline where it appears — `[C2]` — so the fragment
carries its own pointer when someone copies it.

| # | Claim | Where it appears | Status | Needs before it ships |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | | | | |
| C2 | | | | |

**Status is one of four:**

- **Supplied fact** — the brand's own knowledge of its own product. Usable.
- **⚠️ Client-asserted, unverified** — an estimate, comparison or superlative about the outside
  world. Record it; do not build a line on it.
- **⚠️ Outcome claim, needs substantiation** — what it does to a person.
- **Attributed quote** — one named customer's report. Must stay visibly a quote in the artifact.

**Record the quarantined claims too, with "nowhere" in the where-it-appears column.** A claim that
is simply missing looks overlooked. A claim recorded as deliberately excluded is a decision, and it
stops someone quietly reinstating it three weeks later.

⭐ **The hedged sentence in the brief is usually the most quotable one.** *"Our nutritionist reckons
it's about 3x"* is twice-hedged, unsubstantiated, and born wanting to be a headline. That
combination is exactly what the register exists to catch.

## A worked example

ACA's own, for a supplement brand, carried through the full sheet. **Every line is illustrative** —
a real version needs the brand's actual research, and the mechanism claim in particular would need
to be true of the actual formulation.

⚠️ **This example shows the reasoning, not the delivery format.** ACA gives the layer choices
without the provenance headers, and it is reproduced that way rather than being dressed up. A real
delivered sheet carries a marker on every section and a register at the end. The markers each
section *would* need are noted below, so the gap does not read as permission.

**Sections 1 to 3** (ACA's original example):

> `⚠️ ASSUMPTION, NOT VERIFIED: no research behind these choices — they are a teaching example.`

| Layer | Choice |
|---|---|
| Life-Force drive | Survival and life extension |
| Awareness level | Problem aware |
| Emotional trigger | Fear of decline, hope for vitality |
| Persuasion principles | Authority, scarcity, social proof |

Note the coherence: a problem-aware audience gets validation rather than differentiation, the
trigger is a pair (a fear and its matching hope), and all three principles serve credibility, which
is what a health claim needs most.

**Section 4** — core message: *the tiredness you have accepted as normal is not normal.*

| Frame | Line |
|---|---|
| Benefit | "Wake up before your alarm, because you actually slept" |
| Pain avoidance | "Stop planning your day around the 3pm crash" |
| Status / identity | "The people who seem to have endless energy are not lucky" |
| Process / mechanism | "Why magnesium timing matters more than magnesium dose" |
| Testimonial / story | "I thought I needed more coffee. I needed the opposite." |

**Section 5** — hooks by awareness:

- *Unaware / problem aware:* "You are not tired because you are busy."
- *Solution / product aware:* "Most magnesium supplements use the form your body absorbs worst."
  `[C2]`
- *Most aware:* "Your first month, and if it does nothing, keep it." `[C3]`

Each traces back: the first names a problem someone has normalised, the second differentiates on
mechanism, the third removes friction for someone already convinced.

**The Claims Register for this example**, which is the point of showing it — three of these lines
cannot ship as written, and reading the sheet without the register would not tell you which:

| # | Claim | Where | Status | Needs before it ships |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | "magnesium timing matters more than dose" | S4 mechanism | ⚠️ Outcome claim, needs substantiation | Evidence that timing changes the result for this formulation |
| C2 | "most magnesium supplements use the form your body absorbs worst" | S5 hook 2 | ⚠️ Client-asserted, unverified | A survey of what competitors actually use, and absorption data |
| C3 | "if it does nothing, keep it" | S5 hook 3 | Supplied fact, if true | Confirm the refund terms actually say this |
| C4 | "wake up before your alarm" | S4 benefit | ⚠️ Outcome claim | Substantiation, or soften to a customer quote |

Note that **C2 is the sharpest hook in the set and the one on the weakest ground.** That is the
normal case, not a coincidence: the line that differentiates hardest is usually the line making the
biggest claim about someone else.

**Section 7** — the question that would catch this one is #3, transformation not features. A cut
opening on capsule shots and absorption percentages fails it even though the strategy was sound,
which is exactly the drift the gate exists to find.

**A second ACA example**, for contrast, showing a different shape:

| Layer | Productivity app |
|---|---|
| Maslow level | Esteem needs (achievement, confidence) |
| Life-Force drive | To be superior; winning |
| Awareness level | Solution aware |
| Emotional trigger | Pride, ambition, fear of falling behind |
| Persuasion principles | Social proof, loss aversion, distinctiveness |

Here the audience already knows the category, so the work is differentiation. Maslow and the
Life-Force drive agree with each other, which is a good sign the driver was identified rather than
picked from a list.

### The 5 awareness stages

Section 1. Place the audience, and say what the evidence was.

| Stage | They are | Message focus | Funnel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unaware** | Don't know they have a problem | Problem identification, disruption, curiosity | Top |
| **Problem Aware** | Know the problem, not the solution | Problem validation, education, hope | Top |
| **Solution Aware** | Know solutions exist, not yours | Differentiation, unique mechanism, comparison | Middle |
| **Product Aware** | Know your product, haven't bought | Objection handling, proof, specific benefits | Middle |
| **Most Aware** | Ready to buy with the right offer | Offers, urgency, easy action steps | Bottom |

Tactical detail per stage:

- **Unaware** — raise awareness of the problem itself, use pattern interrupts and curiosity
  triggers, lead with stories that illuminate an unrecognised pain point.
  *Example: "The hidden reason you're exhausted even after 8 hours of sleep"*
- **Problem Aware** — validate the experience ("yes, this is real, and you're not alone"), educate
  about the problem's nature, introduce solution categories **without pitching your product yet**
- **Solution Aware** — differentiate from alternatives, focus on unique mechanisms, show why your
  category is superior.
  *Example: "Why natural supplements outperform synthetic options for sustainable energy"*
- **Product Aware** — address specific objections, provide proof (studies, testimonials,
  demonstrations), compare directly to what they are considering
- **Most Aware** — urgency, scarcity, offers, reduce friction, reinforce with guarantees

⚠️ **One audience is rarely at one stage.** Broad targeting puts all five in the same ad set. Name
the *primary* stage for the worksheet, and note if a second is significant.

### Market sophistication

Awareness is about the person; sophistication is about the market. As a category matures, plain
claims stop working because everyone makes them.

Two escapes:

1. **A unique mechanism** — *"how our proprietary technology works differently"*. Not a bigger
   claim, a different reason the claim is true.
2. **Reframe the problem** — *"you've been solving the wrong problem all along"*. Moves the
   conversation rather than competing inside it.

Diagnostic: if every competitor ad makes the same promise, the promise is spent.

### Maslow's hierarchy

Section 2, the coarse cut. Check all that apply.

1. **Physiological** — basic survival needs
2. **Safety** — security, stability, protection
3. **Belonging** — connection, relationships, community
4. **Esteem** — recognition, status, respect, achievement
5. **Self-actualization** — growth, creativity, purpose

### The Life-Force 8

Section 2, the sharp cut. These are the ones that produce copy.

| Drive | What it is |
|---|---|
| **1. Survival, enjoyment of life, life extension** | Products promising health, vitality or safety |
| **2. Enjoyment of food and beverages** | We are programmed to seek pleasurable taste |
| **3. Freedom from fear, pain, and danger** | People spend heavily to eliminate worry: financial, physical, emotional |
| **4. Sexual companionship** | Beyond romance: feeling desirable and attractive |
| **5. Comfortable living conditions** | Products that make life easier, more efficient, more luxurious |
| **6. To be superior; winning** | Status, achievement, recognition |
| **7. Care and protection of loved ones** | Powerful for parents, pet owners, caregivers |
| **8. Social approval** | Acceptance and respect from peers |

**Tie-breaker when several apply:** which drive explains why they would buy **now**, rather than
agreeing the product is nice in principle? Freedom from fear and care of loved ones tend to produce
urgency; comfortable living and social approval tend to produce agreement.

### The 14 persuasion principles

Section 3. Select **2 to 3** and say how each will be used.

⚠️ **The printed worksheet's grid lists only 10.** All fourteen are here. The four the grid omits
are marked, and two of them (distinctiveness, self-centeredness) are among the most commonly
violated.

**Cialdini's seven:**

| Principle | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Reciprocity** | Giving something valuable first creates obligation |
| **Commitment and consistency** | Small yeses lead to bigger ones |
| **Social proof** | When uncertain, people follow others. Strongest when the others are recognisably *like them* |
| **Authority** | Credentials transfer confidence, fastest where buyers cannot judge quality themselves |
| **Liking** | Rapport and relatability. Much of why creator-led ads work |
| **Scarcity** | Limited availability or time. Converts, and easiest to abuse |
| **Unity** | Shared identity, a sense of *us*. Strongest and slowest to build |

**The seven the course adds:**

| Principle | Mechanism | On the grid? |
|---|---|---|
| **MAYA** (Most Advanced Yet Acceptable) | The most novel thing they will still accept. Useful for unfamiliar mechanisms | ⛔ omitted |
| **Loss aversion** | Losing hurts more than gaining pleases. Reframe the same fact as what they lose | ✅ |
| **Anchoring** | First information sets the reference point. Set value before naming price | ✅ |
| **Pratfall effect** | Minor flaws make strong claims believable. A claim with no admitted downside reads as advertising | ✅ |
| **Distinctiveness** | What stands out gets remembered. A memory argument as much as persuasion | ⛔ omitted |
| **Self-centeredness** | People care about their problems, not your product. The corrective for copy opening with the company | ⛔ omitted |
| **Primacy and recency** | Beginning and end are remembered. The hook and CTA carry disproportionate weight | ⛔ omitted |

**Choosing:** pick what the *evidence* supports. Social proof without numbers, authority without
credentials and scarcity without real limits are claims the audience can check.

Pairings that compound: authority + social proof · scarcity + loss aversion (and both push urgency,
so overuse reads as pressure) · pratfall + authority (admitting a limitation makes expertise more
credible). Liking + authority pull against each other; warm-and-relatable sits awkwardly beside
credentialed-and-expert, and the ad has to pick a register.

### The 5 framing angles

Section 4. One core message, five ways to say it. Not five claims.

| Frame | Answers | Pulls from |
|---|---|---|
| **Benefit** | What do I get? | The "TO" side of the transformation |
| **Pain avoidance** | What do I stop suffering? | The "FROM" side |
| **Status / identity** | Who does this make me? | Social approval, to be superior, esteem |
| **Process / mechanism** | Why does it work? | The sophistication escape |
| **Testimonial / story** | Who else has done this? | Social proof |

Two checks: could a competitor put their name on any line unchanged (then it describes the
category, not the product)? And do all five read as one argument from five angles, or as five
different products (then the core message was never settled)?

### The two hook menus

Section 5. Use when a hook will not come, or to force variety past the usual two or three shapes.

**Ten psychological approaches** — what the hook *does to the viewer*:
FOMO · curiosity gap · identity reinforcement · unexpected statement or statistic · problem
agitation · dream scenario · pattern interrupt · status elevation · loss aversion · before/after
contrast

**Ten structural approaches** — what *shape the sentence takes*:
question-based · statistic-based · story-based · identity-based · pain-point · curiosity ·
counterintuitive statement · bold promise · future-pacing · exclusivity

Picking one from each list produces combinations the obvious approach misses.

⚠️ A hook containing a specific claim (a number, a timeframe, a result) needs that claim to be
real. If the research does not support it, soften it or mark it as needing verification before
ship.

### The 4-exposure sequence

Section 6, for multi-touch campaigns only.

| Exposure | Job |
|---|---|
| **1st** | Introduce the problem |
| **2nd** | Reveal your unique solution |
| **3rd** | Provide proof it works |
| **4th** | Present the offer with urgency |

This walks someone from problem aware to most aware across four touches instead of asking one ad to
do all four jobs, which is what produces the overstuffed ad.

⚠️ The awareness target should **advance** down the rows. Same target in row 1 and row 4 means the
plan repeats rather than sequences.

Each ad still has to work standing alone: someone may enter at exposure 3.

## The AI prompts

Ad Creative Academy ships 20 prompts with Course 102. All 20 are here, reproduced as written, but
**grouped by the worksheet section they feed** rather than by ACA's own categories, so you can find
the one that unblocks the section you are stuck on.

The `[BRACKETED]` placeholders are the interface. Fill them; do not rewrite them.

### ⚠️ Read this before running any of them

**These prompts do not check whether you gave them anything.** Prompt 4 asked to map an emotional
transformation with the product field filled and the audience field guessed will produce a complete,
confident, well-written transformation map built on nothing. It reads exactly like one built on 300
reviews.

So the rule from `SKILL.md` applies harder here, not less:

- A prompt whose bracket says `[INSERT CUSTOMER FEEDBACK]` or `[INSERT AUDIENCE DATA/RESEARCH]`
  **needs real material pasted in.** If you have none, that prompt is not available to you yet.
- A prompt that only needs product and audience descriptions can run on what you know, and its
  output goes into the worksheet flagged as an assumption.
- Whatever comes back is a **first draft with no provenance attached**. Attaching it is your job:
  `GROUNDED IN:` if it came from supplied research, `⚠️ ASSUMPTION, NOT VERIFIED:` if it did not.

ACA says the same thing at the end of the pack, and it is worth taking literally:

> These prompts should be adapted to your specific needs and combined with your strategic judgment
> as a Creative Strategist. **They are tools to enhance your process, not replace your expertise.**

## Section 1 — Audience awareness

#### Prompt 1 — Awareness Stage Assessment

Needs real research pasted in. This is the one that grounds Section 1.

```
Analyze the following target audience for [PRODUCT/SERVICE]. Based on market research data below,
identify which stage of awareness they're in (Unaware, Problem Aware, Solution Aware, Product
Aware, or Most Aware) and explain why:

[INSERT AUDIENCE DATA/RESEARCH]

For each awareness stage, suggest how our messaging should shift to match their current
understanding.
```

Take the *why* it gives you, not just the label. Section 1 asks for the evidence, and "problem
aware" with no reasoning attached is the answer the worksheet explicitly rejects.

#### Prompt 3 — Market Sophistication Analysis

Needs competitor ads pasted in. Feeds the mechanism frame in Section 4 as much as Section 1.

```
Analyze the current advertising landscape for [PRODUCT CATEGORY]:

[INSERT COMPETITOR ADS/MESSAGING]

Based on Eugene Schwartz's 5 levels of market sophistication:
1. What level of sophistication does this market demonstrate?
2. What claims/promises are becoming cliché or overused?
3. What new angle or mechanism could we focus on to stand out?
4. Draft 3 potential headlines that would break through the current sophistication level.
```

⚠️ Question 3 will invent a mechanism if you let it. **A mechanism has to be true of the actual
product.** Take the direction, then check it against what the thing really does before it reaches
the worksheet.

## Section 2 — Emotional drivers

#### Prompt 2 — Audience Pain Point Mining

⭐ **The highest-value prompt in the pack for this worksheet.** It is the one that turns a pile of
reviews into Section 2, and it returns the customer's own language, which is what Section 2 is
asking for.

```
Review these [CUSTOMER REVIEWS/SURVEY RESPONSES/SOCIAL MEDIA COMMENTS] about [PRODUCT CATEGORY]:

[INSERT CUSTOMER FEEDBACK]

Identify:
1. The top 5 emotional pain points expressed
2. The specific language patterns customers use to describe their problems
3. Which of the Life-Force 8 drives these pain points connect to
4. How I should frame my messaging to address these specific emotional triggers
```

Ask it to quote, not paraphrase. Output 2 is only worth having if the phrases come back intact.

#### Prompt 4 — Emotional Transformation Mapping

Produces the FROM/TO transformation Section 2 ends on.

```
For [PRODUCT/SERVICE], help me identify the emotional transformation it provides:

Product details: [INSERT PRODUCT INFORMATION]
Target audience: [INSERT AUDIENCE DESCRIPTION]

Create a detailed transformation map showing:
1. The "BEFORE" state (negative emotions, frustrations, pains)
2. The "AFTER" state (positive emotions, benefits, outcomes)
3. Connect each transformation to specific Maslow's needs or Life-Force 8 drives
4. Generate 5 powerful hooks that highlight this transformation journey
```

⚠️ This one runs on descriptions alone, so it is the easiest in the pack to run on nothing. Paste
the output of Prompt 2 into `[INSERT AUDIENCE DESCRIPTION]` rather than describing the audience
from memory, and the map arrives grounded.

Check the BEFORE state is an emotion and not a purchase. "Has not bought yet" is the failure
Section 2 names.

#### Prompt 6 — Life-Force 8 Messaging Development

Run after you have picked the drive. Tests whether the drive actually produces copy, which is the
real check on whether you picked the right one.

```
Our product [PRODUCT] addresses the Life-Force drive of [SPECIFIC DRIVE]. Create 5 messaging
frameworks that tap into this fundamental desire:

Product details: [PRODUCT DETAILS]
Target audience: [AUDIENCE DETAILS]

For each framework provide:
1. A headline that triggers this drive
2. Supporting body copy (2-3 sentences)
3. A call-to-action that reinforces the drive
```

## Section 3 — Persuasion principles

#### Prompt 7 — Strategic Principle Application

Run it once per principle you shortlisted. Output 1 is what fills the "How I'll use it" column, and
output 4 is what stops the principle being used badly.

```
I want to apply the persuasion principle of [PRINCIPLE] to my ad for [PRODUCT].

Product details: [PRODUCT DETAILS]
Target audience: [AUDIENCE DETAILS]
Current messaging: [CURRENT MESSAGING]

Provide:
1. 3 specific ways to incorporate this principle authentically
2. Examples of how this principle could be visualized in creative
3. How to phrase my CTA to reinforce this principle
4. Potential pitfalls to avoid when applying this principle
```

Works for any of the fourteen in `frameworks.md`, not only the ten printed on the worksheet grid.

#### Prompt 8 — Social Proof Strategy

⚠️ Needs your real metrics. Social proof with invented numbers is the fastest way to make a
worksheet dangerous, because the number travels into the ad and the ad is checkable.

```
Help me develop a social proof strategy for [PRODUCT/SERVICE]:

Our key metrics: [INSERT RELEVANT METRICS]
Audience: [AUDIENCE DETAILS]

Generate:
1. 5 ways to present our customer numbers/reviews most effectively
2. How to frame testimonials for maximum impact
3. Social proof elements to include in different ad placements
4. Language templates that convert abstract social proof into concrete emotional benefits
```

#### Prompt 9 — Authority Building Elements

Same warning: `[INSERT CREDENTIALS/EXPERTISE]` means credentials you actually hold.

```

Our credentials: [INSERT CREDENTIALS/EXPERTISE]
Audience pain points: [INSERT PAIN POINTS]

Provide:
1. How to present our expertise without sounding boastful
2. Ways to connect our authority directly to customer benefits
3. Authority signals to include in different parts of our funnel
4. Trust-building language that establishes credibility quickly
```

## Section 4 — Message framing matrix

#### Prompt 15 — Before-After-Bridge Frameworks

Feeds the matrix directly: five versions off one transformation is close to the five frames the
section wants, and it is built from Section 2 rather than alongside it.

```
Using the Before-After-Bridge framework, create 5 different messaging structures for [PRODUCT]:

Product details: [PRODUCT DETAILS]
Target audience: [AUDIENCE DETAILS]
Key transformation: [KEY TRANSFORMATION]

For each version:
1. Before: Vividly describe the painful/frustrating "before" state
2. After: Paint an emotionally compelling picture of the "after" state
3. Bridge: Position our product as the path between these states

Each version should focus on a different emotional driver or pain point.
```

#### Prompt 17 — Creative Variation Generator

Its five framings are not the worksheet's five frames, but they overlap enough to be useful when

```
Original concept: [ORIGINAL CONCEPT]
Product details: [PRODUCT DETAILS]
Target audience: [AUDIENCE DETAILS]

Generate variations using:
1. Fear of loss framing
2. Aspiration/identity framing
3. Proof/credibility framing
4. Curiosity/mystery framing
5. Belonging/tribal framing

Keep the core benefit consistent but change the psychological angle.
```

## Section 5 — Hook development

#### Prompt 5 — Multi-Angle Hook Generator

The ten **psychological** approaches: what the hook does to the viewer.

```
For our [PRODUCT/SERVICE], generate 10 scroll-stopping hooks using different psychological
approaches:

Product: [PRODUCT DESCRIPTION]
Audience: [AUDIENCE DESCRIPTION]
Awareness level: [AWARENESS STAGE]

- Curiosity gap
- Identity reinforcement
- Unexpected statement/statistic
- Problem agitation
- Dream scenario
- Pattern interrupt
- Status elevation
- Loss aversion
- Before/after contrast
```

Note the awareness field. Fill it from Section 1, and the hooks arrive aimed at somebody.

#### Prompt 12 — Multiple Hook Variations

The ten **structural** approaches: what shape the sentence takes. Running 5 and 12 and crossing
them is how you get past the two or three hook shapes everyone defaults to.

```
For our upcoming ad about [PRODUCT/FEATURE], generate 10 different hook variations:

Current messaging: [CURRENT MESSAGING]
Target audience: [TARGET AUDIENCE]
Key benefit: [KEY BENEFIT]
Awareness level: [AWARENESS LEVEL]

Create hooks using different approaches:
- Question-based hook
- Statistic-based hook
- Story-based hook
- Identity-based hook
- Pain-point hook
- Curiosity hook
- Counterintuitive statement hook
- Bold promise hook
- Future-pacing hook
- Exclusivity hook
```

⚠️ Statistic-based and bold-promise hooks will arrive with invented numbers unless you supplied

#### Prompt 19 — Meta/TikTok Ad Hooks Based on Awareness

Section 5 does not ask for platform splits, but if you know where this is running, this produces

```
1. Address the specific awareness level
```

## Section 6 — Sequencing plan

#### Prompt 13 — Awareness Journey Sequencing

Maps straight onto the four rows. The starting and goal awareness levels come from Section 1, which
is what stops the sequence repeating instead of advancing.

```
Help me create a sequenced messaging strategy that moves customers from [STARTING AWARENESS LEVEL]
to [GOAL AWARENESS LEVEL] for [PRODUCT]:

Audience characteristics: [AUDIENCE DETAILS]

1. Initial message focus and hook
2. Second touch messaging evolution
3. Third touch messaging evolution
4. Final conversion-focused message

For each stage, explain which psychological elements to emphasize and why.
```

## Section 7 — Evaluation

#### Prompt 16 — Psychology-Based Creative Audit

**This is the Section 7 gate, run by machine.** Point it at finished creative, not at the
worksheet. Its six questions cover most of the ten checklist items.

```
Evaluate this ad creative against consumer psychology best practices:

[INSERT AD COPY/CREATIVE]

Analyze:
1. Which awareness stage does this address? Is it appropriate?
2. Which emotional drivers does it trigger, if any?
3. Which persuasion principles are used effectively or missed?
4. How clear is the transformation being offered?
5. Rate the hook's stopping power (1-10) and suggest improvements
6. Is the CTA psychologically aligned with the emotional appeal?

Provide specific recommendations for psychological enhancement.
```

⚠️ It does not ask the checklist's question 7: *would I be excited to see this if I were the target
customer?* That one is not a machine's to answer. Ask it yourself after this runs.

#### Prompt 14 — Objection Handler Development

Serves checklist item 9, *does it address objections the audience might have?* Best run against
objections you found in real research rather than ones you imagined.

```
Based on these common objections to [PRODUCT/SERVICE]:

[LIST OBJECTIONS]

Create persuasive responses that:
1. Acknowledge the concern empathetically
2. Reframe the objection using psychological principles
3. Provide evidence that overcomes the objection
4. Return to the core emotional benefit

Address each objection using at least one persuasion principle from Cialdini's framework.
```

⚠️ Point 3, "provide evidence", is an invitation to fabricate. Evidence you do not have is not
evidence.
