# Fill the Consumer Psychology Worksheet

Ad Creative Academy's Course 102 artifact, produced for a specific brand. This skill runs the
worksheet. It does not teach the psychology behind it, beyond what is needed to fill each section
correctly.

**The output is a document someone else can use.** It gets handed to a writer, argued with in a
review, and referred back to when creative drifts. Produce it as filled sections with headings, not
as prose.

## What this skill needs from you

**Required:**

1. **The product or service**, and what it actually does
2. **Who it is for**, as far as you know
3. **Research on those people** — reviews, support tickets, sales-call notes, survey responses,
   social comments, or your own notes from talking to them

**Optional, and it sharpens the artifact:**

4. Current messaging or existing ads
5. Price point and the offer
6. What competitors are saying
7. Whether this is for one ad or a multi-touch campaign (decides whether Section 6 matters)

### If something is missing, ask. Do not invent it.

The worksheet is only worth what it is grounded in. A fabricated pain point produces a worksheet
that reads exactly like a real one and sends a writer in the wrong direction, and nobody downstream
can tell.

Three situations, and the middle one is the trap:

**1. Research supplied.** Work from it. Quote it.

**2. ⚠️ A source NAMED but not supplied** — "we have 400 reviews", "sales talks to them daily",
"there's loads of comments". **This is not the same as having the research.** The sentence sounds
like evidence arrived; nothing did. Ask for a bounded slice rather than the whole corpus:

> Paste 20 to 30 review lines, weighted towards the critical ones and anything that repeats, plus
> any themes you have already spotted. That is enough to ground Sections 1 and 2.

Asking for all 400 gets refused. Asking for a specific, achievable slice gets an answer. Skew the
request to negative and repeated reviews deliberately: complaints carry the pain point in the
customer's own words, which is exactly what Section 2 needs.

**Then produce the sheet anyway**, on what you have, flagged. Asking and delivering nothing is not
a service to someone with a deadline. Send the ask **and** the draft, and say which sections the
answer would upgrade.

**3. No research at all.** Say what would be needed, then offer to produce a draft on assumptions
**marked as assumptions inside the worksheet itself**.

### ⚠️ A handful of quotes is not the corpus

The common real situation is not one of the three above. It is a corpus **named** plus a few lines
**hand-picked by the person selling the product**. Two rules:

- **Below roughly ten customer lines, Section 1's stage placement is an assumption**, however
  confident the lines sound. A small sample can carry Section 2's *language* — the words are real
  whatever else is true — but it cannot establish where a population sits. State the sample size in
  the section header and flag the placement accordingly.
- **Say who selected them.** Lines chosen by the brand skew positive and skew towards what the brand
  already believes. That is not dishonesty, it is what happens when someone picks their favourite
  four. Note it, and it costs nothing.

### ⚠️ Reviews are written by people who have finished shopping

A review is customer voice, not prospect voice, and Section 1 asks about prospects. Someone who
bought and came back to write about it is by definition past the awareness stage you are trying to
place.

Sort what you are given before using it:

- **Pre-purchase language** — describing the problem, what they tried, why they were sceptical.
  This can carry Section 1.
- **Post-purchase language** — what surprised them, what they now do differently, whether it was
  worth it. Gold for Sections 3 and 4, and **evidence about buyers, not about the people you are
  trying to reach.** Using it to place the awareness stage produces a confident, wrong answer.

### What may be reasoned, what must be supplied, and what "supplied" does not mean

| Fair to reason towards, flagged | Must come from you |
|---|---|
| How this product category generally behaves | What **this** brand's customers actually say |
| Which awareness stage a category typically sits at | Any specific number, claim, timeframe or result |
| Which emotional drivers a category usually touches | Testimonial content, review quotes, named benefits |
| Structural advice: which angle suits which stage | Anything a customer could disprove |

General category reasoning is a legitimate draft. **Brand-specific invention is not**, even
flagged, because a fabricated quote outlives the flag attached to it.

⛔ **But "the client said it" is not the same as "it is true", and this is where sheets get people
into trouble.** Two very different things arrive labelled as supplied:

| A supplied **fact** | A supplied **assertion** |
|---|---|
| They know it because it is theirs: price, ingredients, process, how long it simmers, what is in the box | An estimate, a comparison, or a superlative about the outside world |
| *"£6.50 a jar", "18-hour simmer", "10-jar subscription"* | *"our nutritionist reckons about 3x the collagen", "we're the only one doing this", "competitors all say X"* |
| Use it | **Quarantine it.** Record it, do not build a line on it |

The tell is whether the claim is checkable by someone outside the company — a competitor, a
customer, a regulator. Anything hedged (*"reckons"*, *"about"*, *"I think"*) is an internal
estimate wearing the clothes of a fact, and it is usually the most quotable sentence in the brief,
which is exactly why it ends up in the ad.

## How to run it

**Work the sections in order.** Later ones depend on earlier ones: the hooks in Section 5 are
supposed to follow from the awareness stage in Section 1 and the emotional driver in Section 2.
Filling 5 first produces hooks that sound fine and are aimed at nobody.

1. **Confirm the inputs.** Name what you have and what is missing. Ask for the missing required
   ones before starting.
2. **Fill Sections 1 to 3** — awareness, emotional drivers, persuasion principles. These are the
   grounded ones and they carry the rest.
3. **Fill Sections 4 and 5** — the framing matrix and the hooks. These are derived from 1 to 3, not
   invented alongside them.
4. **Fill Section 6** only if this is a multi-touch campaign. Say so and skip it if it is one ad.
5. **Deliver Section 7 as a checklist to run later**, against finished creative. It is a gate, not
   a summary, and ticking it against the worksheet proves nothing.
6. **Flag every section's provenance.** Open each with one of the three markers below.
7. **Carry Section 8 through unchanged.** Four ready-made formulas, nothing to fill. They are what
   the writer receiving this reaches for, along with the line the sheet closes on: the goal is not
   manipulation, it is connecting people who have real problems with something that helps.
8. **Deliver a Claims Register.** Required, not optional. See below — it is the part that stops a
   flagged worksheet turning into an unflagged ad.

### The three provenance markers

Open every filled section with whichever applies:

```
GROUNDED IN: [what was supplied — e.g. 34 Amazon review quotes, Oct 2025]
⚠️ ASSUMPTION, NOT VERIFIED: [what was reasoned about the category, and what would confirm it]
⚠️ CLIENT-ASSERTED, UNVERIFIED: [what they told you that nobody has checked]
```

The third one matters as much as the other two. Without it, a hedged internal estimate arrives
inside the same header as a hard fact and is silently promoted.

### ⛔ The Claims Register, and why the section flags are not enough

**Nobody copies a section. They copy a line.**

A flag on line one of Section 4 does not travel with line four of Section 4. The copywriter opens a
document and pastes `eighteen hours in the pot` and `3x the collagen` — seven-word fragments,
which are the unit that actually moves through a business. Both arrive at the ad with no history,
and three weeks later nobody can reconstruct where the number came from.

So the worksheet ends with a register, and it is a required output field:

| Claim | Where it appears | Status | What it needs before it ships |
|---|---|---|---|
| 18-hour simmer | S4 mechanism, S5 hook 3 | Client-supplied process fact | Confirm it is always 18h, not "up to" |
| "3x the collagen" | **Nowhere — quarantined** | ⚠️ Client-asserted, hedged | Lab data, named comparator, claims review |
| "The only 18-hour simmer" | **Nowhere — quarantined** | ⚠️ Unverified superlative | A documented competitor audit |
| ~900 reviews | S3 social proof | Count not confirmed | Exact figure and rating on the day of use |

**Every line in Sections 4, 5 and 6 that carries a number, a superlative, or a claim about what the
product does to a body gets a row.** Tag it inline where it appears (`[C2]`) so the fragment carries
its own pointer when it is copied.

**Write the quarantined ones down too, and say where they are not used.** A claim that is missing
from the register looks overlooked; a claim recorded as deliberately excluded is a decision, and it
stops someone else quietly reinstating it.

### On claims about what the product does to a body

The frameworks here are equally good at selling something that works and something that does not,
which is why this matters in health, supplements, food, beauty, finance and anything with a
regulator.

Three different things, and they are not interchangeable:

- **A mechanism claim** — how it is made, what is in it, what the process is. Usually the brand's
  own knowledge and the safest ground. It is also the sophistication escape, so this is a happy
  alignment rather than a compromise.
- **An outcome claim** — what it does to the person. Needs substantiation the brand actually holds,
  and often a regulatory check. Do not generate these from research; research tells you what
  customers *say*, which is not the same as what is *demonstrated*.
- **An attributed customer quote** — what one named person reported. Different from a brand claim
  and must stay visibly different in the artifact, because the moment it loses its quotation marks
  it has become one.

You are not the compliance check and should not pretend to be. **Sort claims into those three
buckets, put the outcome claims in the register, and say plainly that they need substantiation
before use.** That is the strategist's job and it is genuinely useful; guessing at what is legal is
neither.

## Getting the output right

**Quote the customer's own language wherever the research has it.** Section 2 asks what customers
want to stop feeling and start feeling. Their words beat your paraphrase of their words every time,
because they were produced by someone in the state you are trying to name. *"I'm tired of waking up
tired"* is worth more than `combats fatigue`.

**Narrow, do not hedge.** Section 2 invites checking every applicable driver and then asks for the
primary transformation. The second step is the one that matters. A worksheet naming four emotional
drivers has not made a decision, and the writer downstream will make it for you, arbitrarily.

**Say why, not just what.** "Problem aware" is a guess wearing a label. *"Problem aware, because
these lines describe the symptom repeatedly and almost never name a product category"* is an
assessment someone can disagree with, which is what makes it useful.

**Where the research runs out, stop and mark it.** A worksheet with four grounded sections and
three flagged as assumptions is genuinely useful. One where the reader cannot tell which is which
is worse than useless.

## What this worksheet is not, and what to send with it

This is the **message** decided. It is not a production brief, and handing it to a writer on its own
leaves them with real questions it has no field for.

Say so when you deliver it, and name what still has to travel alongside:

- **Channel, format and count** — statics, UGC scripts, emails, a landing page? "£54 for ten jars"
  behaves completely differently in a six-second hook and in a subject line, and Section 6's four
  touches are four touches of *something*.
- **Brand voice** — existing copy, or examples of what they like. Section 3 can specify a register
  (warm and self-deprecating, or credentialed and precise) without knowing whether it is theirs.
- **Product specifics a writer needs and research will not contain** — what it tastes like, how it
  is used, what is in the box, the guarantee and cancellation terms. Lines get written out of these.

None of that is a gap in the worksheet; ACA's sheet is about what to say and it does that. It is a
gap in the **hand-off**, and naming it takes one sentence and saves a round trip.
