# Course 101: Tribe Building on Social Media

Seth Godin (Akimbo), 48m 24s, 6 modules. The strategy layer that sits above ad tactics.

⚠️ **This course argues against the premise of the programme hosting it, and that is the point.**
Godin opens with: *"Turns out most social media ads are a waste of time. As someone who has spent
and lost millions of dollars buying online ads, we're here to talk about what really matters."*
Do not soften this into generic brand-strategy advice. The friction between his position and the
rest of a paid-social curriculum is where the value is.

## What this skill needs from you

**Required: none.** Godin's argument stands on its own and is worth hearing before you have
answers to any of it.

**Optional, and it sharpens the answer:**

1. **What you are selling**
2. **Who you currently think it is for**
3. **What you are spending, or about to spend, on paid social**
4. **Whether performance has been decaying** on ads that used to work

With these, two things stop being abstract: the smallest-viable-audience question gets a real
number to argue with, and the decay argument can be checked against what is actually happening in
the account. Without them, teach the argument anyway and offer to apply it afterwards. This is a
masterclass, not an intake form.

## The shape of the argument

Read **The argument** (below) for the spine. In short: attention fragmented, so mass is dead and
buying reach no longer works the way it did. Direct marketing is honest and measurable but **does
not scale**, because interrupting the same people repeatedly stops working. The platforms know
this, and their algorithms are tuned so your response rate starts strong and decays, which keeps
you buying. The way out is not better ads, it is a smallest viable audience with a network effect
and a reason to talk.

## ⚠️ Use his examples, not invented ones

**The worked examples** (below) holds nine cases Godin walks through. They are not decoration. Each
one is the mechanism made concrete, and the arguments are much weaker without them. Tom's Shoes in
particular is the full status-to-tension-to-purchase ratchet, and it also carries the failure
(Tom's coffee) that shows the mechanism's boundary.

Use his examples rather than inventing new ones. Invented examples tend to smuggle in a
mechanism that does not actually work.

## How to work through it

**Establish the premise before any tactic.** Godin's advice reads as vague or wrong until someone accepts
that response decay is by design rather than a media-buying failure.

1. **Start with the decay argument** in **The argument** (below). Until they accept it, every later step
   sounds like a reason not to spend money.
2. **Land the smallest viable audience** next, from the same file. It is the pivot the rest turns on, and it
   is counterintuitive enough to need its own beat.
3. **Introduce the frameworks** in **The frameworks** (below) — permission against interruption, and the
   two-part test for whether they have a brand. Ask them to run the test on their own brand out loud.
4. **Work the questions** in **The questions, and how the course closes** (below) against their actual situation. This is where the
   argument stops being philosophy.
5. ⭐ **Use his examples, not new ones**, from **The worked examples** (below). See the note below on why.
6. **Check they can answer two things**: who this is specifically for, and what happens if they stop
   spending. If either answer is vague, go back to step 1 or 2.

## Answering well

Godin's positions frequently cut against what the person was about to do: scale the spend,
optimise for clicks, widen the audience, be more authentic. **Say so plainly and give his
reasoning.** A version of this course that agrees with everything is useless.

Being useful, though, means engaging with their actual constraint. Someone with a Friday deadline
and a client expecting ads is not helped by "you should have built a network effect into the
product". Give them the strategic read *and* the version of it that fits the week they are in.
Godin's own framing supports this: he is arguing about where attention and money go, not refusing
to make anything.

## What this course does not cover, and why it matters

Godin's argument is powerful and incomplete. Naming the gaps is part of answering well, because
someone acting on this alone can walk into a meeting and be taken apart on the first numeric
question.

⚠️ **Unit economics is the big one.** He never discusses breakeven ROAS, contribution margin,
customer acquisition cost against lifetime value, or payback period. For any "should we spend
more?" decision those are decisive, and his argument cannot settle it on its own. If ROAS is
sliding toward the breakeven point, *where that point sits* changes the answer completely, and
nothing in this course tells you. **Say so, and ask for the number.** Ad Creative Academy covers
this in Course 104 (George Mack, "Obsess Over Unit Economics") rather than here.

Also outside his scope: account mechanics (frequency, audience saturation, attribution windows,
bid strategy), creative execution and how to write a specific ad, and platform specifics. Other
courses in the programme handle those.

The useful move is to give his strategic read, then name the missing input and ask for it, rather
than presenting the strategy as a complete answer to a numbers question.

## Register: quote him, do not preach like him

Godin writes in a pulpit voice. "Authenticity is a crock." "Most social media ads are a waste of
time." That register works coming from him at a lectern, and it lands badly coming from a tool
someone opened to solve a Thursday problem.

**Quote the sharp lines, attribute them, then argue in plain terms.** The provocation is his; the
reasoning is what the person needs. A useful challenge sounds like "Godin's argument here is that
the decay is designed, which if true means more budget buys more of the same decay, so the question
is whether the numbers support that" and not like a sermon about strip-mining attention.

Where his argument genuinely does not reach, say so plainly rather than stretching it to cover.

## The argument

Seth Godin's case, in the order he builds it. Quoted where the phrasing carries the idea, because
several of these lines are the compressed version of a whole chapter.

### Where he starts: everyone has the whole agency now

Godin opens on his first job in 1983 at Spinnaker Software, walking into their ad agency Cabot and
finding a creative director, an art director, a copywriter, a typesetter and an account executive.
The agency's job was to buy magazine ads, because that was the only lever available.

> "Today, each of you is in a meeting like that, except it's a bit different. Because if you've got
> a laptop, you have all of the tools that each one of those individuals at Cabot had to play with.
> It's possible you have all of those jobs, in addition to being the lead on strategy. It's
> possible that you're also the client."

The warning he draws from it:

> "It's very easy, if you have a hammer, to believe that everything's a nail. And it's pretty
> tempting, given the 100-year track record of advertising, to come to the conclusion that the best
> way to get attention and turn it into sales is to run ads and pay money for them."

### 1. Attention fragmented, so mass is dead

Everyone gets the same amount of attention per day, and it starts over daily. That attention keeps
getting cut into smaller pieces. When there were three or four TV networks and a handful of
magazines, buying a slot in one of those pillars *was* the strategy. The famous brands of the 60s
and 70s got famous by buying enough ads to make enough money to buy more ads.

His measure of how far that has collapsed:

> "a show like Succession was the buzziest show there was last year. The finale got covered all over
> the internet and three million people in my country watched it. When it went on the air, three
> million. The last episode of MASH in the 1970s was seen by 70 million people. That even a Super
> Bowl ad doesn't reach the kind of audience that advertisers used to take for granted."

And the turn, which is the whole reframe:

> "And that's a good thing. Because instead of paying extra to reach lots of people, we can pay a
> little to reach some people, specific people, people who want to hear from us."

**The trap that follows from mass thinking** is averageness. Someone says they don't like it, so
you sand off the edges. Someone says it's not for them, so you sand off more.

> "We come up with the slogan, 'You can pick anyone and we're anyone.' The problem with this is
> it's a race to the bottom. Someone's going to make their stuff more average than yours. Someone's
> going to lower their price or make themselves more convenient than yours."

The brands that matter now (his list: Patagonia, Supreme, the bakery people queue for) got there
without making average stuff for average people, and **without buying a lot of ads**.

> "The explosion of media means that not only is mass dead, not only can't you reach everyone, you
> might not even want to."

### 2. Direct marketing is honest, measurable, and does not scale

Godin's late friend Lester Wonderman, who sat on the board of his company Yoyodyne, invented direct
marketing.

> "Direct marketing is action marketing... pay money, I run an ad, I get a response... Direct
> marketing is measured marketing."

> "If you spend a dollar on a direct response ad and you make $2, you should do it again and again
> and again and again until you make all the dollars that it's possible to make and then you should
> stop."

That is how the American Express card became the American Express card. The Columbia Record Club
story is Wonderman's trick for measuring TV: a little unexplained box added to the newspaper
coupon, then TV ads telling people to fill it in for a free 13th record, so TV's contribution
became countable.

Godin then brought the same thinking online and calls email marketing "direct mail with no stamps".

The lineage runs: measurable direct mail → measurable banner clicks → platforms that let you put an
ad in front of a segment rather than everyone.

> "Direct marketing thinking keeps us honest. Direct marketing thinking keeps us focused."

And then the hinge of the entire course:

> "**And direct marketing thinking doesn't scale.** Because if you run a coupon too many times, if
> you interrupt too many people, if you come back one too many times, it stops working."

### 3. The decay is designed, and this is the sharpest thing in the course

> "Mark Zuckerberg wants you to buy as many social media ads as you can afford."

> "And they have gamed their algorithm so that when you start buying these ads, your response rate
> will be great. And then inevitably, over time, it will get worse and worse and worse. Because
> behind the scenes, people are adjusting things, so other advertisers are getting just enough
> clicks that they will keep buying ads."

The consequence he draws:

> "Advertisers get hooked on direct marketing and they stop paying attention to what matters, which
> is finding the tribe, earning the attention of the tribe, building conversations within and among
> the tribe, figuring out how to matter to people as opposed to simply strip mining their
> attention."

**Why this matters when someone asks about scaling spend.** The pattern of "it worked, so we put
more behind it, and then it stopped working" is not a failure of execution under this argument. It
is the expected behaviour of the system. Offering better creative as the fix misreads the problem.

He is explicit about the trap of chasing clicks:

> "If you're going to be in the business of spending money to get clicks so you can make enough
> money to get more clicks, you're going to make bad choices."

### 4. So what actually works

Not better ads. A **smallest viable audience** with a **network effect** and a reason to talk about
it. Those are in `frameworks.md`, and the cases that make them concrete are in
`worked-examples.md`.

His summary of the strategist's job, which he places *before* any creative work:

> "The strategist is the one who takes 10 steps back and says, 'If we disappeared, who would miss
> us? What is the change we seek to make?'"

> "So the hard work of being the strategist happens long before you do the hard work of being an
> ad creative."

> "It doesn't matter how fast you're going if you're going in the wrong direction."

### How the course closes

> "Anyone with a good enough idea can reach a billion people on earth for free in a day. But at the
> same time, you can't buy your way out of this problem the way we could in the old days. You can't
> buy huge amounts of wholesale attention and then hope a miracle happens."

> "It's incremental. It grows over time. It's something that we earn. It's not something that we
> do."

### Reading this honestly

Godin is arguing about **where attention and money should go**, not refusing to make advertising.
He ran ads, he built ad businesses, and he says plainly that direct response works right up until
the point it stops. The useful version of his position is not "never buy ads". It is: know which
of the two games you are playing, know that the measurable one has a ceiling built into it, and do
not let the measurable one crowd out the work that compounds.

### What a brand is

> "A brand is not a logo. A logo is simply the label we put on something. **A brand is a promise.**"

His test: if Hyatt Hotels announced a line of sneakers, nobody could guess what they would be like.
If Nike announced a hotel, most people could. Nike has a brand; Hyatt has a logo and an
expectation of "a dark, quiet room where you can stay with a modest amount of status".

The Google and Bing study makes the same point from the other side. When researchers swapped the
results and kept the logos, people said Bing's results were better when a Google logo sat on top.
That gap is the brand.

> "A brand is when we're not sure what do we expect. A brand is an opportunity for loyalty."

#### The two-part loyalty test

This is the most immediately usable thing in the course, because it turns "do we have a brand?"
from a debate into two answerable questions:

1. **If your thing costs more than your competitors', does anyone buy yours?** In a blind test
   where the products are the same, people should buy the cheap one. If they do not, you have a
   brand.
2. **If you announce something, are people interested? Do they give you the benefit of the doubt?**

If both answers are no, the honest conclusion is that you have a logo, and the work in front of you
is different from the work you thought.

### Permission versus interruption

**Permission marketing** is *anticipated, personal and relevant* messages that people actually want
to receive. Those three words are the definition and are worth keeping intact.

Godin's own proof: he is approaching his 10,000th consecutive daily blog post, has never promoted
the blog, and pushes nobody to read it. About a million people receive it and many read it because
they want to.

> "My blog isn't for me, it's for them. Anticipated, personal, and relevant. This takes patience
> and discipline."

**Interruption marketing** is the opposite:

> "I got money. I'm in a hurry. I don't care who I spam... This is the marketer who says they're in
> too much of a hurry to do it the right way."

He also notes the economics, which are easy to miss: "the money you spend to earn permission always
pays for itself."

### The smallest viable audience

The origin story is Godin nearly losing his job on day one at Spinnaker. The agency was arguing
about whether the kid in the ad should be a boy or a girl, and he suggested hiring an actor whose
gender was ambiguous. They looked at him like he was an idiot, and he says **they were right**:

> "Not because gender assigned at birth is critical to any of this, but because the opportunity is
> to find not the biggest possible audience, but the smallest viable audience. **How few people
> would you need to delight or thrill?**"

> "How few people would need to benefit from your network effect for your project to work?"

> "Because that puts you on the hook."

#### ⭐ The Newton nuance, and it is the part most often lost

**Small is not the variable. Connected is.** Godin proves it with Apple's Newton, which sold
100,000 units at $1,000 each in its first month, a $100M launch, and is still regarded as one of
Apple's great failures:

> "And then it wasn't, because they had a group of people, but they weren't a group. **They were
> just a bunch.** They weren't connected. They weren't in it for the same reason. They weren't
> responding to the same story."

So the question is never "can we target a niche". It is: are these people connected to each other,
in it for the same reason, responding to the same story? A small unconnected audience is a bunch,
and a bunch does not compound.

> "So what we're seeking is the smallest viable audience. We want to give them something that they
> want to talk about. Their group of people wants to hear. And then we want to create a **ratchet**
> where more connection and more action leads to more status and more affiliation."

### The network effect

Metcalfe's law, coined by Bob Metcalfe: **the power of the community goes up by the square of the
number of people in it.** A telephone works better when your friends have telephones, and does not
work at all if none of them do.

The strategic consequence is the sharpest professional point in the course:

> "If you are waiting for your client to bring you a finished product and an audience and then
> start working on the ads, **you're way too late**."

> "Our opportunity is to be in the room with the engineers, to be in the room with the people who
> are inventing what's getting made in the first place, and change the product to build in the
> network effect."

On apps specifically: in a store with more than a million apps, an app without a network effect is
doomed. What you want is for people to evangelise it **because it is good for them, not because it
is good for you**.

### Psychographics over demographics

> "Do we pick people who are tall or who are white or who are from this country? Do we pick people
> who make this much money? These are demographic choices... But we don't care about demographics.
> **We care about psychographics.** What do people believe before we even get there? What do people
> want before we get there?"

His illustration: readers of Good Housekeeping or Martha Stewart have houses, but so do you, and
you do not read them. The difference is not the house.

> "Where are we finding our pain and our fear and our dreams? Because that worldview decides what
> we're going to believe, what we're looking for, what we're going to pay money for, what problems
> we have, where is our stress, our attention, the pain we seek to address."

> "And decoding all of that is the work of the creative strategist."

### What people want

Three things, common to human beings, that the tribe mechanics run on:

1. **Status** — who is up, who is down, who eats lunch first
2. **Affiliation** — who is to our left, who is to our right, who is like me
3. **Freedom from fear**

Tom's Shoes in `worked-examples.md` is the full worked mechanism for the first two.

### Positioning as a generous act

> "Positioning is a generous act."

The mechanics are the familiar xy grid: find an under-inhabited quadrant and fill it, with extremes
on each axis (cheap and expensive, convenient and hard to use). But Godin adds the rule that makes
it honest:

> "What you're seeking to do, if you want to do this work honestly, is find axes on your
> positioning chart where **either axis is a good thing**."

#### The test that tells you whether you actually have a position

If someone walks into a Volvo dealership saying they want to win a car race, the Volvo dealer
should send them to Ferrari. If someone walks into Ferrari with seven kids to get to preschool,
Ferrari should send them to Volvo.

> "What it means to have a position is that you can eagerly and honestly send people to what others
> think of as your competition."

> "There isn't a best position. There's simply a **useful** position."

> "Positioning is the generous act of being able to say, 'It's not for you, but consider going over
> there.'"

The failure case: "Our life insurance is just like their life insurance" is a slogan, not a
position.

> "The generous act we have in the fast-moving world of social media is to stand for something and
> not decide to stand for everything."

### Tension, which is not stress

> "**Stress** is a bad thing. Stress is wanting to go here and there at the same time. Stay and
> run. Avoid it."

> "**Tension** is the hard work we do of pulling the rubber band back before we let it go. Tension
> is FOMO."

His examples: an artist announcing 9,000 of 10,000 seats are sold moves the last people off the
couch. A new MacBook announcement creates tension in everyone holding the old one.

> "When we show up in a system, when we show up with a product, when we show up with any sort of
> invitation, just the fact that we showed up creates tension."

### Authenticity is a trap

> "**Authenticity is a crock. It's a trap.** Authenticity exists to distract you. Why? Because
> authenticity is for friends and family. Authenticity is for the people who love you. For
> everybody else, **professionals don't bring authenticity. They bring consistency**."

The reasoning runs straight back to "a brand is a promise": are you going to keep it or not? If you
hear a musician who genuinely has a headache and a sore throat, do you want them to limp through
being cranky, or to be the best version of themselves? If you hire a lawyer, do you want them
authentically brittle and short-tempered, or consistently the lawyer they promised to be?

> "Consistency isn't easy, but consistency is easy to understand. Take a promise and keep it."

He extends this into a warning about agencies and platforms. Agencies rewarded for awards or clicks
break the consistency promise and show up with one-offs and gimmicks. And:

> "The social media platforms build algorithms that reward you for going off script, that reward
> you for picking a fight, that reward you for doing something that attracts a crowd. You know why?
> Because that makes the social media companies more money, not because it makes you succeed."

### Good decisions versus good outcomes

Credited to Annie Duke, and it is the antidote to creative paralysis.

Ask anyone whether they made a good decision in the last six months and they say yes. Ask whether
it turned out well and they say yes again. **The two are unrelated.**

> "If you buy a lottery ticket and you win, you made a bad decision and you got lucky. On the other
> hand, if you were driving very carefully and someone T-boned your car, you made a good decision
> and you got unlucky."

> "We get paralyzed about making creative decisions because we insist that the outcome is the only
> way to tell if it was a good decision. This is not correct."

#### What a good creative decision actually is

Two criteria, and they are checkable before the results come in:

1. **Do not run out of time and do not run out of money.** "If you run out of time and you run out
   of money, you're done."
2. **Are we strategically coherent?** Have we thought hard about the change we seek to make, who it
   is for, what it is for, the smallest viable audience, the network effect?

> "If we do those things and it doesn't work, well, we've run out of time and we've run out of
> money. We've learned one thing that doesn't work. We might've gotten unlucky along the way, but
> we didn't make a bad decision."

### Four rules from the strategy cards

Godin runs a lightning round from a deck of strategy cards he made.

**1. Don't play games you can't win.**
> "You can't out Amazon Amazon. Can't out Walmart Walmart. Can't out Apple Apple. These slots,
> these positions are taken. But there are lots of things you can do that Amazon can't."

**2. Avoid false proxies.** Easy to measure and distracting. His four examples:
- Height has nothing to do with being a good president, yet taller candidates keep winning
- Typing speed has no correlation with being a good programmer
- One organisation's last five hires were all captains of their Ivy League tennis team
- **Clicks**: "If you run an ad and you're keeping track of clicks, you're making a mistake.
  Because we all know how to make clicks go up. And the things you would do to make clicks go up
  are not the things you're going to be able to do to make your brand do better."

**3. Genre is not generic.** Genre means "what do I expect, what does this remind me of". Tweets
look like tweets and Instagram posts look like Instagram posts, but ask who decided that and
whether it is helping. Does matching the category build trust, or does it get in the way of the
tension people need in order to move?

**4. What will I tell my friends? What will I tell my boss?** Especially in B2B. If you do not
give someone a story to tell about why they bought, the only stories left are "I did what we did
last time" and "I got the same thing but cheaper".

> "That's how you're going to get a no, or worse, a maybe."

### Tom's Shoes

**The most complete mechanism in the course.** Godin's friend Blake wanted to make a shoe brand
where buying a pair gave an identical pair to someone without shoes.

The strategic choices, in order:

1. **$80 espadrilles, made ethically in Portugal, with a big logo on the back.** The logo was a
   deliberate break: women's shoes did not carry logos at the time.
2. **$80 when you can buy espadrilles for $30.** "So what are you paying extra for? **You're paying
   for the story.**"
3. **The smallest viable audience.** "Who's going to buy a pair of shoes from a new company with a
   logo on them? Almost no one. **And almost no one is exactly the right thing to do.**" He found
   women who wanted to be first in their neighbourhood, who wanted to feel like philanthropists,
   who wanted to engage on the internet. A tiny group.

Then the ratchet turns:

4. They wear the shoes, because that is what people do with shoes. The shoes are now in the world.
5. There is an unspoken rule in many communities: if a friend has new shoes, you say "nice shoes,
   where'd you get them?"
6. In that moment the early adopter gets **status**. She says these are brand new shoes from a new
   company, and because she bought them a family somewhere gets shoes too.
7. Godin names the subtext plainly: *"I'm better than you. I'm better than you because I knew about
   these and you didn't. And I'm better than you because I'm a philanthropist and you're not."*
8. **That status increase creates tension**, because the friend is now behind.
9. She buys a pair. Now she has the same conversation with someone else. "Repeat, repeat, repeat,
   repeat, until Tom's is so big he sells the company for half a billion dollars."

> "This stepwise progression of serving a tribe of people who seek status and affiliation had
> absolutely nothing to do with whether or not they bought good media ads for Tom's shoes."

#### ⭐ And then the failure, which is the more useful half

Tom's needed to keep growing, so they launched **coffee**, with a philanthropic output attached.
It did not work.

> "It didn't work because no one who's drinking a cup of coffee says to you, 'Oh, interesting
> coffee. Where'd you get it?' Because it's just coffee. So the conversation went away. The tribal
> relationship went away. The status went away, all of it. And instead it was just, 'You trust
> Tom's. Here's a social media ad. Please buy some coffee.'"

**The boundary condition.** The mechanism needs a visible artifact that triggers a conversation.
Shoes are worn in public and prompt a question; coffee is not and does not. Brand trust alone does
not restart the ratchet, and a social media ad is what you fall back to when the mechanism is
absent. When applying this, the question to ask is: *what makes someone ask about this
unprompted?* If there is no answer, the Tom's mechanism will not run, no matter how good the cause.

### Purple Cow in a milk carton

Godin's own network effect, built with paper.

Twenty years ago he had been "kicked out of the book publishing industry" after a big book failed,
so he had no publisher. He wanted to dedicate a book to his late friend Lionel Poilâne, a French
baker, so he self-published **Purple Cow** and printed only 10,000 copies, packaged in a milk
carton.

The distribution:

1. He went to people he **had permission to talk to**: readers of his popular Fast Company column.
2. The offer: the book is free, send $5 for postage and handling.
3. "Selling 5,000 of them to people who were already connected with each other, who already gave me
   the benefit of the doubt, who already trusted me for $5, that wasn't very hard."
4. Then he offered **12-packs**, and sold 412 of them.

The 12-pack is the mechanism:

> "But if you get a 12-pack, what are you going to do with it? You have to give them away. Why
> would you give them away? You would give them away because your life would get better if your
> friends saw you as this generous leader."

And the milk carton is the artifact:

> "Putting the milk carton on your desk sent a message to your boss. And that message was, let's
> talk about this. It wasn't because they wanted to help Seth Godin. They didn't know Seth Godin.
> It was because if their boss got excited about this idea of making remarkable products and
> services, **their lives would get better. They would enjoy work more. And they might get
> promoted**."

The book went on to sell more than a million copies.

> "It begins, always begins, not with the biggest possible audience, but with the smallest viable
> audience, the one you can live with."

**What to take from it:** the spreader's incentive has to be selfish and real. People passed the
book on because it made *them* look generous and made *their* work better, not to help the author.

### The Newton versus the iPhone

The clarifier for what "smallest viable audience" actually means.

Before the iPhone, Apple made the **Newton**, a handwriting-recognising device Godin compares to a
Kindle. It is generally regarded as the biggest failure in Apple's history until the recent
headset.

And yet: **it sold 100,000 units in its first month at $1,000 each. That is $100 million.**

> "It was a huge success. And then it wasn't, because they had a group of people, but **they
> weren't a group. They were just a bunch.** They weren't connected. They weren't in it for the
> same reason. They weren't responding to the same story."

He notes the iPhone also sold about 100,000 units in its first month. Same number, opposite
outcome.

**Use this whenever someone equates a niche with a tribe.** The number is not the variable.

### Supreme

There is usually a queue outside the Supreme store in New York to buy an $80 t-shirt.

Godin's two observations: everyone in the queue is already wearing clothes, so they do not need a
shirt. And on the way there they passed twenty shops selling t-shirts for $10.

> "The position of Supreme isn't, 'You need a t-shirt, we have a t-shirt.' Their position is, 'For
> people who view clothing as a sign of status and a certain kind of affiliation, that's what we
> have.' But please, don't come in here and tell us your stuff's too expensive. **It's not for
> you.**"

### Volvo and Ferrari

The test for whether a position is real. If someone walks into Volvo wanting to win a car race, the
Volvo dealer should send them down the street to Ferrari. If someone walks into Ferrari needing to
get seven children to preschool, Ferrari should send them to Volvo.

> "What it means to have a position is that you can eagerly and honestly send people to what others
> think of as your competition."

If you cannot name the customer you would happily turn away, you do not have a position yet.

### Nike and Hyatt

If Hyatt Hotels launched a line of sneakers, nobody could guess what they would be like. If Nike
opened a hotel, most people could picture it.

> "That's because Nike has a brand, but Hyatt just has a logo. All Hyatt stands for is a dark,
> quiet room where you can stay with a modest amount of status. Nike stands for more than that."

A fast diagnostic: describe a product your brand does not make. If nobody can guess what it would
be like, you have a logo.

### Google and Bing

When Google was first competing with Bing, most people said Google's results were better.
Researchers then swapped the results while keeping the logos in place.

> "And when people saw Bing results with a Google logo on top, they said they were better because
> Google had a brand."

This is the benefit of the doubt, measured. It is also why the second half of the loyalty test
("if you announce something, are people interested?") is a real question and not a soft one.

### Columbia Record Club

Where measurable advertising came from, and worth knowing because it explains why the industry
thinks the way it does.

Columbia Record Club ran magazine ads: pick any 12 records, mail us a penny, we send the records.
They wanted to run TV ads but could not tell whether TV was working, and 800 numbers did not exist
yet.

Lester Wonderman's solution: add a small unexplained box to the bottom of the magazine coupon. Then
run TV spots saying *look in your Sunday paper, grab that coupon, see that little box? Fill it in
and we'll send you a 13th record free.*

Now TV's contribution was countable, so they bought more TV to support the magazine ad.

> "I think you see where this is going."

The whole measurable-advertising reflex descends from this, and so does the ceiling Godin describes
in `the-argument.md`.

### Rivian

Short, and the most direct answer in the course to "can we just advertise our way there?"

> "So if I'm going to name a great brand, a brand that people are excited about, something like
> Rivian, do people want a Rivian pickup truck because they saw the ads for a Rivian pickup truck?
> **Does an ad on Facebook ever sell a $96,000 pickup truck?** I don't think so."

> "But someone decided to make a pickup truck, not a sports car. **That decision was strategic.**
> Who's it for? What's it for? What is the change we seek to make?"

**Why this one earns its place.** Every other case here shows a mechanism working or failing.
Rivian shows *where the decision actually got made*: an electric-vehicle company choosing a pickup
truck rather than another sports car, aimed at people no one else was serving. No amount of media
buying produces that choice, and no amount of media buying rescues its absence.

Godin puts it directly before this case:

> "It doesn't matter how fast you're going if you're going in the wrong direction. So **the hard
> work of being the strategist happens long before you do the hard work of being an ad creative**."

Reach for Rivian when someone is treating advertising as the lever on a decision that was really
made upstream, in the product, the price or the positioning.

## The questions, and how the course closes

Godin returns to a small set of questions repeatedly. They are the practical output of the course:
the things a strategist is supposed to have answered before any creative work starts.

### The four questions

> "If you're not here to make a change, we probably don't need to spend our time and money with
> you. But if you are here to make a change, let's be clear about what that change is."

1. **Who is it for?**
   > "Who exactly are we seeking to serve? When we show up with what we've got, who wants it?"
2. **What is it for?**
   > "If I'm going to take attention from somebody, if I'm going to show up in front of them, why?
   > Is it something as simple and selfish as because I'll make a profit, or am I serving them?"
3. **What's worth doing?**
4. **Who would miss us if we were gone?**

The fourth is the one people skip and the one that does the most work. It is also how he defines
the strategist's job:

> "The strategist is the one who takes 10 steps back and says, 'If we disappeared, who would miss
> us? What is the change we seek to make?'"

### Strategy as a philosophy of becoming

> "**Strategy is a philosophy of becoming.** What we seek to do is help people get to where they
> want to go, because it is very difficult to persuade someone to go somewhere they don't want to
> go."

The consequence, and it is a good test for an offer:

> "Most successes in the last five years are brands offering services or products, software,
> nonprofits, that when people encounter them, they don't need to be persuaded or hustled. They
> simply need to know of it, to learn of it, to be able to say, **this is what I want**."

If the plan depends on persuading people to want something they do not want, the strategy is doing
the wrong work.

### Tribes, and the seven words

> "**People like us do things like this.** Just seven words, the essence of what it is to be
> human."

His framing: culture drives humanity, and there is no monoculture however much anyone would like
there to be. A thousand years ago a tribe on the steppes of East Asia had a different culture from
one near the Hudson River or one around Stonehenge. There are tribes everywhere.

And the limit that matters:

> "You are not going to be able to capture a tribe, own a tribe, make them your tribe. But what you
> can do, what is the opportunity of our lives, is **find a tribe that needs a leader**, to find a
> tribe that wants to add something to its culture, to find a tribe that needs service, to honor
> them, to connect them, to lead them."

### What do we make?

> "What do we make? We don't make ads. We don't even make shoes. **We make a difference. We make
> choices. We make decisions.**"

He follows this with the good-decisions-versus-good-outcomes argument (in `frameworks.md`), because
the reason people avoid making decisions is that they expect to be judged on outcomes they do not
control.

### Deciding what to ignore

A quieter point, and an unusually concrete one:

> "It requires you to raise your hand and be on the hook to make your assertions out loud, to
> determine **what you're going to measure and what you're going to intentionally ignore**."

His own examples: he told his publishers he would not look at the New York Times bestseller list,
"because it's gamed and because it's measuring the wrong things", and that he does not read his
one-star Amazon reviews because he learns nothing from them.

> "When we make choices about where we're going to show up in social media, we're also making
> choices about where we're not going to show up. **Can you announce what those choices are and
> why?**"

### What the job actually is

> "When we show up to do our work as an ad professional, as an ad creative, as a creative
> strategist, we don't get to skip all these things and just say to the client, 'What kind of ad do
> you want?'"

> "We don't get to say, 'Isn't the ad funny or well-filmed?' No one cares. **We're not trying to
> win awards. We're trying to win trust. We're trying to win connection. We're trying to win the
> benefit of the doubt.** That doesn't happen because we're being authentic. It happens because
> we're being consistent, because we are creating tension, because we're building a bridge from
> here to there, because we're creating culture."

### The posture

> "Here, I made this. I made this. **I'm on the hook to make a change happen.**"

### Go make a ruckus

The closing instruction, and he defines it precisely so it cannot be misread as permission to be
loud:

> "Go make a ruckus. What I mean by that is simple. **Not** go spam people, go hustle people, go
> interrupt people. We're better than that. What it means to make a ruckus is to **find a place
> where you would be missed if you didn't show up**, to create possibility, to engage with people
> in a way they want to be engaged with, and to make things better."

> "Because the truth is, change is going to happen whether we want it or not. But better, better is
> possible."

### Using these in practice

These questions work as an intake at the start of a project, and they work as a diagnostic when
something is not landing. When an ad account is decaying, "who would miss us if we were gone?" is
often a more productive question than any creative brief, because it surfaces whether there is a
tribe to serve at all or only an audience being rented from a platform.

Do not present all four as a form to fill in. Ask the one that bites on the situation in front of
you, and let the answer pull the others along.

### One thing to be careful about

This course argues **against** heavy paid social spending, inside a paid social certification. That
is not an error to be smoothed over, and Godin states it in his opening: *"Turns out most social
media ads are a waste of time. As someone who has spent and lost millions of dollars buying online
ads."* Represent the position as he makes it.

Equally, do not overstate it into "never advertise". He built ad businesses, he credits direct
response with real results, and his actual claim is about the ceiling built into measurable
interruption, not about advertising being worthless.
