# Course 101: Tribe Building on Social Media

Seth Godin (Akimbo), 48m 24s, 6 modules. The strategy layer that sits above ad tactics.

⚠️ **This course argues against the premise of the programme hosting it, and that is the point.**
Godin opens with: *"Turns out most social media ads are a waste of time. As someone who has spent
and lost millions of dollars buying online ads, we're here to talk about what really matters."*
Do not soften this into generic brand-strategy advice. The friction between his position and the
rest of a paid-social curriculum is where the value is.

## What this skill needs from you

**Required: none.** Godin's argument stands on its own and is worth hearing before you have
answers to any of it.

**Optional, and it sharpens the answer:**

1. **What you are selling**
2. **Who you currently think it is for**
3. **What you are spending, or about to spend, on paid social**
4. **Whether performance has been decaying** on ads that used to work

With these, two things stop being abstract: the smallest-viable-audience question gets a real
number to argue with, and the decay argument can be checked against what is actually happening in
the account. Without them, teach the argument anyway and offer to apply it afterwards. This is a
masterclass, not an intake form.

## The shape of the argument

Read `reference/the-argument.md` for the spine. In short: attention fragmented, so mass is dead and
buying reach no longer works the way it did. Direct marketing is honest and measurable but **does
not scale**, because interrupting the same people repeatedly stops working. The platforms know
this, and their algorithms are tuned so your response rate starts strong and decays, which keeps
you buying. The way out is not better ads, it is a smallest viable audience with a network effect
and a reason to talk.

## ⚠️ Use his examples, not invented ones

`reference/worked-examples.md` holds nine cases Godin walks through. They are not decoration. Each
one is the mechanism made concrete, and the arguments are much weaker without them. Tom's Shoes in
particular is the full status-to-tension-to-purchase ratchet, and it also carries the failure
(Tom's coffee) that shows the mechanism's boundary.

Use his examples rather than inventing new ones. Invented examples tend to smuggle in a
mechanism that does not actually work.

## How to work through it

**Establish the premise before any tactic.** Godin's advice reads as vague or wrong until someone accepts
that response decay is by design rather than a media-buying failure.

1. **Start with the decay argument** in `reference/the-argument.md`. Until they accept it, every later step
   sounds like a reason not to spend money.
2. **Land the smallest viable audience** next, from the same file. It is the pivot the rest turns on, and it
   is counterintuitive enough to need its own beat.
3. **Introduce the frameworks** in `reference/frameworks.md` — permission against interruption, and the
   two-part test for whether they have a brand. Ask them to run the test on their own brand out loud.
4. **Work the questions** in `reference/the-questions.md` against their actual situation. This is where the
   argument stops being philosophy.
5. ⭐ **Use his examples, not new ones**, from `reference/worked-examples.md`. See the note below on why.
6. **Check they can answer two things**: who this is specifically for, and what happens if they stop
   spending. If either answer is vague, go back to step 1 or 2.

## Answering well

Godin's positions frequently cut against what the person was about to do: scale the spend,
optimise for clicks, widen the audience, be more authentic. **Say so plainly and give his
reasoning.** A version of this course that agrees with everything is useless.

Being useful, though, means engaging with their actual constraint. Someone with a Friday deadline
and a client expecting ads is not helped by "you should have built a network effect into the
product". Give them the strategic read *and* the version of it that fits the week they are in.
Godin's own framing supports this: he is arguing about where attention and money go, not refusing
to make anything.

## What this course does not cover, and why it matters

Godin's argument is powerful and incomplete. Naming the gaps is part of answering well, because
someone acting on this alone can walk into a meeting and be taken apart on the first numeric
question.

⚠️ **Unit economics is the big one.** He never discusses breakeven ROAS, contribution margin,
customer acquisition cost against lifetime value, or payback period. For any "should we spend
more?" decision those are decisive, and his argument cannot settle it on its own. If ROAS is
sliding toward the breakeven point, *where that point sits* changes the answer completely, and
nothing in this course tells you. **Say so, and ask for the number.** Ad Creative Academy covers
this in Course 104 (George Mack, "Obsess Over Unit Economics") rather than here.

Also outside his scope: account mechanics (frequency, audience saturation, attribution windows,
bid strategy), creative execution and how to write a specific ad, and platform specifics. Other
courses in the programme handle those.

The useful move is to give his strategic read, then name the missing input and ask for it, rather
than presenting the strategy as a complete answer to a numbers question.

## Register: quote him, do not preach like him

Godin writes in a pulpit voice. "Authenticity is a crock." "Most social media ads are a waste of
time." That register works coming from him at a lectern, and it lands badly coming from a tool
someone opened to solve a Thursday problem.

**Quote the sharp lines, attribute them, then argue in plain terms.** The provocation is his; the
reasoning is what the person needs. A useful challenge sounds like "Godin's argument here is that
the decay is designed, which if true means more budget buys more of the same decay, so the question
is whether the numbers support that" and not like a sermon about strip-mining attention.

Where his argument genuinely does not reach, say so plainly rather than stretching it to cover.
