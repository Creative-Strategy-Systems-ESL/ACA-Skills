# The worked examples

Eight cases Godin walks through. They are not illustrations bolted onto the frameworks, they are
how the mechanisms become usable. Reason from these rather than inventing new ones, because
invented examples tend to smuggle in a mechanism that does not actually work.

## Tom's Shoes

**The most complete mechanism in the course.** Godin's friend Blake wanted to make a shoe brand
where buying a pair gave an identical pair to someone without shoes.

The strategic choices, in order:

1. **$80 espadrilles, made ethically in Portugal, with a big logo on the back.** The logo was a
   deliberate break: women's shoes did not carry logos at the time.
2. **$80 when you can buy espadrilles for $30.** "So what are you paying extra for? **You're paying
   for the story.**"
3. **The smallest viable audience.** "Who's going to buy a pair of shoes from a new company with a
   logo on them? Almost no one. **And almost no one is exactly the right thing to do.**" He found
   women who wanted to be first in their neighbourhood, who wanted to feel like philanthropists,
   who wanted to engage on the internet. A tiny group.

Then the ratchet turns:

4. They wear the shoes, because that is what people do with shoes. The shoes are now in the world.
5. There is an unspoken rule in many communities: if a friend has new shoes, you say "nice shoes,
   where'd you get them?"
6. In that moment the early adopter gets **status**. She says these are brand new shoes from a new
   company, and because she bought them a family somewhere gets shoes too.
7. Godin names the subtext plainly: *"I'm better than you. I'm better than you because I knew about
   these and you didn't. And I'm better than you because I'm a philanthropist and you're not."*
8. **That status increase creates tension**, because the friend is now behind.
9. She buys a pair. Now she has the same conversation with someone else. "Repeat, repeat, repeat,
   repeat, until Tom's is so big he sells the company for half a billion dollars."

> "This stepwise progression of serving a tribe of people who seek status and affiliation had
> absolutely nothing to do with whether or not they bought good media ads for Tom's shoes."

### ⭐ And then the failure, which is the more useful half

Tom's needed to keep growing, so they launched **coffee**, with a philanthropic output attached.
It did not work.

> "It didn't work because no one who's drinking a cup of coffee says to you, 'Oh, interesting
> coffee. Where'd you get it?' Because it's just coffee. So the conversation went away. The tribal
> relationship went away. The status went away, all of it. And instead it was just, 'You trust
> Tom's. Here's a social media ad. Please buy some coffee.'"

**The boundary condition.** The mechanism needs a visible artifact that triggers a conversation.
Shoes are worn in public and prompt a question; coffee is not and does not. Brand trust alone does
not restart the ratchet, and a social media ad is what you fall back to when the mechanism is
absent. When applying this, the question to ask is: *what makes someone ask about this
unprompted?* If there is no answer, the Tom's mechanism will not run, no matter how good the cause.

## Purple Cow in a milk carton

Godin's own network effect, built with paper.

Twenty years ago he had been "kicked out of the book publishing industry" after a big book failed,
so he had no publisher. He wanted to dedicate a book to his late friend Lionel Poilâne, a French
baker, so he self-published **Purple Cow** and printed only 10,000 copies, packaged in a milk
carton.

The distribution:

1. He went to people he **had permission to talk to**: readers of his popular Fast Company column.
2. The offer: the book is free, send $5 for postage and handling.
3. "Selling 5,000 of them to people who were already connected with each other, who already gave me
   the benefit of the doubt, who already trusted me for $5, that wasn't very hard."
4. Then he offered **12-packs**, and sold 412 of them.

The 12-pack is the mechanism:

> "But if you get a 12-pack, what are you going to do with it? You have to give them away. Why
> would you give them away? You would give them away because your life would get better if your
> friends saw you as this generous leader."

And the milk carton is the artifact:

> "Putting the milk carton on your desk sent a message to your boss. And that message was, let's
> talk about this. It wasn't because they wanted to help Seth Godin. They didn't know Seth Godin.
> It was because if their boss got excited about this idea of making remarkable products and
> services, **their lives would get better. They would enjoy work more. And they might get
> promoted**."

The book went on to sell more than a million copies.

> "It begins, always begins, not with the biggest possible audience, but with the smallest viable
> audience, the one you can live with."

**What to take from it:** the spreader's incentive has to be selfish and real. People passed the
book on because it made *them* look generous and made *their* work better, not to help the author.

## The Newton versus the iPhone

The clarifier for what "smallest viable audience" actually means.

Before the iPhone, Apple made the **Newton**, a handwriting-recognising device Godin compares to a
Kindle. It is generally regarded as the biggest failure in Apple's history until the recent
headset.

And yet: **it sold 100,000 units in its first month at $1,000 each. That is $100 million.**

> "It was a huge success. And then it wasn't, because they had a group of people, but **they
> weren't a group. They were just a bunch.** They weren't connected. They weren't in it for the
> same reason. They weren't responding to the same story."

He notes the iPhone also sold about 100,000 units in its first month. Same number, opposite
outcome.

**Use this whenever someone equates a niche with a tribe.** The number is not the variable.

## Supreme

There is usually a queue outside the Supreme store in New York to buy an $80 t-shirt.

Godin's two observations: everyone in the queue is already wearing clothes, so they do not need a
shirt. And on the way there they passed twenty shops selling t-shirts for $10.

> "The position of Supreme isn't, 'You need a t-shirt, we have a t-shirt.' Their position is, 'For
> people who view clothing as a sign of status and a certain kind of affiliation, that's what we
> have.' But please, don't come in here and tell us your stuff's too expensive. **It's not for
> you.**"

## Volvo and Ferrari

The test for whether a position is real. If someone walks into Volvo wanting to win a car race, the
Volvo dealer should send them down the street to Ferrari. If someone walks into Ferrari needing to
get seven children to preschool, Ferrari should send them to Volvo.

> "What it means to have a position is that you can eagerly and honestly send people to what others
> think of as your competition."

If you cannot name the customer you would happily turn away, you do not have a position yet.

## Nike and Hyatt

If Hyatt Hotels launched a line of sneakers, nobody could guess what they would be like. If Nike
opened a hotel, most people could picture it.

> "That's because Nike has a brand, but Hyatt just has a logo. All Hyatt stands for is a dark,
> quiet room where you can stay with a modest amount of status. Nike stands for more than that."

A fast diagnostic: describe a product your brand does not make. If nobody can guess what it would
be like, you have a logo.

## Google and Bing

When Google was first competing with Bing, most people said Google's results were better.
Researchers then swapped the results while keeping the logos in place.

> "And when people saw Bing results with a Google logo on top, they said they were better because
> Google had a brand."

This is the benefit of the doubt, measured. It is also why the second half of the loyalty test
("if you announce something, are people interested?") is a real question and not a soft one.

## Columbia Record Club

Where measurable advertising came from, and worth knowing because it explains why the industry
thinks the way it does.

Columbia Record Club ran magazine ads: pick any 12 records, mail us a penny, we send the records.
They wanted to run TV ads but could not tell whether TV was working, and 800 numbers did not exist
yet.

Lester Wonderman's solution: add a small unexplained box to the bottom of the magazine coupon. Then
run TV spots saying *look in your Sunday paper, grab that coupon, see that little box? Fill it in
and we'll send you a 13th record free.*

Now TV's contribution was countable, so they bought more TV to support the magazine ad.

> "I think you see where this is going."

The whole measurable-advertising reflex descends from this, and so does the ceiling Godin describes
in `the-argument.md`.

## Rivian

Short, and the most direct answer in the course to "can we just advertise our way there?"

> "So if I'm going to name a great brand, a brand that people are excited about, something like
> Rivian, do people want a Rivian pickup truck because they saw the ads for a Rivian pickup truck?
> **Does an ad on Facebook ever sell a $96,000 pickup truck?** I don't think so."

> "But someone decided to make a pickup truck, not a sports car. **That decision was strategic.**
> Who's it for? What's it for? What is the change we seek to make?"

**Why this one earns its place.** Every other case here shows a mechanism working or failing.
Rivian shows *where the decision actually got made*: an electric-vehicle company choosing a pickup
truck rather than another sports car, aimed at people no one else was serving. No amount of media
buying produces that choice, and no amount of media buying rescues its absence.

Godin puts it directly before this case:

> "It doesn't matter how fast you're going if you're going in the wrong direction. So **the hard
> work of being the strategist happens long before you do the hard work of being an ad creative**."

Reach for Rivian when someone is treating advertising as the lever on a decision that was really
made upstream, in the product, the price or the positioning.
