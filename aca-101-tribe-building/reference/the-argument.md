# The argument

Seth Godin's case, in the order he builds it. Quoted where the phrasing carries the idea, because
several of these lines are the compressed version of a whole chapter.

## Where he starts: everyone has the whole agency now

Godin opens on his first job in 1983 at Spinnaker Software, walking into their ad agency Cabot and
finding a creative director, an art director, a copywriter, a typesetter and an account executive.
The agency's job was to buy magazine ads, because that was the only lever available.

> "Today, each of you is in a meeting like that, except it's a bit different. Because if you've got
> a laptop, you have all of the tools that each one of those individuals at Cabot had to play with.
> It's possible you have all of those jobs, in addition to being the lead on strategy. It's
> possible that you're also the client."

The warning he draws from it:

> "It's very easy, if you have a hammer, to believe that everything's a nail. And it's pretty
> tempting, given the 100-year track record of advertising, to come to the conclusion that the best
> way to get attention and turn it into sales is to run ads and pay money for them."

## 1. Attention fragmented, so mass is dead

Everyone gets the same amount of attention per day, and it starts over daily. That attention keeps
getting cut into smaller pieces. When there were three or four TV networks and a handful of
magazines, buying a slot in one of those pillars *was* the strategy. The famous brands of the 60s
and 70s got famous by buying enough ads to make enough money to buy more ads.

His measure of how far that has collapsed:

> "a show like Succession was the buzziest show there was last year. The finale got covered all over
> the internet and three million people in my country watched it. When it went on the air, three
> million. The last episode of MASH in the 1970s was seen by 70 million people. That even a Super
> Bowl ad doesn't reach the kind of audience that advertisers used to take for granted."

And the turn, which is the whole reframe:

> "And that's a good thing. Because instead of paying extra to reach lots of people, we can pay a
> little to reach some people, specific people, people who want to hear from us."

**The trap that follows from mass thinking** is averageness. Someone says they don't like it, so
you sand off the edges. Someone says it's not for them, so you sand off more.

> "We come up with the slogan, 'You can pick anyone and we're anyone.' The problem with this is
> it's a race to the bottom. Someone's going to make their stuff more average than yours. Someone's
> going to lower their price or make themselves more convenient than yours."

The brands that matter now (his list: Patagonia, Supreme, the bakery people queue for) got there
without making average stuff for average people, and **without buying a lot of ads**.

> "The explosion of media means that not only is mass dead, not only can't you reach everyone, you
> might not even want to."

## 2. Direct marketing is honest, measurable, and does not scale

Godin's late friend Lester Wonderman, who sat on the board of his company Yoyodyne, invented direct
marketing.

> "Direct marketing is action marketing... pay money, I run an ad, I get a response... Direct
> marketing is measured marketing."

> "If you spend a dollar on a direct response ad and you make $2, you should do it again and again
> and again and again until you make all the dollars that it's possible to make and then you should
> stop."

That is how the American Express card became the American Express card. The Columbia Record Club
story is Wonderman's trick for measuring TV: a little unexplained box added to the newspaper
coupon, then TV ads telling people to fill it in for a free 13th record, so TV's contribution
became countable.

Godin then brought the same thinking online and calls email marketing "direct mail with no stamps".

The lineage runs: measurable direct mail → measurable banner clicks → platforms that let you put an
ad in front of a segment rather than everyone.

> "Direct marketing thinking keeps us honest. Direct marketing thinking keeps us focused."

And then the hinge of the entire course:

> "**And direct marketing thinking doesn't scale.** Because if you run a coupon too many times, if
> you interrupt too many people, if you come back one too many times, it stops working."

## 3. The decay is designed, and this is the sharpest thing in the course

> "Mark Zuckerberg wants you to buy as many social media ads as you can afford."

> "And they have gamed their algorithm so that when you start buying these ads, your response rate
> will be great. And then inevitably, over time, it will get worse and worse and worse. Because
> behind the scenes, people are adjusting things, so other advertisers are getting just enough
> clicks that they will keep buying ads."

The consequence he draws:

> "Advertisers get hooked on direct marketing and they stop paying attention to what matters, which
> is finding the tribe, earning the attention of the tribe, building conversations within and among
> the tribe, figuring out how to matter to people as opposed to simply strip mining their
> attention."

**Why this matters when someone asks about scaling spend.** The pattern of "it worked, so we put
more behind it, and then it stopped working" is not a failure of execution under this argument. It
is the expected behaviour of the system. Offering better creative as the fix misreads the problem.

He is explicit about the trap of chasing clicks:

> "If you're going to be in the business of spending money to get clicks so you can make enough
> money to get more clicks, you're going to make bad choices."

## 4. So what actually works

Not better ads. A **smallest viable audience** with a **network effect** and a reason to talk about
it. Those are in `frameworks.md`, and the cases that make them concrete are in
`worked-examples.md`.

His summary of the strategist's job, which he places *before* any creative work:

> "The strategist is the one who takes 10 steps back and says, 'If we disappeared, who would miss
> us? What is the change we seek to make?'"

> "So the hard work of being the strategist happens long before you do the hard work of being an
> ad creative."

> "It doesn't matter how fast you're going if you're going in the wrong direction."

## How the course closes

> "Anyone with a good enough idea can reach a billion people on earth for free in a day. But at the
> same time, you can't buy your way out of this problem the way we could in the old days. You can't
> buy huge amounts of wholesale attention and then hope a miracle happens."

> "It's incremental. It grows over time. It's something that we earn. It's not something that we
> do."

## Reading this honestly

Godin is arguing about **where attention and money should go**, not refusing to make advertising.
He ran ads, he built ad businesses, and he says plainly that direct response works right up until
the point it stops. The useful version of his position is not "never buy ads". It is: know which
of the two games you are playing, know that the measurable one has a ceiling built into it, and do
not let the measurable one crowd out the work that compounds.
