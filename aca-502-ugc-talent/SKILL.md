# Course 502: How to Cast and Direct UGC Talent Effectively

**Chloe Rhys**, Ex-TubeScience, now at OpenStore. 32m 52s, 6 modules.

> "No matter how good your brief is, no matter how strong your strategy, **if the person on camera
> doesn't feel believable or engaging, the ad won't land.**"

**This is the tactical layer.** Sourcing, casting, briefing, directing and collecting the right
footage. It picks up exactly where the strategic creator course stops.

⭐ **This skill emits the casting review grid and the deliverables checklist** in the structures
Chloe uses, rather than describing what a grid or a checklist would contain.

## What this skill needs from you

**Required: none, to explain any of it.** Ask what UGC means here, where to find creators, or why a
brief should not be a script, and get an answer.

**To cast, brief or run a review, required:**

1. **The product**, and what problem it solves
2. **What the content is for** — testing angles at volume, or a hero asset. That choice decides
   whether UGC is even the right format

**Optional, and each one is a field in the artifacts:**

3. The persona: who should appear, and the tone or vibe
4. Budget and rate, and the deadline
5. The deliverables you need back, and whether you want edited video or raw clips
6. Brand constraints: words to avoid, claims needing disclaimers, competitor mentions
7. Reference content that nailed the vibe
8. Past performance by creator, if you have run UGC before

⛔ **Never invent a rate.** The course says to state one and be specific, and it gives no benchmark
anywhere. The only figure in the whole course's material is a single template's example. A rate you
made up becomes an offer someone sends.

⛔ **Never write casting notes about a real person that you would not want read back to them.**
Chloe's own grid scores demographic fit and her example rationales describe hair colour, accent and
body type. That is ordinary advertising casting, and a scored spreadsheet about named applicants is
also a document that can be read by other people later. Keep every note tied to the role and the
content, and see **Reviewing talent and casting** (below) before writing one.

## The through-line

> "**80% of great UGC is in the brief.**"

> "A vague brief is a wasted budget. If you don't tell creators what's important to include, what to
> avoid, or how to structure the video, they'll guess. And their guess might not be what you needed."

⭐ **So most UGC failures are diagnosed backwards.** When footage comes back unusable, the instinct
is to blame the creator. Chloe's position is that the brief is the quality control system, and the
seven clip types exist so the brief can be checked against them before anyone films.

## How to run it

**Sourcing, casting, briefing, directing, delivering — in that order.** Each step's output is the next step's
input, and skipping one shows up as unusable footage.

1. **Decide UGC or elevated content** using **When UGC is right, and where to find the people** (below). They need different people and
   different money.
2. **Source candidates** from the same file, with the trade-offs of each platform stated rather than implied.
3. ⭐ **Cast against the five criteria** in **Reviewing talent and casting** (below), and emit the review grid. ⚠️ Course 202
   gives six criteria and each list has one the other lacks — say so if both are in play.
4. **Write the brief with prompts, never a script**, using **Briefing a self-directed shoot, and directing a live one** (below). ⛔ This is
   the load-bearing rule: a script produces a performance, prompts produce usable material.
5. ⭐ **Emit the deliverables checklist** from **The seven clip types, and producing the checklist** (below) — the seven clip types that make
   footage editable, with the prompt to give the creator for each. ⛔ **This goes out with the brief, not
   after the shoot.** It is the instrument that decides what gets captured, and without it you get one long
   take and no ad.
6. **Cover the five parts of a self-directed shoot brief**, same file as step 4, if the creator films alone.
7. **Direct on the day** with the four common on-set problems and their fixes, same file.
8. ⚠️ **For ACA's Content Creator Brief filled for a real brand, route to `aca-brief-ugc-creator`** if it is
   installed.
9. **Check the brief contains no lines to perform.** Any dialogue written for them is step 4 undone.

## Answering well

**UGC is a format choice, not a default.** *"UGC is not one-size-fits-all. And it's not always the
answer to every brief."* It is the testing engine: fast, iterable, built for volume. Elevated
content is where brand equity gets built. ⭐ *"Most of the time, the real answer is: you need both."*

**Cast for charisma over looks, and it is not close.** *"9/10 times the more relatable talent with
strong energy outperforms traditionally attractive talent with no charisma."*

**Prompts, never a script.** *"Very very rarely is it a good idea to hand a UGC creator a full script
and say, 'Say these exact words.'"* It kills authenticity, and Chloe also claims it raises platform
rejection risk. ⚠️ That second claim is hers and unsourced; see **Provenance** (below).

**Direct like a coach.** *"You're not a critic. You're a coach."* Give emotional intentions, not line
readings. And when it is not working, *"don't push. Pivot."*

**Collect building blocks, not takes.** *"Your goal isn't just to get a good take. Your goal is to
collect the right building blocks — modular, usable footage that can be remixed, trimmed, tested and
scaled. Think of it like creative legos."*

⭐ **Sometimes you are the talent.** *"In performance marketing, creative strategists, copywriters,
even director-level or VPs will step in front of the camera. This isn't just a cost-saving move.
It's fast. It's real. And often it works."*

## Reviewing talent and casting

> "Once the submissions start rolling in, it's easy to get overwhelmed. You'll likely get a mix of
> strong contenders and a lot of noise."

⚠️ **How much filtering you do depends on where you posted.** On Trend or Cohely *"you'll already
have some built-in filters — demographics, category experience, content style."* On Backstage or a
broader platform *"expect to do a lot more manual filtering. That's not a bad thing, it just means
you need a system."*

### The first pass: three buckets

> "I like to start by sorting creators into three buckets: **Strong yes · Maybe · No.**"

### What she looks at, in her order

#### 1. ⭐ Real UGC samples, first and above everything

> "The first thing I look at? **Do they have actual UGC samples** — not just acting clips or glam
> reels, but real, vertical format social content where they speak to camera."

> "No matter how strong someone's photography or narrative film work is, **if they don't have any
> evidence they can sell a product authentically on camera, it's a risk.**"

#### 2. Presence and energy

- Do they make strong eye contact with the lens?
- Are they expressive?
- Can they deliver with natural conviction?

> "If someone mumbles, avoids the camera, or seems unsure, they're probably not a great fit for
> performance-driven content. **Even if they have the perfect look!**"

⭐ **Her strongest claim in the course, and it should be quoted whenever a client pushes for looks:**

> "**9/10 times the more relatable talent with strong energy outperforms traditionally attractive
> talent with no charisma.**"

#### 3. Tone and persona against the brief

> "If your brief called for a warm, maternal vibe, and the creator is giving influencer-hype energy,
> **they might not be right for this particular shoot, even if they're great on camera.**"

⭐ **"Not right for this shoot" is not "not good."** That distinction is what makes the grid worth
keeping rather than deleting, and it is the reason for the reuse column below.

### ⭐ The casting grid, and producing it

> "That's why using a casting grid can be incredibly helpful. You can build one in a simple
> spreadsheet."

**Chloe's five scoring criteria, and her own descriptions of each:**

| Criterion | What she says to record |
|---|---|
| **Demographic fit** | *"Age, skin tone, hair type, etc."* |
| **On-camera energy** | *"Calm? Upbeat? Playful?"* |
| **Vocal style** | *"Chatty, deadpan, bubbly, serious"* |
| **Believability** | *"Would you trust them as a real user of this product?"* |
| **Standout factor** | *"What makes them unique?"* |

**The scale:** *"Use a scale — like 1 to 5, or Must Have / Nice to Have / Not a Fit — and jot down a
few notes."*

> "The goal isn't perfection. **It's to narrow your pool to the top 3–5 people who best fit the
> brief.**"

⭐ **Keep the near-misses in the grid.** *"You can also keep folks who might have rated high on
certain criteria, but aren't perfect for this specific project, in the grid to cast for future
projects that might fit for them."*

#### Emit it in this shape

```csv
Name,Link to samples,Real UGC samples?,Demographic fit,On-camera energy,Vocal style,Believability,Standout factor,Score,Bucket,Rationale (one sentence),Reuse for
```

⚠️ **Illustrative rows, to show the shape.** Replace with real applicants.

| Name | Real UGC samples? | Demographic fit | On-camera energy | Vocal style | Believability | Standout factor | Bucket | Rationale | Reuse for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Yes, 6 vertical to-camera | Fits the 35+ brief | Upbeat | Chatty | High, speaks like a user | Deadpan humour | **Strong yes** | Reads as a real customer and lands the sceptical opener | — |
| B | No, acting reel only | Fits | Calm | Serious | Untested to camera | Trained delivery | **No** | No evidence of selling a product to lens; the reel is narrative work | Revisit if we shoot scripted |
| C | Yes, 2 clips | Outside this brief's persona | Playful | Bubbly | High | Very high energy | **Maybe** | Strong performer, wrong tone for a calm brief | ⭐ The playful product line |

⛔ **Fill the "Real UGC samples?" column first and let it gate the rest.** It is the criterion she
puts above all others, and a grid that averages it away with four other scores defeats the point.

#### ⛔ Before you write a word in this grid

Chloe's grid scores **demographic fit** on age, skin tone and hair type, and her worked rationales
describe hair colour, accent and body type. That is ordinary casting for advertising: you are
casting someone to read as your customer, and appearance is part of the role.

**It is also a written, scored, durable record about named real people.** So:

- **Write what the content needs, not a rating of the person.** *"Brief calls for 35+, this reads
  35+"* is a note about the role. A number out of five next to someone's skin tone is a note about
  them.
- **Keep every cell defensible against the brief.** If a criterion is not in the brief, it should
  not be scoring anyone.
- **Never record anything you would not want read back to the applicant**, because a spreadsheet
  shared for client approval is a document that outlives the shoot.

⚠️ **The course does not raise any of this.** It is added here because the grid is the artifact this
skill produces, and producing it is the moment the question arises.

### ⭐ Presenting talent for approval

> "Here's something I've learned the hard way: **don't present talent for approval without
> context.** If you need buy-in from a client or internal stakeholder, don't just drop a few
> headshots in a Slack message. **Give a one-sentence rationale for each person.**"

**Her two examples:**

> "Katie has a really bubbly, inviting presence. Her red hair and British accent make her instantly
> memorable."

> "Brian isn't a typical male model, but he's been a top performer in our pants ads. His relatable
> tone and 'no-BS' energy connects with our 35+ male audience."

> "When you frame it like that, **decision-makers understand the strategy and are more likely to
> trust your recommendation.**"

⭐ **The second example is the more useful pattern**, because it leads with performance evidence and
pre-empts the objection ("isn't a typical male model") instead of waiting for it. A rationale that
names a past result is much harder to overrule on taste.

### Tracking who performs

> "Once you start producing content with a few creators, **keep tabs on which ones consistently
> deliver great results.** If someone's content consistently drives high CTR, low CPA, or strong
> hook rates, keep them in your rotation."

**At an agency:** *"tag creators in your internal tracker for potential reuse across other brands or
product categories."*

> "At OpenStore, we have a running **'Favorite Talent' list** based on ad performance. It saves time
> and gives us a reliable bench when we need to scale fast."

⭐ **This is the compounding part of the whole course.** Casting is expensive the first time and
nearly free the fifth, but only if somebody wrote down who worked. Add the performance column to the
grid after the ads run rather than starting a separate document.

⚠️ **Judge the creator on the metrics creative actually owns.** CTR and hook rate reflect the
performer and the hook; CPA is contaminated by the landing page, the price and the offer, so a
creator marked down on CPA may have been let down by something they never touched.

## When UGC is right, and where to find the people

### What UGC means here

> "UGC technically stands for user-generated content—but in performance marketing, we use that term
> a little differently. We're not just talking about organic content, **UGC typically means native-
> looking, creator-made video content—often scripted, directed, and edited** but still giving al
> native, personal, and real to the platform"

> "Someone holding the product up to the camera. Talking straight to lens. Explaining their
> experience. Showing a transformation. Using the kind of casual, unscripted energy that feels like
> **a friend giving you a rec, not an ad pushing you to buy.**"

⭐ **Note what that definition does not require: that the creator is a real customer, or that the
content is unpaid, or that it is unscripted.** It is a production style, not a provenance claim. The
word "authentic" in this course means "reads as native", not "unpaid testimonial".

### ⭐ The spectrum, not the binary

> "For the brands I oversee at OpenStore, I often think about the creative mix along a spectrum."

| | UGC / native | Elevated / studio |
|---|---|---|
| **Character** | Low-lift, lo-fi, fast turnaround | Polished, brand-forward, production-heavy |
| **What it is for** | *"Your scrappy, high-velocity testing engine"* | *"Where you build brand equity"* |
| **Because it is** | Fast to produce · easy to iterate or redo · built for volume · blends into the feed | Premium positioning · cinematic storytelling · reinforces product quality and lifestyle aspiration |
| **Use it for** | Testing value props, pain points or personas · collecting quick signal data on hooks and CTAs | Evergreen hero ads · website banners and PDP imagery and video · seasonal campaigns · establishing trust through aesthetics |

⚠️ **Each end is itself a spectrum**, and this is the part people flatten: *"A studio e-commerce
shoot versus a full-on TV commercial production, or a more elevated UGC product shoot versus a quick
and dirty in-car testimonial."* "Let's do UGC" does not specify a budget or a look.

**The choice, in her words:**

- **UGC over elevated** *"when speed, signal and relatability matter most"*
- **Elevated over UGC** *"when you need brand lift, premium perception, or a strong visual identity"*

> "Most of the time, the real answer is: **you need both.**"

⭐ **This is a budgeting argument as much as a creative one.** A system that scales native UGC while
leaving room for hero moments is the goal; treating UGC as the cheap default gets you volume with no
brand, and treating studio as the standard gets you a beautiful account with no test data.

### The four routes to talent

> "Let's start with where to look."

| Route | What you get | The trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Backstage** | *"A massive platform used by actors, models, voiceover artists, dancers."* High volume | ⚠️ *"A very wide mix of experience levels. A lot of people submit to every listing that pops up."* Expect dozens or hundreds of responses, many *"completely unfamiliar with short-form, social-first UGC"* |
| **UGC platforms** — Trend.io, Billo, Cohely | *"Built for brands looking for social content."* Creators experienced at vertical, to-camera, prompt-following work. You pay per deliverable and the platform handles some logistics | ⭐ *"If you're in a time crunch or working with a limited budget, they can be a great shortcut"* |
| **Organic outreach, especially TikTok** | ⭐ *"The third and often most underrated option."* Creators who already know how to speak to camera and hook attention. Search within your product niche | Micro-creators with small followings but *"very tapped into what works on short-form video. And they're often excited to do UGC work if you reach out directly"* |
| ⭐ **You** | See below | |

⚠️ **TikTok sourcing works even if you do not advertise there.** *"Even if you're not running ads on
TikTok, it's one of the best places to find creators."* The skill you are buying is on-camera
delivery, and it ports.

#### ⭐ Sometimes you are the talent

> "here's one more thing no one tells you: sometimes, **you are the talent.** In performance
> marketing—especially in fast-moving sprints—creative strategists, copywriters, even directors-
> level or VPs will step in front of the camera. We shoot testimonials. We use our hands in POV
> product demos. We test new hooks in selfie format."

> "This isn't just a cost-saving move. It's fast. It's real. And often, it works. **You know your
> product. You know your angle. You know the voice and delivery style that performs.**"

⚠️ **She frames this as a career asset, not a fallback:** *"If you're comfortable filming content
with your phone and can deliver with energy and clarity, it's a superpower. And it's something
hiring managers in this space increasingly look for when building creative teams."*

### The casting call

> "Your casting posting should clearly include:"

| # | What to state |
|---|---|
| 1 | **What the product is** |
| 2 | **Who the brand is** — *"ok if you don't want to disclose the actual brand, but it helps"* |
| 3 | **The tone or vibe you're going for** |
| 4 | **Who you're looking for** in terms of energy, look and/or persona |
| 5 | **Exactly what you need submitted** — a final edited video? Raw clips? Both? |
| 6 | **The rate** |

⛔ **The rate is a required field and the course gives no benchmark for it.** Chloe says to be
specific and never says what specific looks like. Ask for the budget rather than filling it in.

> "Be specific, but not rigid. **If you're too broad, you'll waste time reviewing people who aren't
> a fit. But if you're too narrow, you'll limit the number of submissions** — especially if you're
> on a tight timeline."

#### ⭐ The 30-second sample, and its cost

> "You can also explicitly request a quick **30-second video sample** — ideally speaking unscripted
> about a product they like. This gives you a fast read on camera presence, vocal tone, energy level
> and authenticity. **You'll learn more in 30 seconds of off-the-cuff talking than in an entire
> polished reel.**"

⚠️ **And she names the trade-off rather than selling it:** *"Requiring a video sample will no doubt
reduce the overall number of submissions you'll receive, and often if they have a solid UGC reel
this step can be avoided."*

⭐ **So the rule is conditional, not universal:** ask for the sample when the applicant pool is broad
or the reels are not UGC. Skip it when someone already has a real UGC reel and the timeline is tight.

### Matching the route to the situation

> "Use the right platform for your needs. **Backstage gives you volume. Trend, Billo and Cohely give
> you speed and relevance. TikTok gives you nuance and potential long-term collaborators.**"

⭐ **That last phrase is the one worth acting on.** The first two routes are transactional and the
third builds a bench, which matters because the course later tells you to track and reuse the
creators who perform.

## Briefing a self-directed shoot, and directing a live one

> "This is where so many UGC projects fall apart — **not because the creator wasn't talented, but
> because the brief didn't give them what they needed to succeed.**"

> "A vague brief is a wasted budget."

> "**80% of great UGC is in the brief.**"

---

### The five parts of a brief

#### 1. Product Information

> "Be clear about what the product is, what it does, and what problem it solves. You don't need to
> include every spec, but you do need to make sure the creator knows what makes the product special.
> **Especially the emotional benefit — how it makes the customer feel.**"

**Her worked example:**

> "This is a skincare mist designed to calm redness and replenish the skin barrier. Customers love
> it for how gentle it feels and how quickly it takes down inflammation. It's perfect for anyone
> who's self-conscious about redness."

⭐ **Three sentences: what it is, what customers say about it, who it is for.** Notice the middle one
is customer language, not a feature list — that is what the creator will end up paraphrasing.

#### 2. ⛔ Script Prompts, not a script

> "This is one of the most important distinctions. **Very very rarely is it s good idea to hand a
> UGC creator a full script and say, “Say these exact words.”**"

**Two reasons she gives:**

1. It kills authenticity.
2. ⚠️ *"It increases your risk of content rejection on Meta or TikTok. Platforms know when something
   is scripted, and often flag that content as low-quality or 'inauthentic behavior.'"*

⚠️ **The second reason is her claim and carries no source.** See **Provenance** (below). The first
reason stands on its own and is the one to lead with.

**Use bullet-point prompts instead. Her examples:**

- Describe the problem you were having before using the product
- Show your real reaction to unboxing or using it for the first time
- Tell us one thing you didn't expect, but loved

> "When you let creators use their own words, the footage feels natural, human and native to the
> platform."

#### 3. Structure Guidance

> "You don't need to control every word, but you do need to guide the flow."

**The structure she says most high-performing UGC follows:**

| # | Beat |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Before state / problem state** — the problem I was experiencing |
| 2 | **Category discovery** — other things I tried that didn't work |
| 3 | **Product discovery / experience** |
| 4 | **Solution / resolved state** |

⭐ **Beat 2 is the one people drop, and it is the one that does the persuasive work.** Naming what
failed first is what makes the product's claim credible rather than another promise.

**How she words it in an actual brief:**

> "Start with a strong hook that calls out a problem youve experienced and any solutions you tried
> in the past that you were disappointed with Then show how the product works or talk through your
> experience using it. End with a clear recommendation or why you'd tell a friend to try it."

#### 4. Visual & Audio Instructions

> "This part gets overlooked, but it's so important."

| Instruction |
|---|
| **Format: always vertical, 9:16** |
| **Lighting** — natural light is great, avoid dark or blown-out shots. She recommends shooting in front of a window |
| **No background music**, unless requested |
| **No cluttered backgrounds or visible brands** |
| **No jump cuts**, unless specified |
| **Stable framing** — no handheld wobble unless it adds energy |

> "I always include a visual reference when possible — either from past winning content or a similar
> format that nailed the vibe."

#### 5. Do's and Don'ts

> "Finally, include any compliance notes or brand safety guardrails."

- **Words to avoid**, like *"miracle"* or *"cure"*
- **Claims that require disclaimers**
- **Legal reminders** to avoid competitor mentions, profanity or sensitive topics

> "It's better to spell these out upfront than to get unusable footage and lose time on reshoots."

⭐ **This section is the cheapest insurance in the whole process.** A compliance problem discovered
in the edit costs a reshoot; the same problem prevented costs three lines in a document.

---

### What a good brief sounds like

**Hers, quoted from real creator briefs:**

> "Talk to the camera like you're FaceTiming a friend about this product."

> "Show us your real reaction when you use it — don't be afraid to make a face!"

> "Include a quick demo if possible, but keep it casual. You're not doing a tutorial."

**And a bad one:**

> "Say this line exactly: 'This shampoo changed my life!'"

> "Nope. That can be a red flag for authenticity and for performance."

⭐ **Every good example gives a relationship or a situation, not an instruction.** "FaceTiming a
friend" tells the creator how to be, which they can act; "be authentic" tells them a quality, which
they cannot.

### ⭐ The creator's own checklist

> "Always include a checklist at the end of the brief."

| Filmed in vertical 9:16 |
|---|
| Spoke clearly and confidently |
| No music or filters |
| Product shown or used clearly |
| Hook, story and CTA all included |

⛔ **This checklist is written for the creator, in their voice, and belongs at the end of the brief.**
It is not the same document as your own deliverables checklist in **The seven clip types, and producing the checklist** (below), which
is what you check the returned footage against.

> "A strong brief isn't just a doc. **It's your quality control system.**"

### Directing a live shoot

> "**You're not a critic. You're a coach.** Live direction is not about control, it's about
> collaboration."

⚠️ **Start from who is actually in front of you.** *"Most UGC creators, and even many professional
models or influencers, aren't trained actors. They're real people trying to look comfortable on
camera, talk about a product, and follow your lead all at once."*

#### 1. Start with warmth

> "Before you even start rolling, spend **about five to ten minutes just chatting** with the talent.
> Ask how they're feeling, talk about something totally unrelated to the shoot. Show them the set,
> the lighting, the camera setup. Anything to reduce anxiety"

⭐ **And she is specific about awkward subject matter**, which is where this matters most:

> "Especially if the shoot involves sensitive or slightly embarrassing subject matter — like shaping
> underwear, acne products, or body odor — **you need to be the first one to bring warmth and
> energy.**"

**Her worked example**, a Jack Archer testimonial shoot built on a line about *"pants that make your
butt look good"*: *"Instead of tiptoeing around it, I leaned in. I made jokes. I told the talent,
'This is a fan favorite, people LOVE these pants. Let's hype it up like we're on QVC.' And they
matched my energy. They loosened up."*

> "**Your confidence gives them permission to relax.**"

#### 2. Direct with intentions, not line reads

**Avoid:** *"No, say it more like this."* · *"That didn't sound natural."*

**Instead:**

> "Can you imagine you're explaining this to your best friend who's skeptical?"

> "Let's try one where you sound totally over it — like this product saved your sanity."

> "That was great — can we also get one that's a bit slower, a bit more thoughtful?"

> "These types of notes give the creator **an emotional context to work with, rather than just
> trying to parrot your delivery.**"

⭐ **Shoot for options even when the first take is good.** *"Have them repeat the same line or beat
2 to 3 times in slightly different ways. Even if the first take is great, get another. This gives
you options in the edit, especially if you're cutting multiple variants or testing different hooks."*

#### 3. When the energy is low, pivot

> "Sometimes talent just isn't “on” that day."

⭐ **Don't force it** is the operative advice.

| If the delivery is flat | Try |
|---|---|
| They are locked to the script | **Ask them to improvise.** Let them tell a story or demo the product without pressure |
| Testimonial mode is not working | **Change the format** — switch to a reaction or unboxing |
| They are stiff in the body | **Use movement.** Walk and talk, or hold and handle the product while speaking |

> "**If it's not working, don't push. Pivot.** Optimize the time you have on set. It's better to
> redirect to something that works than to waste 45 minutes forcing something that doesn't."

#### 4. Four common on-set problems

| Problem | Her fix |
|---|---|
| **Talent seems stiff or robotic** | ⭐ A **throwaway take** where they say whatever they want. *"Usually this is when they loosen up, and that take might even be the winner"* |
| **The creator keeps messing up a line** | **Let them paraphrase.** If it is a critical claim, break it into parts or write it on a cue card out of frame |
| **They're too influencer-y or salesy** | Ask for one version that feels like a voice note to a friend: *"Imagine you're texting your friend about this product. What would you say?"* |
| **They don't believe in the product** | ⛔ *"Don't try to manufacture excitement."* Pivot to a different angle — curiosity or social proof. *"Just tell us what stood out to you in the instructions or packaging"* |

⭐ **The fourth one is the ethically important one and she gets it right.** The fix is to change what
you ask them to say, not to coach them into sounding convinced. A testimonial performed by someone
who does not believe it is the thing the whole format's credibility depends on not doing.

#### 5. Leave space for magic

> "Some of the best moments in UGC happen when you don't over-direct. A funny improv, a moment of
> surprise, a real emotion — that's the stuff people stop scrolling for."

> "Once you've got your 'safe' takes, give the talent room to play. Say: **'We got what we needed,
> now let's try one just for fun.'** You might capture something better than anything in the brief."

⭐ **The order matters.** Safe takes first, play second. Reversed, a flat shoot has no usable footage
and no time left.

## The seven clip types, and producing the checklist

> "Even the best brief and most charismatic creator can fall flat **if the right clips don't get
> captured.**"

### ⭐ The mindset shift

> "Your goal isn't just to get 'a good take.' **Your goal is to collect the right building blocks —
> modular, usable footage that can be remixed, trimmed, tested and scaled.**"

> "Think of it like **creative legos**: the more thoughtful clips you capture, the more ad formats
> you can build."

⚠️ **These clips are what separate a deliverable from a file.** In her words, they are *"the
difference between a usable deliverable and one that ends up in the trash folder."*

---

### The seven

> "Here's the checklist I use across all the brands I work on, refined across hundreds of
> deliverables, to ensure the final ad is actually editable."

#### 1. A strong face-to-camera testimonial take

**The anchor clip.** Chest-up, vertical, directly into the lens, *"delivering their story with
energy and clarity. You want natural pacing, emotional conviction and clean eye contact."*

> "This clip usually carries the hook, the product insight, or the relatable 'before' moment."

⭐ **Always ask for 2 to 3 takes with different tones** — *"one relaxed, one punchy, one a little
more exaggerated, so you have flexibility in edit."*

#### 2. Clear product demo footage

> "**This one's non-negotiable.** You need to see the product being used."

*"Whether it's applying skincare, zipping up a pair of Jetsetter Pants, turning on a device —
whatever your product does, show it in action. If it solves a problem, **that problem should be
visible.** And if it has a wow moment, make sure it's captured."*

**Her pro tip:** if the creator is demonstrating with their hands, especially for beauty, *"ask for
tight close-ups and natural light. Think POV or overhead angles if relevant."*

#### 3. Still product hold

> "This one's often forgotten, **but it saves the day in post.**"

Hold the product still, in good lighting, framed nicely, for **3 to 5 seconds**. Ideally smiling or
nodding while holding it.

> "This gives your editor a clean moment to **overlay pricing, promo language, or a call-to-action**
> in a way that looks natural and native."

⭐ **This is the clip that makes an ad reusable across offers.** Without it, every promotion needs a
new shoot; with it, the same deliverable carries a different price on it next month.

#### 4. Reaction shots and micro-moments

> "These are small but powerful. A five-second smile. A look of surprise. A head tilt. A moment of
> laughter or playful sarcasm."

Ask creators to film a few separately, or capture them between takes.

> "They give your editor flexibility, especially for jump cuts or voiceover overlays, **and make the
> video feel human and dynamic.**"

**How to prompt them, in her words:**

- *"Give us your 'omg I can't believe this worked' face."*
- *"Now show us your skeptical face, like you're not sure this is for real."*
- *"Smile like you're texting your best friend about this product."*

#### 5. Hook line in 3 to 5 variations

> "The first three seconds are everything. That's why we always ask for multiple takes of just the
> hook. **Think of it like an audition — same line, but delivered with a range of tones: bold,
> whispery, dramatic, cheeky.**"

**Her examples:**

> "I was this close to giving up on my skin…"

> "Men over 40 are not going to like this ad."

> "I would never have believed this if I hadn't tried it myself."

⚠️ **Same line, different deliveries.** This is a performance test, not five different hooks —
writing five different hooks is a separate job, and confusing the two loses the clean comparison.

#### 6. B-roll lifestyle or problem footage

> "This one depends on the product, but **can elevate your ad from functional to emotional.**"

Ask for **2 to 3 short clips** of the product in its natural habitat, and/or problem shots showing
the "before" state. **Her examples:**

- A frustrated mom picking up a mess in the playroom
- A guy packing Jetsetter shorts into his suitcase for a trip
- A guy struggling with pants that are too baggy
- A CEO drinking a coffee calmly with morning light streaming in after she finished her taxes

> "This footage builds trust and relatability. **It shows that the product fits into a real
> routine.**"

#### 7. Voice-only or voiceover clips ⚠️ *(she marks this one optional)*

> "If the creator is comfortable, ask for a clean voice recording of their testimonial script or the
> hook."

> "This gives you the option to **pair a strong voiceover with alternate visuals — and sometimes
> that combo outperforms face-to-camera content.**"

Also useful for TikTok-style edits and product montage formats.

⭐ **Optional in her list, but it is the cheapest one to add.** It costs the creator a minute and it
is the only clip that lets you rebuild the ad entirely if the on-camera footage disappoints.

---

### ⛔ Producing the checklist

> "How do you make sure creators actually give you all these clips? Simple: **you bake it into your
> brief.** Use bullet-point checklists. Include time stamps or sample videos. And if you're
> live-directing, walk them through this sequence on the call."

> "**If you treat your shoot like a checklist, not** just **a vibe, you'll walk away with everything
> you need to build three to five performing ads from one deliverable.**"

**Emit it in this shape**, filled for the actual product:

```csv
#,Clip type,Required?,What to capture for this product,How many,Prompt to give the creator,Received?
```

⚠️ **Illustrative rows.** Replace the middle columns with the real product's specifics.

| # | Clip type | Required? | What to capture | How many | Prompt to give the creator | Received? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Face-to-camera testimonial** | Required | Chest-up, vertical, to lens. Carries the hook and the before moment | 2-3 takes, different tones | "Tell me the story like you're catching up with a friend. Then one punchier, then one bigger" | ☐ |
| 2 | **Product demo** | ⛔ Required, non-negotiable | The product doing the thing, with the problem visible | 1-2 | "Show me it working. If there's a moment where it clicks, make sure we see it" | ☐ |
| 3 | **Still product hold** | Required | Held still, good light, nicely framed, smiling or nodding | 1, **3-5 seconds** | "Just hold it steady and smile for a slow count of five" | ☐ |
| 4 | **Reaction shots** | Required | Smile, surprise, head tilt, scepticism | 3-4 micro-clips | "Give us your 'omg I can't believe this worked' face" | ☐ |
| 5 | **Hook line variations** | Required | The same hook line, different tones: bold, whispery, dramatic, cheeky | **3-5 of one line** | "Same words, five ways. Treat it like an audition" | ☐ |
| 6 | **B-roll lifestyle / problem** | Required | The product in a real routine, and/or the before state | 2-3 | "Show me the annoying version of this without the product" | ☐ |
| 7 | **Voiceover** | ⚠️ Optional | Clean audio of the testimonial or the hook | 1 | "If you're comfortable, record just the audio somewhere quiet" | ☐ |

⛔ **The "Received?" column is the point.** This checklist is used twice: once inside the brief so
the creator knows what to film, and once against the returned files before the creator is paid and
released. Checking it only at the first moment is how a reshoot happens.

⚠️ **Never mark a clip received without opening it.** A file named `demo.mp4` is not a demo, and the
whole failure mode this checklist exists to prevent is discovering in the edit that something is
missing.

⛔ **If a clip type genuinely does not apply, write why in the row rather than deleting it.** A
service with nothing to hold has no still product hold; a deleted row is indistinguishable from a
forgotten one.
