# Course 103: Creative Fundamentals

Mirella Crespi (Creative Milkshake), 19m 46s, 1 module: The Performance Framework.

The course's own promise:

> "When you look at an ad, what do you see? Most people just see a product being sold. But after
> this part of the course, you will see a strategic framework of carefully chosen elements working
> together to drive a specific action. You'll be able to analyze and reverse engineer any creative
> you want."

The central idea is **modularity**. An ad is an assembly of interchangeable parts, not a monolith.
Once you can name the parts, you can diagnose which one is failing and replace it without
rebuilding anything else.

> "It's surgical rather than shotgun."

## What this skill needs from you

Teaches first, asks second. "What are the 10 angle types?" or "how does the building blocks
framework work?" needs no inputs at all.

**Required, to analyse a specific ad:**

1. **The ad itself** — a link, a script, a file, or a description. For video, describe it
   **chronologically with rough timestamps**, since the worksheet is built around timing
2. **What the product is**

**Required, to generate angles or concepts:**

1. **The offer** — what is being sold, and how it is packaged
2. **Who it is for**
3. **The main pain points or benefits**, as far as they are known

**Optional, and it sharpens the output:**

4. Performance data, if the ad is live (hook rate, thumbstop, CTR, drop-off points)
5. The awareness stage being targeted
6. What has already been tried

⚠️ **A description is enough; a guess is not.** If someone has not supplied the ad, ask for it
rather than analysing a hypothetical. The worksheet's value is in per-block effectiveness ratings
against real creative, and those cannot be invented. Equally, if they describe the ad but omit
timings, work without timestamps and say so rather than inventing them.

## Diagnosing to a level

The framework's most valuable use is turning "the ad isn't working" into a specific, fixable
statement. The mapping the course gives:

| Symptom | Suspect | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Low hook rate / thumbstop | The **Hook block** | They never got past 3 seconds, so nothing else was seen |
| Drop-off mid-video | A specific **Body block** | Find the timestamp and name the block that occupies it |
| Views but no clicks | The **CTA block**, or congruency | They watched and were not moved to act |
| Clicks but no conversions | **Congruency** with the post-click experience | The ad promised something the page does not deliver |

This is why timestamps matter in the analysis. "The testimonial section is where people leave" is
actionable; "the ad is boring" is not.

## How to run it

**Decide which of the two jobs is on the table first**, per the section above: learning the framework, or
taking a specific ad apart. The steps differ.

1. **Establish the Hook-Body-CTA spine** from **The modular building blocks framework** (below). Everything else hangs off it.
2. **Introduce the fourteen blocks by family** — Structure, Product, Person — from the same file. Families
   first, then blocks, or it becomes a list to memorise.
3. **Give the vocabulary** in **The language of creative strategy** (below) if they will brief anyone. Fifteen

4. **Then angles** in **The 10 types of angles** (below), all ten with their triggers. ⚠️ Keep angle and block

5. **Diagnose to a level** using the section below, so a flat ad gets attributed to a part rather than to
   taste.
6. **Produce the worksheet** from **The Ad Building Blocks Analysis Worksheet** (below) when there is a real ad in front of you.
   ⚠️ For a single ad teardown or a set of angles, `aca-analyze-building-blocks` and `aca-build-angles` are
   the direct routes — **if they are installed**.
7. **Check they can name the block that is missing** from an ad they think is weak. "It doesn't land" means
   step 2 did not.

## Answering well

The frameworks are inventory, not the deliverable. Naming that an ad uses a Demonstration block is
not analysis; saying the demonstration arrives at 0:08 after the emotional arc has already peaked,
and would work better at 0:04, is.

When you have the ad, be specific and cite timestamps. When you do not, teach the framework and ask
for it.

**Where the ad is genuinely good, say so and say why.** The purpose of a teardown is a transferable
lesson, and "this works, here is the mechanism, here is where else it would work" is a better
outcome than a list of improvements invented to seem rigorous.

## What this course does not cover

It is about the **anatomy** of an ad, not the psychology of the person watching. Awareness stages
and emotional drivers appear here as inputs to the analysis (Section 5 of the worksheet asks for
both), but the frameworks behind them are taught in Course 102. If the question is really "who is
this for and what do they want", that is the other course.

It also does not cover production, media buying, or platform mechanics.

## The modular building blocks framework

The core of Course 103. An ad is not a monolith, it is an assembly of interchangeable parts. Once
you see the parts, you can diagnose which one is failing and swap it without rebuilding the ad.

### The three-part structure

Every successful direct response ad has it.

#### The Hook (0-3 seconds)

The scroll-stopping moment. Roughly three seconds to convince someone not to scroll past. The hook
must immediately signal relevance and generate curiosity.

Great hooks do one or more of these:

- **Identify a specific pain point** — *"Tired of struggling with patchy beard growth?"*
- **Challenge a common belief** — *"The one skincare ingredient dermatologists say you should NEVER use"*
- **Make a bold promise** — *"How we generated 427 qualified leads in 24 hours without paid ads"*
- **Create a pattern interrupt** — something unexpected that breaks the scroll rhythm

#### The Body

The main persuasive content. Where the argument is made, the product is shown, and objections are
handled.

#### The CTA

The action-driving closer.

### The 14 blocks, in three categories

#### Structure blocks — the backbone

| Block | Is |
|---|---|
| **Hook Block** | Your attention-grabbing opener |
| **Body Block** | Your main persuasive content |
| **CTA Block** | Your action-driving closer |

#### Product blocks — these showcase your offering

| Block | Is |
|---|---|
| **Product Introduction** | How you first reveal the product |
| **Demonstration** | Showing the product in action |
| **Features / Benefits** | Highlighting key selling points |
| **Buying Experience** | Making the purchase process clear |
| **Unboxing** | Creating anticipation and trust |

#### Person blocks — these create emotional connection

| Block | Is |
|---|---|
| **Problem Statement** | Articulating the pain point |
| **Failed Alternatives** | What did not work before |
| **Desired Outcome** | The transformation promised |
| **Before & After** | Visual proof of change |
| **Social Proof** | Testimonials and social validation |
| **Storytelling** | Narrative that ties everything together |

#### Why the categories matter

Product blocks argue; Person blocks connect. An ad built only from Product blocks is a
specification sheet, and one built only from Person blocks never explains what is being sold. The
category split is a fast diagnostic for an ad that feels flat: check whether one category is
missing entirely.

### Why modularity is the point

> "The power of this approach is its flexibility. Current hook has a low hook rate? Swap in a
> different one. Seeing a big drop off in the part of the video with a testimonial? Switch it for a
> visual product demo. Test both and let the data decide."

And the consequence for optimisation:

> "Performance drops by 20%? Perhaps it's time to test a new hook while keeping the high-performing
> body and CTA intact. This targeted approach to optimization is far more efficient than scrapping
> entire ads and starting over. **It's surgical rather than shotgun.**"

### Proven block combinations

Three high-performing formats:

**Problem-Solution format**
- Hook: Problem Block (emotional pain point)
- Body: Demonstration Block + Failed Alternatives Block
- CTA: Desired Outcome Block + CTA Block

**Authority-Driven format**
- Hook: Social Proof Block (expert endorsement)
- Body: Features/Benefits Block + Demonstration Block
- CTA: CTA Block with risk reversal

**Transformation format**
- Hook: Before & After Block (visual impact)
- Body: Storytelling Block + Product Introduction
- CTA: Social Proof Block + CTA Block

These are starting points, not a menu to pick from and stop. The value of naming them is that they
give you a baseline to vary deliberately.

### Building your block library

The implementation process, in four steps.

**1. Audit your existing assets**
- Review current content and categorise elements into the appropriate blocks
- Note which blocks you are missing or need to strengthen

**2. Create block variations**
- Develop 3 to 5 different hooks addressing various pain points
- Record multiple product demonstrations from different angles
- Collect diverse testimonials highlighting different benefits

**3. Test systematically**
- Create A/B tests that isolate specific blocks
- Example: same body and CTA, test Hook A against Hook B
- Let data determine which blocks perform best for which audiences

**4. Document performance**
- Track which blocks perform best together
- Build a performance library to reference for future campaigns

> "Different audiences may respond to different block combinations. What works for cold traffic
> might not work for retargeting audiences who are already familiar with your brand."

The library is the compounding asset here. A team that has run fifty ads without cataloguing blocks
has fifty ads; a team that catalogued them has a reusable inventory and a record of what
combinations worked on whom.

### Five real-world success patterns

Drawn from analysing thousands of high-performing ads across industries.

**1. The First 3 Seconds Rule.** The most successful ads deliver their core promise within the
first three seconds. *"Never save your best for last - viewers won't stick around."*

**2. Authenticity trumps production value.** Authentic, believable content consistently outperforms
high-production, overly polished ads. Especially true for testimonials and demonstrations.

**3. Emotional → Logical → Emotional.** Top performers open with emotional appeal (the pain or
desire), provide logical support (features, proof), then close with emotional reinforcement (the
transformation).

**4. One message, one ad.** Communicating too many benefits dilutes impact. The best ads focus on a
single core message, hammered home through multiple angles.

**5. Visual reinforcement.** When visuals directly support the spoken or written message,
comprehension and retention increase sharply. *"Misalignment between visuals and message creates
cognitive dissonance and reduces performance."*

Pattern 3 and pattern 4 are the two most often violated together: an ad that opens logically with a
feature list and covers four benefits fails both, and it is the single most common shape of a
mediocre ad.

### Reverse engineering any ad

The framework's other use: taking apart someone else's winner.

**1. Identify the three-part structure.** Where is the hook? Where does the body begin? When does
it transition to the CTA?

**2. Catalog the building blocks.** Which specific blocks are used? Is it leading with a problem
block or social proof? Demonstration or storytelling in the body?

**3. Note the execution details.** How long is each section? What visual techniques? What emotional
triggers are activated?

**4. Create your swipe file.** Build a collection of reverse-engineered ads that resonated,
organised by block structure.

`worksheet.md` turns this into the formal analysis ACA ships, and it ends by asking for the ad's
**core building block formula**, for example:

> Hook (Question) → Problem → Failed Alternatives → Demonstration → Social Proof → CTA with Scarcity

That formula is the transferable artifact. The specific ad is not reusable; its block sequence is.

### Section 4 — Overall Analysis & Insights

- **Block combination assessment:** which blocks work particularly well together in this ad?
- **Missing blocks:** which blocks are notably absent that might have strengthened it?
- **Modular replication potential:** which specific blocks or combinations could be reused
  elsewhere?
- **Structure effectiveness:** how well does the Hook-Body-CTA structure work here? What could be
  improved?
- **Key performance drivers:** which 2-3 blocks contribute most to this ad's effectiveness?
- **Modular improvement suggestions:** how could this ad be improved by modifying, adding or
  rearranging specific blocks?

The **missing blocks** question is the one that most often produces the useful finding. It is
easier to see what an ad does than what it declines to do, and the absent block is frequently the
opportunity.

### What to do with a completed sheet

The formula in Section 5 is the transferable output. The ad itself is not reusable, and copying it
produces a worse version of someone else's creative. The block sequence is reusable, and running it
against a different offer, angle and audience is the actual technique.

Analysing three or four ads from the same competitor and comparing their formulas is more
informative than analysing one in depth: the repeated blocks reveal what they have found works,
and the blocks none of them use reveal what nobody in the category is trying.

## The 10 types of angles

The angle is the **why**: the reason someone should buy right now. It is the single most useful
lever in creative, because the same offer aimed at the same audience performs completely
differently depending on the angle chosen.

ACA's ten, each with its core message, when to use it, the psychological triggers it pulls, and
example applications.

**One angle per ad.** An ad attempting three angles communicates none of them clearly. Test angles
against each other rather than stacking them inside one execution.

---

### 1. Promotion
> **"Because it's on sale"**

Emphasises limited-time offers, discounts, special deals, or exclusive pricing. Creates urgency and
appeals to value-conscious customers.

**When to use:** during sales events or seasonal promotions · to clear inventory or increase
short-term revenue · when targeting price-sensitive audiences · to reactivate dormant customers

**Psychological triggers:** scarcity · loss aversion · value perception

**Examples:**
- `[PRODUCT]`: "Flash Sale: 48 hours only! Save 30% on all [PRODUCT] bundles"
- `[SERVICE]`: "Join today and get your first month free - limited spots available"
- `[APP]`: "Unlock all premium features at 50% off - this weekend only!"

---

### 2. Education
> **"Because it does this"**

Focuses on product functionality, features, and unique mechanisms. Appeals to rational
decision-making and works well for complex or innovative products.

**When to use:** for products with unique technical features · when targeting logical,
information-driven buyers · for higher-priced items that require justification · when your solution
has a unique mechanism

**Psychological triggers:** curiosity · desire for mastery and knowledge · rational problem-solving

**Examples:**
- `[TECH PRODUCT]`: "How our proprietary algorithm analyzes 50+ sleep factors to improve your rest quality"
- `[SKINCARE]`: "The science behind our patent-pending delivery system that reaches 7 skin layers"
- `[SOFTWARE]`: "See how our platform automates your workflow in 3 simple steps"

---

### 3. Social Proof
> **"Because everyone loves it"**

Leverages the wisdom of crowds and demonstrates widespread adoption. Shows that others have already
made the purchase decision and are satisfied.

**When to use:** when you have impressive customer numbers · for products with strong reviews and
ratings · in competitive markets where trust is crucial · for status-oriented products

**Psychological triggers:** conformity bias · fear of missing out · desire for belonging

**Examples:**
- `[PRODUCT]`: "Join the 250,000+ customers who've transformed their [problem area] with [product]"
- `[SERVICE]`: "Why 98% of customers renew their subscription after the first year"
- `[APP]`: "The #1 rated solution in the App Store with over 10,000 five-star reviews"

---

### 4. Influencer
> **"Because this person loves it"**

Uses trusted personalities, experts, or celebrities to build credibility. Leverages transferred
authority and trust from a **specific individual** rather than the crowd.

**When to use:** when endorsed by relevant experts or celebrities · for products where expertise
matters in the decision · when targeting communities that follow influencers · to establish
authority in new markets

**Psychological triggers:** authority bias · aspirational identity · credibility through association

**Examples:**
- `[PRODUCT]`: "The exact [product] that [celebrity/expert] uses daily for [benefit]"
- `[FITNESS]`: "Developed with Olympic trainer [name] to maximize your workout results"
- `[BEAUTY]`: "The secret weapon in [celebrity]'s skincare routine for the past 5 years"

⚠️ Distinct from Social Proof: the crowd versus one trusted person. They fail differently too, since
an influencer angle inherits that individual's reputation, good and bad.

---

### 5. Gifting
> **"Because someone you love needs it"**

Positions the product as a perfect gift for someone else. Leverages the buyer's desire to please
others and be seen as thoughtful.

**When to use:** during holiday seasons or special occasions · for products with gifting potential ·
**when targeting gift-givers rather than end-users** · for premium products that make impressive
gifts

**Psychological triggers:** altruism · social signaling · relationship reinforcement

**Examples:**
- `[PRODUCT]`: "The gift they'll actually use every day (unlike last year's present)"
- `[SUBSCRIPTION]`: "Give the gift of [benefit] that keeps giving all year round"
- `[EXPERIENCE]`: "Create unforgettable memories for someone special with our [product]"

⚠️ The buyer and the user are different people here, so the pain point that converts belongs to the
giver, not the recipient.

---

### 6. New Release
> **"Because it's new"**

Emphasises novelty, innovation, or being first. Appeals to early adopters and those who want to
stay ahead of trends.

**When to use:** for product launches or feature updates · when targeting innovators and early
adopters · when your product represents a genuine innovation · to create renewed interest

**Psychological triggers:** novelty seeking · status from being first · curiosity about new
solutions

**Examples:**
- `[PRODUCT]`: "Introducing the first-ever [product] that [unique benefit]"
- `[TECH]`: "Just released: The next generation [product] with [new feature]"
- `[SERVICE]`: "Be among the first to experience our revolutionary approach to [problem]"

---

### 7. Emotional Benefit
> **"Because it'll make you feel better"**

Focuses on the emotional transformation or relief the product provides. Speaks directly to feelings
rather than function.

**When to use:** for products that address emotional needs · when targeting emotionally-driven
buyers · for lifestyle and personal development offerings

**Psychological triggers:** desire for emotional states (joy, relief, confidence) · pain avoidance ·
identity reinforcement

**Examples:**
- `[FASHION]`: "Walk into any room with the confidence that comes from knowing you look your best"
- `[WELLNESS]`: "Finally experience the calm and peace you've been searching for"
- `[HOME]`: "The joy of coming home to a space that feels truly yours"

---

### 8. Functional Benefit
> **"Because it will make this easier"**

Emphasises practical value, efficiency, and problem-solving. Focuses on how the product makes life
easier.

**When to use:** for utility-focused products that save time or effort · when targeting practical,
solution-oriented buyers · for products with clear functional advantages

**Psychological triggers:** desire for ease and convenience · problem-solution alignment ·
practical value perception

**Examples:**
- `[TOOL]`: "Complete your [task] in half the time with none of the frustration"
- `[SOFTWARE]`: "Automate the 3 most time-consuming parts of your [process]"
- `[APPLIANCE]`: "The smart solution that handles [task] while you focus on what matters"

⚠️ Emotional and Functional Benefit are the pair most often confused. The test: does the promise
name a **feeling** or a **task**? Most products can run both, and running both as separate ads is
usually more informative than trying to blend them.

---

### 9. Comparison
> **"Because it's better than our competitors"**

Directly or indirectly contrasts your offering with the alternatives. Highlights competitive
advantages and unique selling points.

**When to use:** in competitive markets with clear alternatives · when you have specific advantages
over competitors · when targeting customers with competing options · for differentiation in
saturated markets

**Psychological triggers:** contrast effect · loss aversion · maximization tendency

**Examples:**
- `[PRODUCT]`: "Why our [product] outperforms leading brands in all 5 key performance tests"
- `[SERVICE]`: "The only [service] that guarantees [benefit] without [common competitor limitation]"
- `[SOFTWARE]`: "What our customers say after switching from [competitor]"

---

### 10. Seasonality
> **"Because now is the time of year you need this"**

Connects to seasonal needs, timely relevance, or calendar-based urgency. Creates time-specific
relevance.

**When to use:** for products with seasonal utility · during specific times of year (holidays,
seasons, events) · when targeting seasonal pain points or desires · for creating calendar-based
urgency

**Psychological triggers:** timeliness · preparedness · cultural and seasonal associations

**Examples:**
- `[SUMMER PRODUCT]`: "Be ready when the temperatures rise with the only [product] that [summer benefit]"
- `[HOLIDAY]`: "Make this [holiday] season memorable with our exclusive [offering]"
- `[SEASONAL CHALLENGE]`: "The solution to [seasonal problem] that works even in the harshest [season]"

⚠️ Distinct from Promotion, though they often ship together. Seasonality says the *need* is now;
Promotion says the *deal* is now. A seasonal angle works without a discount.

---

### Choosing an angle

The worksheet asks, for each type: **how could you apply this angle to your offer?** Working all
ten is the exercise, and it is worth doing even for the angles that seem wrong, because the
forced attempt often surfaces a claim the obvious angles miss.

For choosing between them:

- **What does the evidence support?** Social Proof without numbers and Comparison without a real
  advantage are claims the audience can check.
- **What is the awareness stage?** Education and Emotional Benefit reach earlier stages; Promotion
  and Comparison assume the person already knows what the product is.
- **What has the account already run?** Angle exhaustion is real. If every ad for six months has
  been Functional Benefit, the untested angles are where the upside is, regardless of which
  performs best in theory.

---

### The Multi-Angle Strategy Worksheet

The worksheet does not stop at the ten. It closes with a section that turns ten filled-in options
into a plan, and it is the half that makes the exercise worth doing.

**Your top 3 most promising angles** — based on product, audience and objectives, which three, and
**why**? The "why" column is the section. Three angles named without reasons is a shortlist; three
with reasons is an argument someone can disagree with.

**Audience-angle matching** — different segments respond to different angles. Map them:

| Audience segment | Primary angle | Secondary angle |
|---|---|---|
| | | |

This is the part most often skipped and it is where the leverage is. The same offer aimed at two
segments frequently wants two different angles, and running one angle at everyone is how an account
plateaus.

**Angle combinations** — sometimes combining creates stronger messaging. List two or three:

> `1. [angle] + [angle] = `
> `2. [angle] + [angle] = `
> `3. [angle] + [angle] = `

⚠️ **This sits in tension with "one angle per ad", and both are right.** A combination is one
angle carrying a second as support — Social Proof + Promotion is a promotion made credible, not two
competing arguments. If both angles want to be the headline, it is not a combination, it is a
cluttered ad.

**Angle testing plan** — how will you test to identify the most effective? Sequence and a way to
read the result, not just a list.

**Final notes & insights** — anything else about angle strategy for this specific product.

### The Building Blocks Analysis mega-prompt

A separate one-page asset, designed to be pasted whole into any AI assistant. It teaches the
framework first, then asks for the analysis, which is why it is much longer than the others.

ACA's usage note:

> Copy this entire prompt and paste it into your AI assistant (like ChatGPT or Claude). Then add
> your ad description at the end where indicated. The prompt first teaches the AI the ad building
> blocks framework, then guides it to help analyze your specific ad.

```
I need your help analyzing an ad using the "Ad Building Blocks Framework" - a modular approach
to understanding creative structure. First, I'll teach you the framework, then ask you to
analyze a specific ad.

THE AD BUILDING BLOCKS FRAMEWORK

Effective ads follow a three-part structure (Hook-Body-CTA) and use modular building blocks
from three categories:

STRUCTURE BLOCKS - these form the backbone of any ad:
1. Hook (0-3 seconds): The attention-grabbing opener that stops the scroll. Effective hooks use
   pattern interrupts, curiosity gaps, bold claims, or relatable problems.
2. Body: The main content that builds desire and addresses objections. This typically includes
   product demonstration, benefits explanation, and credibility elements.
3. CTA (Call to Action): The closing segment that drives specific action, often with urgency or
   special offers.

PRODUCT BLOCKS - these showcase the offering:
1. Product Introduction: How the product is first revealed (direct, problem-then-solution,
   benefit-first, etc.)
2. Demonstration: Showing the product in action (how-to usage, before/after, comparison, etc.)
3. Features/Benefits: Highlighting key selling points (feature→advantage→benefit structure)
4. Buying Experience: Making the purchase process clear (pricing, shipping, guarantees)
5. Unboxing: Creating anticipation and trust through packaging reveals

PERSON BLOCKS - these create emotional connection:
1. Problem Statement: Articulating the pain point (personal story, common frustration, scenario)
2. Failed Alternative: What didn't work before this solution (prior solutions, competing
   products)
3. Desired Result: The transformation promised (emotional, physical, lifestyle improvement)
4. Before & After: Visual proof of change (side-by-side, sequential, timeline)
5. Social Proof: Testimonials and validation (customer reviews, user counts, expert
   endorsements)
6. Storytelling: Narrative that ties everything together (journey, origin story, day-in-life)

ANALYSIS INSTRUCTIONS

Based on this framework, please analyze the ad I'll describe. For your analysis:
1. Identify the Hook-Body-CTA structure with approximate timing
2. List all Product and Person blocks used, with timestamps if possible
3. Evaluate how effectively each block was executed (1-10 scale)
4. Identify which blocks work particularly well together
5. Note any missing blocks that might have strengthened the ad
6. Determine the primary awareness stage targeted (Unaware, Problem Aware, Solution Aware,
   Product Aware, Most Aware)
7. Identify the dominant emotional driver
8. Summarize the core building block sequence that makes this ad work
9. Suggest 2-3 ways the ad could be improved using the building blocks approach

AD FOR ANALYSIS

[DESCRIBE THE AD HERE IN DETAIL. Include the product/service, format (video, image, etc.),
length, what happens in the ad, key messaging, and any other relevant details. For video ads,
try to describe the content chronologically with approximate timestamps. The more detail you
provide, the more thorough the analysis will be.]
```

### Strategic workflows for creative strategists

Three workflows that chain the prompts into a repeatable cycle.

⚠️ In ACA's pack this section is stamped **Course 704: AI Powered Iterations & Creative
Improvement**, not Course 103, though it ships inside the Course 103 prompt pack. Included here
because it is what the pack contains and because it is the practical assembly of these prompts.

#### Workflow 1 — Competitor Analysis Cycle

1. **Collect competitor creatives.** Use ad intelligence tools (Facebook Ad Library, TikTok
   Creative Center). Identify top performers by engagement metrics.
2. **Reverse engineer with AI.** Run the Ad Structure Breakdown prompt on each, then the Building
   Block Identification prompt for deeper deconstruction.
3. **Create a competitor block database.** Organise findings in a spreadsheet: hooks by type and
   emotional appeal, body blocks by demonstration style and objection handling, CTAs by urgency
   mechanism and value proposition.
4. **Pattern identification.** Look for commonalities among top performers. Identify gaps or
   approaches competitors are not using.
5. **Concept development.** Use the Building Block Mixer to create concepts that leverage
   successful patterns and fill the gaps.

#### Workflow 2 — Performance Diagnosis Cycle

⚠️ ACA titles this "Competitor Analysis Cycle" as well, which appears to be a copy-paste error:
its content is performance diagnosis, not competitor analysis. Retitled here for what it does.

1. **Performance breakdown.** Segment performance data by ad element (hook retention, mid-video
   engagement, CTA clickthrough). Identify drop-off points in the viewer journey.
2. **Block-level diagnosis.** Use the Block Performance Diagnosis prompt. Determine whether issues
   are with the Hook (attention), the Body (desire) or the CTA (action).
3. **Targeted block testing.** Use the A/B Test Designer to create controlled variables. Test one
   block variation at a time to isolate impact.
4. **Performance learning database.** Document which block variations performed best for specific
   audiences (demographics, interests), platforms (Meta, TikTok, YouTube) and objectives
   (awareness, consideration, conversion).
5. **Scale winning block combinations.** Recombine highest performers into new variations.
   Gradually replace underperforming blocks while retaining top performers.

#### Workflow 3 — New Campaign Development

1. **Awareness stage mapping.** Determine the audience's awareness level and select block types
   appropriate to it.
2. **Block library audit.** Review existing blocks. Identify gaps needing new development.
3. **Block generation.** Use the Hook Generator for multiple hook options. Create product and
   person blocks aligned to campaign objectives.
4. **Modular ad assembly.** Mix and match blocks into complete concepts.

The three workflows map onto the three states a strategist is usually in: starting from nothing
(3), fixing something live (2), or learning from the market (1).

## The language of creative strategy

Fifteen terms. The reason to be precise about them is that most disagreements in creative reviews
are vocabulary problems wearing a disguise: two people arguing about "the concept" while one means
the strategic idea and the other means the script.

### Core strategy elements

The strategic foundation of any creative.

#### 1. Offer — the WHAT

What you are selling. Not merely the product, but **how it is packaged and presented**.

An offer is not just a discount, though a discount can be part of one. It is the complete value
proposition: a physical product, a subscription, a service, a lead magnet.

> "Understanding the offer is crucial because it determines every other aspect of your creative
> strategy."

#### 2. Angle — the WHY

The reason someone should buy **right now**. The big idea that captures interest and inspires
action.

> "When analyzing an ad, always identify the primary angle. Is it emphasizing scarcity?
> Educational value? Emotional transformation? This insight reveals what's driving conversions."

`angle-types.md` holds all ten types in full.

#### 3. Target audience / persona — the WHO

Who the creative speaks to. Platforms handle technical targeting; **your creative must visually and
verbally speak to a specific persona**.

An effective definition goes beyond demographics into psychographic traits, behavioural patterns
and specific pain points.

#### 4. Concept — the strategic intersection

The combination of offer, angle and audience. The core idea that drives a **set** of ads.

| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| Offer | WHAT are we selling? |
| Angle | WHY should they care? |
| Audience | WHO are we talking to? |

> "Each concept typically spawns multiple ads that test different executions of the same strategic
> idea."

This is the term most often used loosely. A concept is not an ad. If two "concepts" share offer,
angle and audience, they are one concept with two executions, and testing them tells you about
execution, not strategy.

### Execution elements

#### 5. Ad — the creative execution

The specific execution of a concept, the actual piece the audience sees. Defined by its platform,
the creative formula it follows, and its execution approach.

#### 6. Iteration — concept variations

A variation of a concept adapted to a new format or style. Ranges from minor adjustments to
complete reimaginations of the same core idea.

Types:
- **Minor variations** — changing the hook visual while keeping the same script
- **Format adaptations** — converting a video concept into a static image
- **Execution style changes** — turning a studio shoot into a UGC-style video

> "Systematic iteration is one of the most powerful tools in creative strategy."

#### 7. Format / asset — size and placement variations

Size and placement variants of an ad: feed (1:1), Stories and Reels (9:16), in-stream (16:9).

> "Each ad should be created in multiple formats to maximize reach and performance across different
> placements."

### Critical ad elements

#### 8. Hook — the scroll-stopper

The first **3 seconds**. The moment that decides whether someone stops.

> "It's consistently the most important element of any winning creative. Without an effective hook,
> your message never gets a chance to be heard."

#### 9. Script structure — the building blocks sequence

How the ad's content is organised. See `building-blocks.md`.

#### 10. Visuals — the show-don't-tell elements

Everything visual: shot types, transitions, text overlays.

> "In social advertising, visuals often communicate more powerfully than words."

#### 11. Supers / text overlays

Copy placed over the visuals. Titles or closed captions.

#### 12. Pacing — the rhythm of information

How quickly information is presented.

> "The rule of thumb is new information or visual changes approximately every 1.5 seconds."

A concrete, checkable standard. An ad that holds one frame for six seconds has a pacing problem
regardless of how good the copy is.

#### 13. Congruency — the seamless experience

Consistency between the ad and the post-click experience. The messaging, visuals and promises in
the ad must align with what users find after clicking.

Congruency failures show up as good CTR with bad conversion, which is easy to misdiagnose as a
landing-page problem when it is really a mismatch.

#### 14. Funnel — the post-click journey

The series of pages users experience after clicking.

> "Your ad's job changes based on the funnel type and complexity."

A long funnel lets the ad do less; a direct-to-cart funnel makes the ad carry the whole argument.

#### 15. Conversion objective — the ultimate goal

The specific action you want taken: completing a purchase, submitting a lead form, and so on.

### Why the precision pays

Using these terms consistently makes three things possible that vague language does not:

- **Diagnosing performance to a level.** "The concept is wrong" and "the hook is wrong" are
  different problems with different fixes, and only one of them means rebuilding.
- **Briefing without ambiguity.** A brief naming offer, angle, audience, and required formats
  cannot be misread the way "make it feel premium" can.
- **Testing something specific.** If you cannot name which element changed between two ads, the
  test result cannot teach you anything transferable.
