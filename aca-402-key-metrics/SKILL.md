## What this skill needs from you

**Required: none, to explain any of it.** Ask what thumbstop is, what CTR (Link Click) means, or why
a high CTR can be bad news, and get an answer.

**To read an account or diagnose an ad, required:**

1. **The actual numbers**, per ad or per creative. At minimum impressions and 3-second views for
   thumbstop, link clicks for CTR
2. **What the ads were** — format, hook, angle. A row of numbers with no creative attached cannot be
   diagnosed, only described

**Optional, and each one sharpens the read:**

3. Spend, purchases, revenue — for CPA, ROAS and CVR
4. 10-second views, for hold rate
5. Platform and placement, because the bands are not portable across platforms
6. The account's own history, which is a better benchmark than anything in this course
7. What the landing page does, when CTR is strong and conversion is not

⛔ **Never invent a metric value, and never fill an empty column with a plausible one.** A read-out
is a document someone forwards to a media buyer. A number that was estimated and not labelled as
estimated becomes a number somebody plans against. If it was not supplied, write `not supplied`.

⛔ **Never present this course's bands as industry benchmarks.** They come from one advertiser in
one category, and the course says so. The honest form is `Chloe Rhys reports X for Jack Archer`,
not `a good CTR is X`.

## How to run it

**Define, then read, then decide.** Reading numbers before defining them is how a confident wrong conclusion
gets into a client email.

1. **Ask what they actually have** — exported data, one ad's numbers, or a client question. The route differs.
2. ⛔ **Define the metrics before reading them**, from **The metric dictionary** (below). ⚠️ **Hold rate especially:
   Course 701 uses a different denominator, and the two numbers are not comparable.**
3. **Separate what creative owns from what it does not**, same file. This is what keeps the read-out honest
   about attribution.
4. **Read thumbstop** with **Thumbstop** (below) and **CTR** with **Click-through rate** (below). ⚠️ Say which of the
   five Ads Manager CTRs is meant; they are different numbers.
5. ⭐ **Produce the read-out and the insights log** from **Producing the read-out and the insights log** (below). Two artifacts, emitted as
   tables that paste into a sheet.
6. **End on what to change next**, which is the only output that makes the analysis worth doing.
7. **Check the read-out says what creative did not cause.** A read-out that claims everything is a read-out
   nobody should act on.

## Answering well

**Separate what creative owns from what it does not.** Chloe's central argument:

> "Conversion metrics like CPA or CVR are affected by lots of things: the landing page, the price,
> inventory or availability, your shipping or checkout process. But engagement metrics like
> thumbstop and CTR? **That's all you. That's your script. Your image. Your format. Your hook.**"

⚠️ **And the counterweight, in her words:** *"Not every number is a creative problem. Sometimes ads
underperform because of targeting, seasonality, product-market fit, or campaign structure."*

**Stopping the wrong person is not a win.** *"It's easy to make someone stop. It's harder to make
the right person stop."* A shocking visual that spikes thumbstop and converts nobody is *"noise, not
signal"*.

**High CTR is not automatically good.** ⭐ Her most useful finding, and it is counterintuitive: at
Jack Archer *"some of our highest CTR ads had some of the lowest conversion rates"* and *"some of our
lowest CTR ads drove the highest ROAS"*. **CTR is directional, not definitive.**

**Metrics make the strategist credible, not just correct.** The course's practical argument is that
reading the numbers is what lets you defend creative decisions to media teams, leadership and
clients. *"If you're the person in the room who can both come up with the idea and interpret the
results, you have a major advantage."*

### ⛔ Where ACA's own courses disagree, and none of them say so

**This is the most important thing in this file.** Course 402 is the only ACA course that attaches
thresholds to metrics *and* names the advertiser they came from. That makes it the folder's
benchmark reference, and it also puts it in direct conflict with two other courses.

#### 1. CTR: this course and Course 210 cannot both be right about the same ad

| | The number | The instruction attached |
|---|---|---|
| **Course 402**, Chloe Rhys | **0.9% to 1.3% is the sweet spot.** Over 1.4%, *"pause and ask, is this traffic qualified?"* | Treat a very high CTR as a warning |
| **Course 210**, TikTok | **Above 2% for ecommerce.** Below it, *"your CTA or landing page mismatches the promise"* | Treat anything under 2% as broken |

⛔ **An ad at 1.1% CTR is a healthy sweet-spot ad by 402 and a failing ad by 210.** Course 210's floor
sits above the point where 402 starts worrying about click quality.

**What resolves it, and what does not.** Different platforms (Meta link clicks out to a store against
TikTok), different advertisers, and near-certainly **different CTR definitions** — 402 spends a whole
section establishing that Ads Manager holds five distinct CTRs and that only CTR (Link Click) should
be used, while 210 names no variant at all. **Neither course mentions the other, and both figures are
unsourced.**

⭐ **So the usable rule is: never carry a CTR band across platforms, and always say which CTR.** Quote
each course's number only alongside its platform and its advertiser.

#### 2. Thumbstop: a threshold in Course 210 that cannot be reconciled or dismissed

Course 210 gives `3-second hold rate, ≥ 70%` with the note *"if lower, your hook is ice-cold"*.
**It gives no formula for it.**

- If that metric is 3-second views ÷ impressions — the thumbstop this course defines — then 70% as a
  health floor is impossible against 402's *"50% or higher is very rare and often means you've struck
  gold"*.
- If it means something else, such as how many of those who started were still watching at 3 seconds,
  then the two are different measurements and neither number applies to the other.

⛔ **The material does not settle which.** ⭐ **The finding is the ambiguity itself: a metric name with
a hard threshold and no formula cannot be acted on**, and this course is the one that supplies the
formulas. Ask which numerator and denominator before applying anybody's 70%.

#### 3. Course 302 deliberately gives no thresholds, and this one does

Course 302's account audit names no thresholds for any of these metrics, and asks you to set

⚠️ **That is not a contradiction of fact, and the prohibition is still right as written.** 302 really
does not give numbers, so quoting one as 302's would be an invention. **What changes is that ACA does

### The five outcome metrics

Each with ACA's formula and ACA's worked example, unchanged.

| Metric | Formula | Chloe's worked example |
|---|---|---|
| **CPA** — Cost Per Action | Spend ÷ Conversions | $200 spend, 10 purchases → **$20 CPA** |
| **ROAS** — Return on Ad Spend | Revenue ÷ Spend | $600 revenue on $200 spend → **3.0 ROAS**. *"For every $1 you spent, you made $3"* |
| **CTR** — Click-Through Rate | Link Clicks ÷ Impressions | 100,000 impressions, 2,000 clicks → **2% CTR** |
| **CVR** — Conversion Rate | Conversions ÷ Clicks | 2,000 clicks, 100 purchases → **5% CVR** |
| **CPM** — Cost Per Thousand Impressions | Spend ÷ (Impressions ÷ 1,000) | $500 spend, 100,000 impressions → **$5 CPM** |

**What she says about each, beyond the formula:**

- **CPA** *"is the end result of everything — creative, targeting, landing page, pricing, offer."*
  The primary indicator that an ad drove sales, and the one most contaminated by things you do not
  control.
- **ROAS** *"in ecommerce, this is gold. But again, like CPA it's influenced by a lot more than just
  creative."*
- **CTR** *"is a great proxy for curiosity and interest. CTR is directly tied to your copy and your
  hook."*
- **CVR** *"is influenced by your landing page, storefront, checkout experience. If your ad is great
  but your product page is confusing, or there's a bug at checkout, the CVR will drop."*
- **CPM** ⚠️ *"Low CPM can be a good thing. But sometimes a low CPM can also mean bringing in lower
  quality traffic, that might be more difficult to convert."* A cheap impression is not a good one.

⚠️ **Her key point, stated as one:** *"Not every number is a creative problem. Sometimes ads
underperform because of targeting, seasonality, product-market fit, or campaign structure. But
knowing the numbers can help you ask the right questions, and defend your creative decisions with
real data."*

### The two engagement metrics

| Metric | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Thumbstop**, also called **Hook Rate** | 3-second views ÷ Impressions |
| **CTR** | Link Clicks ÷ Impressions |

> "Why 3 seconds? Because that's the minimum amount of time someone has to watch for it to count as
> a 'view' in Meta's eyes. And that moment, those first few seconds, that's your hook."

⭐ **On TikTok it is 2 seconds, not 3.** *"On TikTok, hook rate is only 2 seconds."* The same named
metric is computed from a different threshold on each platform, so a thumbstop figure without a
platform attached is not comparable to anything.

**Also worth watching, named but not defined in depth:**

| Metric | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Hold rate** | 10-second views ÷ 3-second views |
| **Saves and shares** | On TikTok and Instagram Reels |
| **Outbound clicks** | Especially when sending to a blog, quiz or lead gen |

⛔ **Hold rate's denominator is 3-second views, not impressions.** It measures whether the people who
stopped kept watching, so it is a body-of-the-ad metric, not a hook metric. Mixing it with thumbstop
is the most common arithmetic error in reading these two.

## Thumbstop

> "Thumbstop rate, also known as Hook Rate, is the percentage of people who pause to watch your ad
> for at least three seconds."

**3-second video views ÷ impressions.** So 100 people see the ad, 35 pause for 3 seconds, thumbstop
is 35%. ⭐ **On TikTok it is 2-second views**, and the same name means a different calculation there.

### ⭐ Not all thumbstops are created equal

> "It's easy to make someone stop. **It's harder to make the right person stop.** A chaotic or
> shocking visual might spike thumbstop, but if those viewers don't go on to watch more, click, or
> convert, it's **noise, not signal**."

> "Our job isn't to just grab attention. It's to **earn the right to tell the rest of the story.**"

**Thumbstop is targeting.** *"In the pre-iOS14 world, we relied on data signals to serve the right
ad to the right person. Now, creative is the targeting."* When someone pauses for 3 seconds, Meta
reads that as *"this person found this interesting"* and starts building a profile of people like
them.

> "The stronger your thumbstop, the faster and more efficiently your creative can train the
> algorithm."

⚠️ **Which is why a high thumbstop that leads nowhere is worse than a low one.** *"A high thumbstop
that doesn't lead to clicks or purchases isn't just ineffective, it's misleading."* It trains the
algorithm on the wrong people.

> "The key question isn't just: did they stop scrolling? It's: **did the creative stop the right
> people, for the right reasons, at the right time?**"

### The bands

| Thumbstop | Chloe's reading |
|---|---|
| **Below 20%**, consistently | *"Time to iterate and revisit your hook approach"* |
| **25-30% and up** | *"Generally considered solid"* |
| **40%+** | *"Very strong"* |
| **50% or higher** | *"Very rare and often means you've struck gold"* |

⛔ **Do not present these as industry benchmarks.** They are ACA's, given without a source or a
vertical, and another ACA course gives a number for a similarly named metric that cannot be
reconciled with these. See **Provenance** (below).

⚠️ **Modest thumbstop is not automatically a failure.** *"You'll sometimes have ads with a modest
thumbstop but excellent conversion rates. That's usually because they stop fewer people, but the
ones they do stop are very qualified."*

⚠️ **And the reverse.** *"Other times, you'll have ads with incredible thumbstop and CTR, but they
never convert. That's often a sign that the hook was strong, but the angle didn't attract the right
audience. Or worse, it misled them."*

> "📌 **Thumbstop is not a number. It's a signal.** It tells us what's grabbing attention, and more
> importantly, **who** it's grabbing."

### The anatomy of a thumbstop: three layers

> "We're talking about a moment, one charged second when a person decides whether to lean in or keep
> scrolling. And that decision is influenced by three elements that work together: **Visual.
> Language. Presentation.**"

#### 1. The hook visual — shot selection and imagery

**The visual should do one of two things:** deliver an emotional or sensory jolt, **or** create a
question the brain wants to answer.

| What works | Why |
|---|---|
| **Close-ups** | Skin, texture, hands, faces, motion. *"Intimacy stops people"* |
| **Incomplete actions** | A knife slicing almost through a cake. A hand reaching. *"The viewer wants to see the resolution, and that curiosity buys you seconds"* |
| **Sensory visuals** | Cream being applied, leggings being pulled, hair being brushed. *"These simulate real-life texture and help eCommerce brands overcome the 'I can't touch this' barrier"* |
| **Colour** | High contrast, bright tones, or super clean minimalism. Vibrant nail polish, powder sparkles, bold packaging |

> "Your visual doesn't need to be wild. It just needs to make someone feel something: delight,
> tension, satisfaction, curiosity."

⭐ **This is a shot list instruction, not a taste note.** *"When you're concepting or briefing, don't
just think about coverage. Think about thumbstop potential. **What is the moment someone will want
to see paused?** That's your opener, that's your hook."*

#### 2. The hook copy / header — messaging

The words on screen in the first second or two. **The header does one or both of:**

- **Frames the visual** — helps the viewer instantly understand what they are seeing
- **Creates tension or desire** — raises a question, suggests a payoff, taps an emotion

> "You're not writing a paragraph. You're writing **a billboard that disappears in 3 seconds.**"

| Header style | Chloe's example |
|---|---|
| Bold problem/solution line | *"Hate your jeans? Try these."* |
| A hooky question | *"Still using 2008 deodorant?"* |
| A POV or first-person setup | *"POV: You finally find a bra that fits."* |
| A conversational testimonial pull | *"I'll never wear wire bras again."* |

**The best ones are: specific** (not vague), **emotional** (fear, relief, pride, curiosity), and
**relatable** (*"that's me"* or *"I want that"*).

⭐ *"Great hooks often already exist in your buyer's words."* Study customer reviews and the patterns
in your own performance data.

#### 3. The presentation — layout, style and timing

| Rule | What she says |
|---|---|
| **Static headers almost always win** | *"If the header animates in, slides around, or competes with motion in the visual, you're splitting the viewer's attention"* |
| **Legibility is everything** | Black or red on a white box, or white on black. Familiar social-native fonts, meme-style layouts. Usually sans serif. *"Anything overly decorative, complex, or hard to read? Pass"* |
| **Limit the noise** | *"If the copy's animating, the talent is moving, and product is spinning, all at once, it becomes overwhelming. **Anchor one thing. Let the eye rest.**"* |
| **Motion should serve the story** | Gentle upward motion mimics scroll behaviour and feels native. Zoom-ins add energy. Reversals trigger curiosity. *"But all motion should feel purposeful"* |
| **Thumbnails still matter** | *"The still frame before autoplay can influence who watches."* If it includes your header and a recognisable image, you have an edge |

⭐ **On brand fonts, and it is a client-handling note as much as a design one:** *"I've been in
situations where clients request to use their specific brand font in all paid advertising. If this
is a calligraphy font, gently remind them that the style with the platform it's running on is always
optimal for performance. Social native style graphics and text will typically always outperform a
heavily branded approach in thumbstop."*

⚠️ **The thumbnail is usually not yours to set.** *"Since media buyers are typically setting the
thumbnail, feel free to reach out to them with a screenshot or recommendation."* Put it in the
hand-off or it does not happen.

**Her four pre-ship questions:**

1. Does this make sense right away to someone with **zero context**?
2. Is my hook tailored to the audience or persona I'm trying to capture?
3. Is there **one clear focal point**?
4. Would I stop to watch?

> "If you're unsure, chances are the algorithm will be too. And it won't deliver your ad the way you
> want."

### The 7 visual hook archetypes

> "seven visual hook patterns that consistently stop the scroll. These are time-tested formats used
> across categories and platforms—and they work. **You can use these patterns to select the right
> first shot for your video ad.**"

| # | Archetype | What it is, and her example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Incomplete Action — the cliffhanger shot** | Begin an action, don't finish it. *"People crave resolution."* Cutting into a dish, cut away right before the bite. Works in food, skincare, fitness, home |
| 2 | **Tactile Close-Ups** | *"Think visual ASMR."* Hands rubbing in lotion, fingers stretching fabric, a razor gliding over skin. *"If the viewer can almost feel it through the screen, you're doing it right"* |
| 3 | **Relatable Problems or Desirable Outcomes** | Emotional targeting. A tangled cord, leggings adjusted at the office; or shiny hair, calm skin. Creates recognition (*"that's so me"*) or aspiration (*"I want that"*) |
| 4 | **Split Screens — before vs after** | Transformation communicated instantly. One leg shaved, one not. One side of the surface spotless |
| 5 | **Unexpected Visuals — the pattern interrupt** | *"I also call these **WTF openers**."* A bird's-eye view, legs rotated upside down, something poured in reverse. *"Just enough confusion to create curiosity, without veering into randomness"* |
| 6 | **Motion, Time and Colour — editing tactics** | `Not about what was filmed, but how it's edited.` Push-ins, zooms, speed ramps, boomerangs, colour grading |
| 7 | **Layered Storytelling — visual + headline harmony** | The image and the line as one unit. A black scrub on a face is odd; paired with *"I had no idea how oily my skin was"* it lands |

⭐ **Her own ranking: if you nail one, nail number 7.** *"The best creative teams think of the visual
and the copy as one unit: a mini-story. **If you only nail one thing, it should be that combo of
visual and headline.**"*

**Three notes she attaches, which are the parts people skip:**

- ⭐ **On split screens (4), exaggerate the "before" deliberately.** *"If your header says 'Stop
  Wearing Sloppy Sweatpants' and you show a split screen, make sure that your shot shows the
  ugliest, baggiest sweatpants shot possible, so that your product clearly looks like an incredible
  alternative in comparison."* This is why an **exaggerated-problem shot list** is an asset worth
  keeping.
- ⚠️ **On split screens and platform policy.** *"For quite a while, it seemed that Meta had strict
  restrictions on showing before-afters when it came to the personal attributes space: weight-loss
  transformations or skincare results, resulting in ads being flagged. Be cautious of this with
  weight-loss and skincare brands. However, I have noticed in recent years they seem to have
  loosened their policies."* ⛔ **This is her observation of a policy at a moment, not the policy.**
  Check the current rules before briefing one.
- ⛔ **On pattern interrupts (5), the clickbait line.** *"These can often garner really high
  thumbstop, but watch out because **you can quickly fall into clickbait territory** if the opener
  visual has no clear connection to the product."* This is the archetype that produces the
  high-thumbstop, no-conversion ad described above.

**On colour grading, one concrete technique:** *"On before/after split screens, we often colour the
before visual to greyscale in order to make the solution stand out even more."*

### The 7 copy hook formulas

> "strong hooks tend to follow predictable copywriting formulas. And the best ones feel like they
> were written by a real person—because **often, they were.** In fact, some of the best copy I've
> used has been pulled straight from customer reviews."

| # | Formula | Structures | Her examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Problem / Solution** | *Hate That? Try This* · *Still doing That?* · *Can your [X] do this?* · *I'll never do X again* · *I've been doing it wrong my whole life* | *"Hate paying extra for pink razors?"* · *"I'll never wear a wire bra again."* |
| 2 | **Desirable Claims** | *The #1 secret to [result]* · *Experts have reinvented [category]* · *This [product] is helping [group] do [thing]* | *"The #1 secret to looking good in every dress."* · *"Psychologists have reinvented weight loss."* |
| 3 | **Reasons to Switch** | *X reasons why [persona] are switching to Y* · *Why [group] is quitting Z* · *Why everyone's buying [number] of this* | *"4 reasons why women with thick hair are switching razors"* · *"Why men are buying 5 pairs of these pants"* |
| 4 | **How-To and Informational** | *How to [result]* · *How to get X without Y* · *What to do when [problem]* | *"How to get rid of dark circles"* · *"How to get a flat stomach without giving up pasta"* |
| 5 | **Easy Trick / Hack Language** | *Easy trick to [result]* · *The lazy way to [result]* · *Hack your [problem]* | *"Easy hack to fix frizzy hair"* · *"The lazy way to whiten your teeth"* |
| 6 | **Myths, Mistakes, Misconceptions** | *Everything you know about X is wrong* · *You've been doing X wrong your whole life* · *X myths about Y* | *"Everything you know about shampoo is wrong"* · *"3 myths about plus-size fashion"* |
| 7 | **Conversational & Social Native** | *PSA to all [group]* · *My friend said I HAD to try this* · *Not me finally trying [product]* · text-bubble opens · POV headers | *"POV: you finally find a bra that doesn't dig in"* |

⛔ **Formulas 2, 4 and 5 generate claims, and several of her examples are claims a brand would have
to substantiate.** *"Psychologists have reinvented weight loss"*, *"how to get a flat stomach"*,
*"the lazy way to whiten your teeth"* are health and results claims. They are reproduced because
they are hers. **A hook written for a real brand must not invent a claim the brand has not made.**
The course does not raise this.

⭐ **Her actual instruction is to steal from reviews, not to fill in the templates.** *"When in
doubt, try stealing directly from the review section. If a customer said 'I'll never wear jeans
again after these', great, that's your headline."* And: *"Think of the formulas we covered **not as
templates, but as tools.** The best hooks sound like they came from your audience, not from a
marketing team."*

**Presentation note:** this copy is *"typically presented as an on-screen header, sometimes static,
sometimes styled like a text bubble or tweet, or even karaoke-style read-along text."*

### ⭐ Persona-callout hooks

Her own most-quoted tactic, from Jack Archer.

> "One of the most effective tactics we've discovered at Jack Archer is **calling out your target
> persona directly in the hook copy.** This means explicitly mentioning the audience you're trying
> to reach, be it their age, gender, or a specific life stage."

**Her example, and its result:** *"One of our recent top-performing hooks, coming in at **almost 50%
thumbstop**, is: 'Why do men over 30 waste money on mediocre pants?'"*

> "The specificity made the message feel personal, almost as if we were speaking directly to them."

**How to run it:**

1. **Start with specificity.** Name a demographic or persona. Either a new persona you want to reach
   or your existing most reliable customer base
2. **Test variations.** *"'Why busy millennials are switching to microwavable meals for one' could be
   adapted to 'Why seniors are switching to microwavable meals for one.'"*
3. **Monitor performance** in Ads Manager, including the demographic breakdowns

#### ⛔ The algorithm claim, and how she qualifies it

She argues the callout may also steer delivery, and she is careful about it. **Quote her hedge or do
not quote the claim:**

> "**Meta doesn't explicitly confirm** that mentioning a specific audience in your copy will
> influence targeting, the platform does use machine learning to predict which users are most likely
> to engage with your ad based on its content. So when your ad copy specifies 'men over 30', the
> algorithm **may** interpret this as a signal to prioritize delivery to users who fit that
> description, especially if initial engagement metrics are strong."

⛔ **Reported as fact, this becomes "writing your audience into the copy targets them", which is not
what she said and not something Meta documents.** The defensible half is the first one: naming the
persona makes the persona feel addressed, and that lifts engagement. The delivery effect is her
inference, and she labels it as one.

## Click-through rate

> "CTR stands for Click-Through Rate, and it's one of the most referenced metrics for creative
> strategists. And personally, I feel like it's also **one of the most misunderstood.**"

**Link Clicks ÷ Impressions.** 1,000 impressions, 10 clicks, 1% CTR.

### ⭐ There are five CTRs in Ads Manager, and only one is the answer

> "Inside Ads Manager, there are actually multiple versions of CTR, and it's important to know which
> one you're looking at."

| CTR | What it counts | Use it? |
|---|---|---|
| **CTR (All)** | *"Any kind of click"* — expanding the caption, tapping your profile picture, pausing the video, the See More button | ⛔ *"Don't use it to evaluate creative effectiveness, because not all of those clicks are meaningful."* Fine for a general sense of engagement |
| **CTR (Link Click)** | Impressions that led to a click on the actual link — Shop Now, the headline link | ⭐ **This is the one.** *"This is your go-to CTR... This is what we looked at most closely at OpenStore for Jack Archer"* |
| **Unique CTR (Link Click)** | One click per person, however many times they clicked | Useful for how many *people* clicked. ⚠️ *"It can slightly understate performance if your ad is compelling enough to get multiple clicks from the same person"* |
| **Outbound CTR** | Clicks that take someone off Meta entirely, to your own site | Useful for measuring who left the platform. *"For most e-comm campaigns, CTR (Link) and Outbound CTR will be nearly identical"* |
| **Unique Outbound CTR** | One outbound click per person | How many individual people reached your site |

⚠️ **Where Link and Outbound diverge:** *"if your ad opens a lead form first."* The click happened
on-platform, so it is a link click and not an outbound one.

⛔ **Agree the metric before you argue about the number.** *"Make sure you're aligned with other
stakeholders on which metric you'll be tracking. I generally recommend always using CTR (Link),
which is the cleanest signal of whether your creative inspired someone to take action."* Two people
comparing CTR (All) against CTR (Link) are not disagreeing, they are measuring different things.

### The bands

> "From what we've seen at OpenStore, specifically on Jack Archer, and across thousands of paid
> social ads, here's a general rule of thumb."

| CTR (Link) | Her reading |
|---|---|
| **Below 0.5%** | *"Usually a red flag. That's when you want to go back and ask: is this ad even making sense to the viewer? Are we saying anything worth clicking?"* |
| **0.6% to 0.8%** | *"Better, but still below average. It might be worth testing a stronger hook or changing the lead visual"* |
| **0.9% to 1.3%** | ⭐ *"The sweet spot we found on Jack Archer. Ads in that range consistently pulled their weight. They drew real interest without driving low-quality clicks"* |
| **Over 1.4%**, into the 2s and 3s | *"That can be super exciting. But it's also a moment to pause and ask: **is this traffic qualified?** Sometimes really high CTRs come from curiosity clicks"* |

⛔ **These are one advertiser's numbers in men's apparel and she says so.** They are not an ACA
benchmark and not an industry one, and another ACA course gives a CTR floor that sits above this
course's "start worrying" line. See **Provenance** (below) before quoting either.

⚠️ **Aggressive CTR goals are a known failure mode, from inside her own team.** *"At OpenStore,
we're often given aggressive CTR goals. And I get it. But what we learned over time is that **CTR is
only part of the story.**"*

### How CTR interacts with everything else

> "If your ad is driving tons of clicks, but those clicks aren't converting, **you're essentially
> lighting money on fire.**"

#### CPC — cost per click

**Spend ÷ Link Clicks.** $200 and 50 link clicks is a $4 CPC; another ad with 50 clicks that cost
$100 is a $2 CPC, *"twice as efficient"*.

⚠️ *"Sometimes you'll see a high CTR but also a high CPC. That might mean the ad is working, but
it's being shown to a more expensive audience."*

#### CPM — cost per thousand impressions

⚠️ *"A high CTR on a high-CPM ad might look impressive, but it can still be inefficient. Even if
your creative is performing well, if you're paying $50+ just to show the ad, it's going to be harder
to hit CPA targets."*

#### ⭐ CVR — and the finding she calls controversial

> "At OpenStore for Jack Archer, we found that **high CTR didn't always mean high CVR.** In fact,
> **some of our highest CTR ads had some of the lowest conversion rates.** Why? Because they were
> curiosity clicks. People were intrigued by the ad, but didn't end up buying."

> "We also found that **some of our lowest CTR ads drove the highest ROAS**, because the few people
> who did click were actually ready to convert."

> "That's why you'll often hear me say: **CTR is directional, not definitive.**"

⚠️ **The other explanation she gives for the same pattern:** *"Sometimes the hook is so strong, it
actually over-promises, and the landing page underwhelms."* ⛔ **That is a creative fault, not a
traffic-quality one**, and the two look identical in the numbers. Distinguishing them means reading
the ad against the page, not reading the metrics harder.

⚠️ *"Over time, that creates friction between creative and performance teams. And as a creative
strategist, you risk chasing performance signals that don't actually move the business."*

**The questions this turns into:**

- *"If CTR is high but CPA is terrible, is the landing page failing?"*
- *"If CTR is average but CVR is high, should we scale this more?"*

> "**Your job is to diagnose, not just report.**"

### ⭐ What drove CTR up at Jack Archer

> "We ran multiple deep-dive studies across thousands of Jack Archer ads and campaigns, and a few
> clear trends emerged."

| Finding | The figure | Her explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **Video beats static** | **12% higher CTR** | *"This was a really surprising learning. The traditional assumption is that it's easier to get someone to click on an image then on a video."* Motion catches attention, and they layered in voiceover, visual proof and story formats that *"primed them better for converting"* |
| **More creative variants** | **60% boost in CTR** | Running **6 to 12 variants** instead of their standard 4. *"Showing different angles to different segments helps more people find the 'in' that resonates with them"* |
| **Strong hooks** | **55% higher CTR** | Ads with **hook rates above 40%**. *"That correlation was consistent. So if your thumbstop is strong, and your hook rate is strong, CTR usually follows"* |

⛔ **All three figures are one advertiser's, uncontrolled, and reported without a sample size or a
time window.** They are the strongest-sounding numbers in the course and the easiest to misquote as
general laws. `Video gets 12% more clicks` is not what the material supports; `Chloe Rhys reports
video drove 12% higher CTR across Jack Archer's tests"* is.

⚠️ **She gives the exception to her own first finding.** *"Many DTC brands rely heavily on static
promo ads, and yes, **promos typically do drive a higher click-through rate** than many other types
of ads. Showing a large 'percent off' offer static image ad will likely get you a high click-through
rate."* The video advantage she measured is against statics that *"just showcase the product"*, not
against a discount static.

⚠️ **The third finding is a correlation and she calls it one.** Hook rate above 40% and CTR moving
together does not establish that lifting the hook lifts the CTR; both plausibly follow from the ad
being good. Her practical reading — check the hook first — survives either way.

### Applying it

**Her four takeaways, in order:**

1. ⭐ **Use CTR as a tool, not a trophy.** *"A high CTR with a low CVR? That's not a win. That's a
   leaky funnel."*
2. **Watch how CTR interacts with CPM, CPC and CVR.** *"Sometimes the creative is great, but the
   campaign targeting is off. Sometimes CTR tanks because the audience is tired of seeing the same
   concept."*
3. ⭐ **Test broadly and log the patterns.** *"When you find a high-CTR ad, don't just rerun it,
   study it."* What was the angle? What was the first line of copy? Was the voiceover doing the heavy
   lifting? Was it UGC or polished?
4. **Read it in context.** *"It's a clue, not a conclusion."*

> "At Jack Archer, we maintained **a running doc of CTR insights across quarters**, and over time we
> started predicting which creative would outperform, just based on hook + angle."

⭐ **That running doc is the artifact of this course.** **Producing the read-out and the insights log** (below) produces it.

#### Isolating what worked inside a test

> "When you're testing a group of ad variants, say 6 versions of the same core concept, but with
> different opening visuals or headline copy, **CTR can help you isolate what's working.**"

**3 headers × 3 visual openers** will produce wildly different CTRs across the combinations.

⭐ **And this is the part worth keeping:** *"Even if none of them are hitting your CPA or conversion
rate goals yet, CTR can still be a directional signal. It helps you identify what's pulling people
in. From there, you can iterate toward conversion. **You keep the parts that are hooking the viewer**
— the image that gets the pause, the line that gets the click — **but you adjust the rest of the
sequence** to better prime someone for the site experience."*

**Then check the seam between ad and page:**

- Is the value prop clear enough?
- Is the product benefit tied to what they'll see next on the landing page?
- Is there a smoother connection between the creative and the page they land on?

> "Sometimes, the issue isn't the ad. It's that **the ad and the landing page feel like two separate
> conversations.** And that disconnect can kill your conversion rate, even if the ad itself is
> strong."

### Her closing position

> "here's my hot take. CTR is a helpful metric, but it is **not the ultimate measure of creative
> success.** Yes, it's one of the few metrics that creative can truly fully own. But if your team
> starts treating CTR as the only and sole goal, that is a mistake. Just because someone clicks
> doesn't mean that they're going to buy. **Engagement** is important, but engagement **without
> conversion doesn't grow your business.**"

> "CTR is a creative strategist's flashlight. It illuminates part of the path, but not the whole
> picture."

## Producing the read-out and the insights log

> "Your job is to diagnose, not just report."

Two artifacts. **Emit them as tables that paste into a sheet**, not as prose about what a read-out
would contain.

⚠️ **Provenance, stated up front because it matters here.** ACA ships no worksheet with this course.
The **insights log's columns are Chloe's**, taken from the four questions she says to ask of every
high-CTR ad and the running doc she says her team kept. **The read-out table's layout is this
skill's**, built from her metrics, her bands and her two-line diagnostic. Say so when you hand it
over; do not present either as an ACA template.

---

### ⛔ Four rules before you fill a cell

1. **Never invent a number.** If it was not supplied, the cell reads `not supplied`. Not a
   plausible-looking estimate, not a category average.
2. **Never derive a metric you were not given the parts for.** Thumbstop needs 3-second views *and*
   impressions. Two of three is nothing.
3. **Every verdict names the band it was judged against, and the band names its advertiser.**
   `0.8% — below Jack Archer's 0.9-1.3% band` is a usable sentence. `0.8% — below benchmark` is not.
4. **The account's own baseline outranks every band in this course.** If prior performance was
   supplied, judge against that first and mention Chloe's bands second.
5. ⛔ **Never state a cause you have not been shown.** The four rules above guard the numbers; this
   one guards the two columns that are prose, and they are the ones that get quoted. *"The landing
   page is failing"* is a diagnosis of a page nobody sent you. **A cause outside the supplied data
   is a hypothesis, and it gets written as one:** *"check whether the landing page carries the same
   promise"*, not `the landing page is the problem`. Chloe's own diagnostics are phrased as
   questions and the table below keeps them that way.
