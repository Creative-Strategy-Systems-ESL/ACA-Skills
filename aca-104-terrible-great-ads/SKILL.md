# Course 104: How to Make Terrible Great Ads

George Mack (The Ad Professor), 17m 29s, 4 modules.

The premise, in his words:

> "The key to making great ads is learning how to make terrible ads and then just avoiding the
> principles behind the terrible ads and you've got great ads."

⚠️ **The four principles below are stated as instructions for making ads WORSE.** That is
deliberate and it is the technique, not a joke. Read each one as the mistake, then flip it. Losing
the inversion framing loses most of the value, because "talk to your customers" is advice everyone
nods at and ignores, while "avoid your customers, dehumanise them, keep them as far away as you
can" is recognisable as something people actually do.

He is explicit that these are his own mistakes: *"these are all the big mistakes I've made that you
can avoid."*

## What this skill needs from you

**Required: none.** The four principles are worth hearing before you have an ad to check.

**Optional, and it sharpens the answer:**

1. **The ad or concept** you want checked
2. **Your unit economics** — price, gross margin, CAC, AOV, LTV, or whichever of those you know
3. **What the ad is meant to achieve**

⚠️ **For principle 4 specifically, the numbers are not optional in practice.** If someone asks
whether they should spend more, or whether a campaign is viable, and no numbers are supplied, the
honest answer is that the question cannot be settled without them.

**Ask for them inside a full answer, not instead of one.** Run the other three principles, use
whatever figures you were given as far as they go, then name the missing one and what it would
settle. A reply that is only a question wastes the turn; a reply that invents the number is worse.
Do not model an answer on invented margins, because George's whole point is that the arithmetic is
decisive and most people skip it.

## Why inversion

Borrowed from mathematics: when an equation resists, flip it and the answer clarifies.

His example is Bezos:

> "People talk about in the next 10 years, how things are going to change and how Amazon needs to
> react to that change. But instead he likes to ask himself, well, what's not going to change in
> the next 10 years? He knows 10 years from now, no customer is going to ask for slower shipping
> speed, more expensive prices."

The motivation is the midwit meme: the person in the middle who overcomplicates.

> "in advertising, being the guy in the middle who overcomplicates things and thinks he's more
> intelligent or thinks she's more intelligent than she is, is a recipe for wasting a lot of time
> and most importantly, wasting a lot of money."

**Inversion is prospective, which is what makes it useful.** Most creative review is retrospective:
it ran, it failed, why. Inversion asks what would guarantee failure, before anything is made, and
then checks whether you are doing any of it. That is a question you can answer on a Tuesday with no
data.

**The four principles** (below) has all four in full. **The pre-ship check** (below) turns
them into something runnable.

## The four, in one line each

| To make it terrible | So instead |
|---|---|
| **1. Avoid customers.** Never speak to them. Call them "customers" so they stay a blob | Interview them, record it, and put their own words in the ad |
| **2. Avoid facial expression changes.** Never watch anyone's face | Watch faces, including your own. It is the earliest signal you have |
| **3. Give them no reason to share.** Make it purely about the individual | Design for the group chat. Sharing halves acquisition cost |
| **4. Avoid the unit economics.** Never open a spreadsheet | Obsess over the maths. It decides whether creative can help at all |

⚠️ **Check what "margin" means before using it.** George's model runs on gross margin, revenue minus the direct cost of goods. If someone quotes a figure that already has overheads, shipping or payment fees in it, the room for acquisition is smaller than the number suggests. Ask which one they mean rather than assuming the favourable reading.

George singles out the fourth: *"I think this might be the most important one because of how common
it is."*

## When to lead with which

| They are asking | Go to |
|---|---|
| Check this ad / concept before we ship | The whole check in **The pre-ship check** (below) |
| Sanity-check this concept **and** we're about to spend $X | Both, and lead with principle 4. This is the most common real shape of the request: the creative question is asked out loud, the money question is the one that decides it |
| The ad feels flat, we cannot say why | Principle 2. The face test is the fastest diagnostic in the course |
| Should we spend more? Is this viable? | **Principle 4 first.** Ask for the numbers before anything else |
| How do we lower CAC? | Principle 3 (sharing) and principle 4 (margin), in that order. Both move it more than creative does |
| Our copy sounds generic | Principle 1. The fix is source material, not more writing |
| Why did a good-looking ad fail? | Run all four. Usually 2 or 4 |

## How to work through it

**Invert first, then build.** The four principles land far harder as ways to ruin an ad than as advice,
which is the whole reason the course is shaped this way.

1. **Run the four failures** from **The four principles** (below) and let them recognise their own work
   in at least one. Recognition is what makes the rest stick.
2. **Flip each one** into what it implies. Same file, and this is the actual teaching.
3. ⛔ **Do the arithmetic before the craft**, using **The unit economics** (below). If the numbers do not
   work, better creative cannot save the campaign and everything after this is wasted effort.
4. **Apply the pre-ship check** in **The pre-ship check** (below) to something real they have made or are
   about to ship.
5. **Check they can name which of the four their last flat ad broke**, and whether they have ever opened a
   spreadsheet on the campaign. The second question is the one most people cannot answer.

## Answering well

**The arithmetic outranks the creative advice.** If someone brings a spend or viability question,
principle 4 is the answer and the other three are secondary. George's margin example is the
sharpest thing in the course: a **5-point** difference in gross margin lets one business spend
**double** on acquisition against an identical competitor. No amount of creative work matches that.

**Be concrete about faces.** Principle 2 sounds soft and is the most testable idea here. "Show it
to three people and watch their eyebrows" is a real instruction that takes ten minutes.

**Do not soften the inversions into platitudes.** "Understand your audience" is not principle 1.
Principle 1 is: you are probably not speaking to any customers at all, you are sitting in a coffee
shop imagining a blob, and the fix is to book the calls.

Where an ad passes all four, say so. The check is meant to find real problems, not to generate a
quota of them.

## Register: George's bluntness is his, not yours

George writes and speaks in a contrarian, deliberately provocative register. *"To make terrible
ads, never open a spreadsheet."* *"Dehumanize them in your head."* That works from a lectern, and
it lands very differently delivered to someone who just told you their creative director loves the
video.

**Almost everyone who runs this check is emotionally invested in the thing being checked**, and
usually so is a colleague they have to keep working with. So:

- **Quote him for the idea, then say the finding plainly in your own words.** The provocation
  belongs in the teaching, not in the verdict.
- **Separate the work from the person.** "This concept has no moment where a face changes" is a
  finding. "Your creative director is wrong" is not, and it ends the conversation you were trying
  to have.
- **Name what is genuinely working first**, when something is. Not as softening, but because a
  check that only ever produces criticism gets read as a formality and stops changing decisions.
- **Be direct about severity anyway.** Kind delivery is not the same as a hedged finding. If the
  arithmetic does not work on a $30k commitment, say that clearly and early; burying it politely
  is the worse failure.

The tone to aim for is a colleague who has seen this fail before and wants you to avoid it, which
is exactly what George is. Not a critic scoring the work.

## ⛔ One rule that applies to every principle

**Do not deliver a verdict on something you have not been given.**

The arithmetic version is obvious and stated repeatedly: no invented margins, no invented LTV. The
version that slips through is principle 2, because its output sounds like judgement rather than
data. If you have a written description of a video, you have not seen a face and neither has
anyone else. An invented "this will not move anyone" is exactly as fabricated as an invented LTV,
and harder to catch because nobody expects it to be a measurement.

**The pre-ship check** (below) covers what can and cannot honestly be assessed from a
description. The general form: **say what the material you have does support, name what would
settle the rest, and hand them a test they can run today.**

## What this course does not cover

Nothing on media buying mechanics, platform specifics, or production. It is a set of judgement
checks, not a workflow.

It is also **deliberately reductive**. George's method is to find the small number of things that
matter and ignore the rest, which is a feature for a pre-ship check and a limitation if you need a
full creative strategy. The four principles will not tell you what your angle should be or who your
audience is.

## The four principles

Each is stated as **how to make an ad terrible**, then flipped. Keep the inversion framing: it is
what makes the advice land, because the failure mode is recognisable in a way the platitude is not.

---

### Principle 1 — Avoid customers

#### The terrible version

> "Avoid customers. Don't speak to them. Don't look at them. Even better, call them customers.
> Dehumanize them in your head. Keep them as far away as you can. Sit there in your coffee shop
> thinking about how you're going to manipulate this blob of customers out there that you're trying
> to get to buy your product. They're just this universal blob that you don't really know that much
> about."

George on how often he has done it himself:

> "One of the biggest mistakes I've made and have made again and again and again is avoiding
> speaking to the customers who are actually buying the product."

#### Inverted

Speak to them as much as you can, and **retire the word "customer"**:

> "They're not a customer. They're a father. They're a mother. They live in New York. They live in
> London. They have specific personalities. They are a human being. And the more you get into how
> they think, the easier it's going to be to advertise to them."

#### How, concretely

**1. One-on-one interviews.** Arrange as many as you can, **record them**, and look for key bits of
language. Study how they speak, then use how they speak in the ads themselves.

**2. The data version, at scale.** If you have history, take the comment section and all the
reviews, scrape the lot, and work with an AI to extract:
- What keywords do my customers use?
- What are the actual pain points they talk about?

Then put that language back into the advertising.

**3. Subreddits and forums**, for specific pain points in the language people actually use.

#### Why it works

> "Then all of a sudden when the person sees the advert, it's talking to them rather than some
> person far, far, far away trying to get them to buy something."

And the rule of thumb underneath it:

> "The best ads don't look like ads and the best sales people don't look like sales people."

#### The writing test

George borrows from Dwarkesh Patel, on writing but applied to ads:

> "What are you trying to say here? Then you answer that question and then you just say that."

His gloss: if you were talking to an actual human being about the product, what is being said? Use
that language and let it write itself.

> "You don't have to sit there trying to come up with something unique and salesy."

⚠️ **This is not "understand your audience".** That is a platitude everyone agrees with and skips.
The claim is sharper: you are probably speaking to nobody, and the fix is booking the calls and
recording them. If someone cannot name the last customer they spoke to, that is the finding.

---

### Principle 2 — Avoid facial expression changes

#### The terrible version

Never look at anyone's face while they see your ad. Never notice your own.

#### The reasoning

> "If you study human beings, language itself has actually not been around that long. Thing that's
> been around much longer than language is our facial expressions. **Your face doesn't lie.**"

Then the chain that makes it matter:

> "Emotion is the liquid energy that gets somebody from where they are scrolling on a newsfeed or
> seeing a billboard to actually taking action. If there's no emotion, just the advertising laws of
> physics conclude that person will not take action."

> "**The upstream from emotion is the facial expression muscles.**"

So the face is not evidence of emotion arriving later. It is the first observable event in the
chain, which is why it is available before you have any performance data.

#### Inverted, and how to run it

> "you want to study facial expressions like you've got a PhD in it. Whenever you hand somebody an
> ad, look. Whenever you see an ad yourself, study it in yourself. It is the gut signal, the
> strongest signal that an advert is going to work that you can know before you run MOD"

Two tests:

- **On other people.** Show the ad and watch. A change in the facial muscles is a good sign.
- **On yourself.** When the idea arrives, do you smile, frown, or raise your eyebrows? Those are
  the signals.

> "Once you see this principle, you can't see it. Notice every advert that you end up enjoying or
> buying from, you will have some kind of facial expression muscle change."

#### The compression test

From George's friend Sean: great advertising can be compressed down to a few well-known sounds or
acronyms.

> "it could be, yes, it could be, LFG, it could be, ouch, anything that compresses it all the way
> down. **Those sounds are basically just representations of our facial expression muscles
> changing.**"

So design with one of those in mind: which sound is this ad trying to produce?

> "And if you do that, you've massively simplified what you're trying to achieve. And remember,
> without their facial expression muscles changing, there is no emotion. And without emotion,
> there's no liquid energy to go out and actually buy the product."

⚠️ **This is the most testable idea in the course and the one most likely to be dismissed as soft.**
Showing an ad to three people and watching their faces takes ten minutes and produces a signal you
otherwise wait days of spend for.

---

### Principle 3 — Give them no reason to share

#### The terrible version

> "Under no circumstance, encourage them to share the advert with anybody. Make sure it's purely
> about that individual. There's no call to action to share. There's no reason to share. You put no
> thought into why they would share."

#### The arithmetic

This is what makes it a numbers argument rather than a nice-to-have:

> "Let's say it costs me one dollar to get somebody to click on this advert. However, let's say in
> another scenario, it costs me one dollar, but on average, each time somebody sees it, they share
> it with one person. **All of a sudden, I've dropped my acquisition click targets in half** just
> by baking in that factor."

And it is not only cheaper:

> "That person is coming at it from an organic word of mouth lens. They haven't seen it as an
> advert. They've seen it as their friend sharing it with them. **So not only is it cheaper, it's
> way more effective.**"

George invokes the K factor, familiar from COVID: the higher you push it, the larger the reduction
in ad spend.

#### Dark social

⚠️ **The recording says "Adam Asori"**; the head of Instagram is **Adam Mosseri**. Quoted as rendered.

> "Adam Asori, who's the head of Instagram, said the following that most content consumed on
> Instagram comes via sharing, comes via DMs. It's all going on in direct messages and group chats."

> "But we don't see that as advertisers. You just see the advert go live... you don't see the dark
> social that exists and it's everywhere. And it's obvious when you look at your own behavioral
> patterns that that's where you consume a lot of content and most importantly, **where you trust a
> lot of content**."

#### Inverted, and how to design for it

> "Design your adverts with the group chat first. So you want to focus on word of mouth, but most
> importantly, **the word of WhatsApp, the word of iMessage, the word of Slack, the word of
> Instagram DMs**."

The questions to ask about the person from principle 1:

- Who is their brother? Their mother? Their father? Their husband or wife?
- Who is their boss? Their colleagues?
- **What is the angle they would share it for?**

For B2B specifically:

> "What is the person who views this or who is the person that views this and how are they going to
> share this advert across their channels? Why are they going to share it?"

⚠️ **A share button is not a share reason.** The question is what the sharer gets: looking
informed, looking funny, being useful to someone, having their own view confirmed. If there is no
answer, adding a prompt to share will not create one.

---

### Principle 4 — Avoid the unit economics

George's own ranking:

> "I think this might be the most important one because of how common it is."

#### The terrible version

> "**To make terrible ads, never open a spreadsheet.** Don't factor in, okay, how much is this going
> to cost per click? What's the CPA? What's the average order value? What's the lifetime value? Is
> that actually sustainable? Who am I competing against in these ad options?... Just avoid all that
> and just focus on all the creative stuff."

He notes what you are actually up against in the auction:

> "Maybe there's a customer that can pay 10 times the price of me that I'm competing against."

#### Inverted

> "Don't avoid the mathematics. **Obsess over them.** You should know inside out before you start
> running advertising campaigns, does my business make sense? Does the advertising side of my
> business make sense? Does the LTV justifies the customer acquisition costs?"

The full argument, including the margin model, the art/science/maths split and the factory map, is
in `the-unit-economics.md`, because it is the part most likely to actually change a decision.

## The pre-ship check

The four principles as something runnable against a real ad or concept. George does not present it
as a checklist; this is his method arranged so it can be applied in ten minutes.

**Run it before spend, not after.** The whole appeal of inversion is that it works prospectively.
Every question below is answerable without any performance data, which is exactly when the answers
are cheapest to act on.

---

### 1. Customer language

**Ask:** where did the words in this ad come from?

- [ ] Can you name the last customer you spoke to about this product?
- [ ] Was any of it recorded, and did you mine it for language?
- [ ] Do any phrases in this ad come from a review, a call, a comment or a forum post?
- [ ] Have you scraped reviews or comments and pulled out the recurring pain points and keywords?
- [ ] Does the ad use the word "customer", or any other language a real person would not use?

**Fails when:** the copy is written from the brand's vocabulary. Symptoms are feature-first phrasing,
category language nobody says aloud, and lines that could belong to any competitor.

**The fix is source material, not more writing.** Another drafting pass on invented language
produces better-sounding invented language.

> "The best ads don't look like ads and the best sales people don't look like sales people."

**Quick test** (Dwarkesh Patel, via George): what are you actually trying to say here? Now just say
that. If the plain answer is better than the ad, use the plain answer.

---

### 2. The face test

**Ask:** does anything happen to a face?

- [ ] Show it to three people. Do their facial muscles change at any point?
- [ ] When you first had the idea, did *you* smile, frown or raise your eyebrows?
- [ ] Can you name the sound this ad is trying to produce? *Ooh. Yes. LFG. Ouch.*
- [ ] Where exactly in the ad is that moment?

**Fails when:** nobody's face moves. The ad may be clear, well-shot and accurate and still produce
no emotion, and without emotion there is no "liquid energy" to move anyone from scrolling to acting.

**This is the fastest diagnostic in the course.** It takes ten minutes and gives a signal you would
otherwise buy with several days of spend.

⚠️ A neutral face on a competent ad is a real finding, not a null result. It usually means the ad
is informing rather than affecting.

#### ⛔ You cannot run this test on a description

**The same rule that governs principle 4's numbers governs this one, and it is easier to break
because the output sounds like judgement rather than data.**

If all you have is a written description of a video, or a script, or someone's account of what
happens on screen, **you have not seen a face and neither has anyone else.** Do not deliver a
verdict on emotional impact as though you had. An invented "this will not move anyone" is exactly
as fabricated as an invented LTV, and it is harder to spot because nobody expects a number.

What you **can** legitimately assess from a description:

- Whether the concept contains an identifiable emotional **mechanism** at all: a surprise, a
  tension, a recognition, a payoff
- Whether anyone could name the sound it is aiming for
- Whether the described structure has a moment where a face plausibly *could* change, or whether
  it is uniformly pleasant from start to finish

What you **cannot** assess: whether faces actually change. That requires showing it to people.

So the honest output is: *"From the description, I cannot see an obvious moment designed to
produce a reaction, and here is why that matters. Show it to three people and watch. That takes
ten minutes and settles it."* That is more useful than a confident guess, and it hands them a test
they can run today.

---

### 3. The share reason

**Ask:** why would anyone send this to someone else?

- [ ] Who specifically would they send it to? Brother, mother, partner, boss, colleague?
- [ ] What does the *sharer* get out of sending it? Looking informed, funny, useful, right?
- [ ] For B2B: what is the "word of Slack" here?
- [ ] Would it survive being pasted into a group chat with no context?

**Fails when:** the ad is addressed entirely to one individual, with nothing in it that makes a
third party worth telling.

**The arithmetic is the argument.** One share per viewer halves acquisition cost, and the shared
version arrives through a trusted channel rather than as an ad. Most consumption on Instagram
happens in DMs, and none of that is visible in the ads manager.

⚠️ A share button is not a share reason. If there is no answer to "what does the sharer get", adding
a prompt will not create one.

---

### 4. The arithmetic

**Ask:** does this make money, and how do you know?

- [ ] What is the gross margin?
- [ ] What can you afford to pay for a customer, given that margin and the LTV?
- [ ] What is CAC now, against that number?
- [ ] Is the factory mapped: CPM → click → landing page → conversion rate → AOV → LTV?
- [ ] Who else is bidding on this audience, and can they pay more than you?
- [ ] Has anyone looked at the money-out levers (margin, AOV, LTV), or only at money in?

**Fails when:** nobody has opened a spreadsheet. George's terrible-ad instruction is literally
*"never open a spreadsheet."*

⚠️ **This check outranks the other three.** If the maths does not work, better creative makes the
loss arrive faster. If the maths has slack in it, worse creative can still perform, which is George's
own claim. Run this one first whenever the question involves spending more.

If the numbers are not available, **say which ones would settle it and ask**. Do not model an
answer on assumed margins.

---

### Reporting the result

Name which of the four it fails and why, in that order of severity: **4, then 2, then 1, then 3.**

- **4** decides whether creative can help at all
- **2** decides whether the ad does anything to anyone
- **1** decides whether it sounds like a person
- **3** decides how far each dollar travels

Where an ad passes all four, say so plainly. This is a check for finding real problems, not a
device for generating a quota of them, and an ad that passes should be told it passes.

Where it fails, be specific about the fix and honest about its size. "Book three customer calls
this week" is actionable. "Improve the margin" usually is not, at least not on the timescale of the
campaign, and saying so is more useful than pretending otherwise.

## The unit economics

Principle 4 in full. George calls it the most important of the four "because of how common it is",
and it is the part of this course most likely to change a decision rather than improve an ad.

⚠️ **This section answers questions the other three cannot.** "Should we spend more?", "is this
campaign viable?", "why is CAC rising?" are arithmetic questions. Creative advice cannot settle
them, and offering creative advice instead is the mistake the principle is about.

### The claim

> "The advertising game, when you really drill it down to first principles, it's money in, money
> out. So everybody focuses on money in and creative things they can do to reduce the amount of
> money in, without thinking about the money out and how much they can extract and build upon that
> just by changing the fundamentals of the business."

Two levers exist. Most teams pull only the first:

- **Money in** — lower the cost of acquisition through creative, copy, targeting
- **Money out** — raise what a customer is worth through margin, AOV, LTV

His comparison of the two:

> "let's say you could come up or leave this module today or leave this whole program today and you
> drop your advertising costs by 10%, which is significant if you're spending millions of dollars a
> year. Or, if you focus on how to increase the lifetime value of the customer or increase the
> average order value of the customer, goes so much further because it's not just for your paid ads
> acquisition, it's for your whole business acquisition."

### ⭐ The margin model

Borrowed from Alex Hormozi, and the single most useful number in the course.

Two identical businesses. Identical products, identical ad campaigns. One difference: **gross
margin of 90% versus 95%.**

> "you may look at that and go, "Hold on, 5% difference? What does it matter really in the grand
> schemes of advertising?" And you'd be seriously wrong."

On a $100 product:

| | 90% margin | 95% margin |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue kept | $90 | $95 |
| Room available for acquisition | **$10** | **$5**… |

George's framing: the 90% business is acquiring customers at $10, the 95% business at $5.

> "**And they can double the amount that they're spending on advertising.** So, they can double the
> other business just by playing with the margins at a 5% side of things. Like, that's how just a
> subtle tweak to the margins can have such a significant impact in the acquisition costs."

The point to carry: **a 5-point margin change moves what you can afford to pay for a customer by
2x.** No creative improvement is reliably worth that, which is why the maths deserves to be checked
before the creative brief is written.

> "If you can fix that, it gives you so much more juice to squeeze out of your front end with all
> the different advertising campaigns that you've got going on."

### Art, science and maths

George's three-way split of the job:

| | Is | Most teams |
|---|---|---|
| **Art** | The creative side | Over-invest here |
| **Science** | The data side, the A/B tests | Do some of this |
| **Maths** | The cold, hard money in, money out | ⚠️ Skip it |

The failure is not that art and science are wrong. It is that they are worked on in isolation from
a maths layer nobody has checked, so improvements are made to a system that may not be viable at
all.

### The factory map

> "A good way to view it is like a factory, and map out each key event."

The chain, in order, as he lists it:

| Stage | The question |
|---|---|
| **CPM** | How much does it cost me to reach a thousand people? |
| **Cost per click** | How much does it cost for somebody to click? |
| **Cost to landing page** | How much does it cost for somebody to hit the landing page? |
| **Conversion rate** | What percentage of them convert? |
| **AOV** | What's the average order value? |
| **LTV** | And then what's the lifetime value? |

Why bother mapping it:

> "Having that all mapped out means that you can then play around with the ad creatives so much more
> and seeing the impact it has in the full funnel or the full bottleneck analysis... And you can
> say, "Oh, when we run these ad creatives, it has this impact here." Or, "You know what? Now we've
> fixed this issue with the LTV. We can pull. We can triple our ad"

And the line that makes the argument concrete:

> "**And even make our ad creatives worse, and it actually performs better.**"

That is the strongest possible statement of the principle: with the maths fixed, worse creative can
outperform better creative, because the constraint was never the creative.

### Using this when someone asks about spend

The sequence that actually answers the question:

1. **Ask for the numbers.** Price, gross margin, current CAC, AOV, LTV. If none are available, say
   plainly that the question cannot be settled and name which figure would settle it.
2. **Find the breakeven.** What can they afford to pay for a customer given the margin and LTV?
3. **Locate the current position against it.** Comfortably inside, near it, or past it?
4. **Only then discuss creative.** If they are past breakeven, more or better creative is not the
   answer, and spending more makes the loss larger faster.
5. **Check the money-out lever.** Before optimising acquisition, ask whether margin, AOV or LTV can
   move, because those change the ceiling rather than the efficiency.

⚠️ **Do not model this on assumed numbers.** Inventing a margin to demonstrate the method produces
a confident answer to a question that was never asked, and the whole principle is that the real
figures are decisive. Show the method, ask for the inputs.

### What is quoted and what is organised

The four principles, their reasoning, the margin arithmetic, the factory map, the art/science/maths

`the-pre-ship-check.md` is this skill's organisation. George does not present his material as a

in that file (4, then 2, then 1, then 3) is also this skill's, derived from George's own statement

that the arithmetic determines whether creative changes can help at all.

### A note on scope

This is a short, deliberately reductive course. George's method is to identify the few things that
decide outcomes and ignore the rest, which makes it a strong pre-ship check and a poor creative
strategy. It will not tell you what your angle is, who your audience should be, or how to structure
an ad. Other courses in the programme cover those.
