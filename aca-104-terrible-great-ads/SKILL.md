# Course 104: How to Make Terrible Great Ads

George Mack (The Ad Professor), 17m 29s, 4 modules.

The premise, in his words:

> "The key to making great ads is learning how to make terrible ads and then just avoiding the
> principles behind the terrible ads and you've got great ads."

⚠️ **The four principles below are stated as instructions for making ads WORSE.** That is
deliberate and it is the technique, not a joke. Read each one as the mistake, then flip it. Losing
the inversion framing loses most of the value, because "talk to your customers" is advice everyone
nods at and ignores, while "avoid your customers, dehumanise them, keep them as far away as you
can" is recognisable as something people actually do.

He is explicit that these are his own mistakes: *"these are all the big mistakes I've made that you
can avoid."*

## What this skill needs from you

**Required: none.** The four principles are worth hearing before you have an ad to check.

**Optional, and it sharpens the answer:**

1. **The ad or concept** you want checked
2. **Your unit economics** — price, gross margin, CAC, AOV, LTV, or whichever of those you know
3. **What the ad is meant to achieve**

⚠️ **For principle 4 specifically, the numbers are not optional in practice.** If someone asks
whether they should spend more, or whether a campaign is viable, and no numbers are supplied, the
honest answer is that the question cannot be settled without them.

**Ask for them inside a full answer, not instead of one.** Run the other three principles, use
whatever figures you were given as far as they go, then name the missing one and what it would
settle. A reply that is only a question wastes the turn; a reply that invents the number is worse.
Do not model an answer on invented margins, because George's whole point is that the arithmetic is
decisive and most people skip it.

## Why inversion

Borrowed from mathematics: when an equation resists, flip it and the answer clarifies.

His example is Bezos:

> "People talk about in the next 10 years, how things are going to change and how Amazon needs to
> react to that change. But instead he likes to ask himself, well, what's not going to change in
> the next 10 years? He knows 10 years from now, no customer is going to ask for slower shipping
> speed, more expensive prices."

The motivation is the midwit meme: the person in the middle who overcomplicates.

> "in advertising, being the guy in the middle who overcomplicates things and thinks he's more
> intelligent or thinks she's more intelligent than she is, is a recipe for wasting a lot of time
> and most importantly, wasting a lot of money."

**Inversion is prospective, which is what makes it useful.** Most creative review is retrospective:
it ran, it failed, why. Inversion asks what would guarantee failure, before anything is made, and
then checks whether you are doing any of it. That is a question you can answer on a Tuesday with no
data.

`reference/the-four-principles.md` has all four in full. `reference/the-pre-ship-check.md` turns
them into something runnable.

## The four, in one line each

| To make it terrible | So instead |
|---|---|
| **1. Avoid customers.** Never speak to them. Call them "customers" so they stay a blob | Interview them, record it, and put their own words in the ad |
| **2. Avoid facial expression changes.** Never watch anyone's face | Watch faces, including your own. It is the earliest signal you have |
| **3. Give them no reason to share.** Make it purely about the individual | Design for the group chat. Sharing halves acquisition cost |
| **4. Avoid the unit economics.** Never open a spreadsheet | Obsess over the maths. It decides whether creative can help at all |

⚠️ **Check what "margin" means before using it.** George's model runs on gross margin, revenue minus the direct cost of goods. If someone quotes a figure that already has overheads, shipping or payment fees in it, the room for acquisition is smaller than the number suggests. Ask which one they mean rather than assuming the favourable reading.

George singles out the fourth: *"I think this might be the most important one because of how common
it is."*

## When to lead with which

| They are asking | Go to |
|---|---|
| Check this ad / concept before we ship | The whole check in `reference/the-pre-ship-check.md` |
| Sanity-check this concept **and** we're about to spend $X | Both, and lead with principle 4. This is the most common real shape of the request: the creative question is asked out loud, the money question is the one that decides it |
| The ad feels flat, we cannot say why | Principle 2. The face test is the fastest diagnostic in the course |
| Should we spend more? Is this viable? | **Principle 4 first.** Ask for the numbers before anything else |
| How do we lower CAC? | Principle 3 (sharing) and principle 4 (margin), in that order. Both move it more than creative does |
| Our copy sounds generic | Principle 1. The fix is source material, not more writing |
| Why did a good-looking ad fail? | Run all four. Usually 2 or 4 |

## How to work through it

**Invert first, then build.** The four principles land far harder as ways to ruin an ad than as advice,
which is the whole reason the course is shaped this way.

1. **Run the four failures** from `reference/the-four-principles.md` and let them recognise their own work
   in at least one. Recognition is what makes the rest stick.
2. **Flip each one** into what it implies. Same file, and this is the actual teaching.
3. ⛔ **Do the arithmetic before the craft**, using `reference/the-unit-economics.md`. If the numbers do not
   work, better creative cannot save the campaign and everything after this is wasted effort.
4. **Apply the pre-ship check** in `reference/the-pre-ship-check.md` to something real they have made or are
   about to ship.
5. **Check they can name which of the four their last flat ad broke**, and whether they have ever opened a
   spreadsheet on the campaign. The second question is the one most people cannot answer.

## Answering well

**The arithmetic outranks the creative advice.** If someone brings a spend or viability question,
principle 4 is the answer and the other three are secondary. George's margin example is the
sharpest thing in the course: a **5-point** difference in gross margin lets one business spend
**double** on acquisition against an identical competitor. No amount of creative work matches that.

**Be concrete about faces.** Principle 2 sounds soft and is the most testable idea here. "Show it
to three people and watch their eyebrows" is a real instruction that takes ten minutes.

**Do not soften the inversions into platitudes.** "Understand your audience" is not principle 1.
Principle 1 is: you are probably not speaking to any customers at all, you are sitting in a coffee
shop imagining a blob, and the fix is to book the calls.

Where an ad passes all four, say so. The check is meant to find real problems, not to generate a
quota of them.

## Register: George's bluntness is his, not yours

George writes and speaks in a contrarian, deliberately provocative register. *"To make terrible
ads, never open a spreadsheet."* *"Dehumanize them in your head."* That works from a lectern, and
it lands very differently delivered to someone who just told you their creative director loves the
video.

**Almost everyone who runs this check is emotionally invested in the thing being checked**, and
usually so is a colleague they have to keep working with. So:

- **Quote him for the idea, then say the finding plainly in your own words.** The provocation
  belongs in the teaching, not in the verdict.
- **Separate the work from the person.** "This concept has no moment where a face changes" is a
  finding. "Your creative director is wrong" is not, and it ends the conversation you were trying
  to have.
- **Name what is genuinely working first**, when something is. Not as softening, but because a
  check that only ever produces criticism gets read as a formality and stops changing decisions.
- **Be direct about severity anyway.** Kind delivery is not the same as a hedged finding. If the
  arithmetic does not work on a $30k commitment, say that clearly and early; burying it politely
  is the worse failure.

The tone to aim for is a colleague who has seen this fail before and wants you to avoid it, which
is exactly what George is. Not a critic scoring the work.

## ⛔ One rule that applies to every principle

**Do not deliver a verdict on something you have not been given.**

The arithmetic version is obvious and stated repeatedly: no invented margins, no invented LTV. The
version that slips through is principle 2, because its output sounds like judgement rather than
data. If you have a written description of a video, you have not seen a face and neither has
anyone else. An invented "this will not move anyone" is exactly as fabricated as an invented LTV,
and harder to catch because nobody expects it to be a measurement.

`reference/the-pre-ship-check.md` covers what can and cannot honestly be assessed from a
description. The general form: **say what the material you have does support, name what would
settle the rest, and hand them a test they can run today.**

## What this course does not cover

Nothing on media buying mechanics, platform specifics, or production. It is a set of judgement
checks, not a workflow.

It is also **deliberately reductive**. George's method is to find the small number of things that
matter and ignore the rest, which is a feature for a pre-ship check and a limitation if you need a
full creative strategy. The four principles will not tell you what your angle should be or who your
audience is.
