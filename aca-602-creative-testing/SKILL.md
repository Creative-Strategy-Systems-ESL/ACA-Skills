# Course 602: Creative Testing Best Practices

**Carlotta Costanzo**, GAIN. 23m 03s, 2 modules.

> "We will be diving into the science behind creative. It's about **testing, learning and
> optimising.**"

> "Let's dive in and turn your creative process into **your most powerful performance engine.**"

⭐ **This skill emits the test plan and the analysis-to-next-test step.** When there is a real tracker
to fill rather than a method to explain, `aca-track-creative-tests` produces ACA's own sheet.

## What this skill needs from you

**Required: none, to explain any of it.** Ask what Level 1 and Level 2 mean, why testing matters, or
what a variation can change, and get an answer.

**To design a test plan, required:**

1. **The brand goal**, with a number. Carlotta's own example: *"Increase sales by 20% year-over-year
   with ROAS above 2.5."* ⛔ Everything hangs off this and the framework has no meaning without it
2. **What is running now**, or what concepts exist to test

**Optional, and each one sharpens the plan:**

3. Monthly spend and CPA, which decide how many tests are affordable
4. Results so far: hook rate, hold rate, CTR, CVR, CPA, ROAS
5. The channel, since a TikTok test and a Meta test are not comparable
6. Which creators, formats or messages have already been tried

⛔ **Never invent a result.** This course is built on worked examples with real numbers, and the
temptation is to produce more of them. If you were not given the performance data, say what the test
would tell you rather than what it would find.

## The through-line

> "Testing is just one piece of the creative strategy puzzle. **The real value comes from what you do
> next**: analyzing your results and turning those insights into smarter, more effective creative
> decisions."

⭐ **A test that produced a number and no next test was wasted.** Every worked example in this course
ends with what to try next, and that is the standard to hold any output here to.

## How to run it

**A test needs a hypothesis before it needs a budget.** Everything here is built to stop launching and hoping.

1. **Make the case that testing compounds**, from **Why test** (below). ⚠️ Quote its numbers with the
   caveat in **Provenance** (below); they carry no dataset.
2. **Set the brand goal**, then build Level 1 concepts under it, using **The two levels** (below).
3. **Build Level 2 variations** beneath each concept, changing exactly one of the five things — messaging,
   visuals, creators, hooks, formats. ⛔ One variable, or the result cannot be attributed.
4. **Write the hypothesis for each test before it launches.** This is what makes the tracker mean anything
   later.
5. **Read the result** with **Reading results, and producing the next test** (below), hook rate and hold rate together rather than separately.
6. ⭐ **Turn the result into the next test**, not a report. That step is the course's actual output.
7. ⚠️ **To log it, route to `aca-track-creative-tests`** if installed — it holds ACA's tracker and flags the
   three fields the sheet omits.
8. **Check each test has a hypothesis and a next step.** Either one missing means the test was an expense.

## Answering well

**Creative is the targeting, and that is why testing is not optional.** *"With detailed audience
targeting becoming less effective, your creative is now what determines who you reach, how you
connect, and whether you win attention."*

**Creative expires.** ⚠️ *"On average, a new creative only performs at its peak for a few weeks. At
first, you'll see high volumes and strong CPAs, but that impact tails off quickly."* So testing is
maintenance, not a project.

**Two levels, and they answer different questions.** Level 1 asks *which direction works*. Level 2
asks *was that real, and what exactly made it work*. Running only Level 1 gives you a winner you
cannot explain; running only Level 2 polishes something that may be the wrong idea.

**One variable at a time is what makes a result readable.** The Moonbird Level 2 test held the visual
hierarchy, the product shot and the layout constant and changed only the headline, which is why the
answer was usable.

⭐ **"All of them" is a legitimate result.** In that test every variation performed well and one drove
three times the conversions. A test where nothing fails still tells you where the volume is.

## ⚠️ Scope

Designing, running and reading creative tests. It does not tell you **how much** creative to produce
at a given spend — that is Course 601 and `aca-plan-creative-volume`. It does not define the metrics
it uses; the formulas and the bands are Course 402. It does not generate the concepts being tested,
which is Course 401.

## Why test

> "You've probably heard the phrase, **'creative is now the new targeting.'** In today's
> privacy-first, algorithm-driven world, it's more than just a catchy line — it's the reality of
> modern advertising. With detailed audience targeting becoming less effective, **your creative is
> now what determines who you reach, how you connect, and whether you win attention.**"

> "Let's get to the core question: **Why do we actually test creatives?** Is it really needed, or is
> it just a matter of blindly launching new creatives to go after scale?"

### The evidence she gives

#### Meta's own research

| Claim | As given |
|---|---|
| **15+ experiments a year** | *"Advertisers who run at least 15 experiments per year see their results improve by **up to 30%** compared to advertisers who don't run any tests"* |
| **Sustained over two years** | *"Advertisers who run a minimum of 15 tests a year for two consecutive years see a **45% uplift** in performance"* — attributed to Meta Marketing Science Research |

⚠️ **Both are Meta's numbers about Meta advertising**, cited without a link or a study name, and the
comparison group is *advertisers who don't run any tests* — a low bar. See **Provenance** (below).

⭐ **The useful part is the shape, not the percentage.** Fifteen tests a year is roughly one every
three weeks, and the second figure says the benefit compounds only if you keep going. That is a
cadence argument, and it survives whatever the exact number is.

#### Her agency's own book

> "When we look at my agency client base — **managing over £100 million in media spend globally** —
> the correlation is crystal clear: brands that consistently test and iterate on creative see
> stronger, more efficient growth."

> "Our data shows **a strong positive correlation between ad spend and the number of new creatives
> launched.** In other words, the more you invest in fresh creative, the greater your potential for
> scale."

⚠️ **That correlation runs both ways and she reads it one way.** Accounts that spend more also
produce more creative because they can afford to; the sentence *"the more you invest in fresh
creative, the greater your potential for scale"* asserts a direction the correlation does not
establish. Course 601 makes the same link and gives the spend-to-volume ladder that operationalises
it.

#### ⛔ Creative expires

> "Here's the extra challenge: **creatives don't last forever.** On average, a new creative only
> performs at its peak for **a few weeks.** At first, you'll see high volumes and strong CPAs, but
> that impact tails off quickly."

⭐ **This is the argument that actually decides the question.** If peak performance lasts weeks, then
testing is not a project with an end date — it is the thing that keeps the account alive. A team that
treats testing as a phase will be back where it started within a quarter.

### The two case studies

| Brand | What changed | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **D2C, hearables** | Pace of testing raised **from 5 to 20 A/B tests** and **from 44 to 120 creative concepts**, in one quarter | **Revenue +119%**, order volume doubled, ⭐ **ROAS held stable while spend scaled** |
| **B2B services** | **Doubled the number of UGC videos** tested over two months | **Qualified leads +83% year-over-year**, average cost per lead **cut by more than half** |

⭐ **The stable ROAS is the detail worth quoting.** Revenue doubling while efficiency holds is the
only version of scale that is actually good news; revenue up with ROAS down is just more spend.

⚠️ **Both are single accounts with several things changing at once**, and neither is a controlled
experiment. The hearables brand raised tests *and* concepts *and* spend simultaneously. Quote them as
what these brands did, not as what a given increase will produce.

⚠️ **The B2B example is the more transferable one**, because only one variable moved: more UGC
videos tested.

### What testing is actually for

> "The goal of creative testing in paid media is to discover **which ideas, messages and visuals
> truly engage your audience and deliver the highest return — efficiently.** By using structured
> tests, you take the guesswork out of creative and let the data show you what really works."

⛔ **"Efficiently" is doing real work in that sentence.** The point is not to run every test
imaginable; it is to spend the least money required to learn something you can act on. That is why
Course 601's volume ladder exists and why a test with no next step is a wasted budget line.

## The two levels

> "Let's walk through a clear, step-by-step process for running creative tests, using this framework
> as our guide."

### The structure

| Tier | What sits there |
|---|---|
| **Brand goal** | ⭐ The North Star. Her example: *"Increase sales by 20% year-over-year with ROAS above 2.5."* **"Every creative decision should support it"** |
| **Level 1 — Concepts** | *"Your big, bold creative swings — completely new ideas, themes or formats that are **fundamentally different** from what you've done before"* |
| **Level 2 — Variations** | *"Here you systematically test and iterate — validating concepts, extending what works, and refining your creative through tweaks to messaging, visuals, hooks or formats"* |

> "This structure ensures you're always balancing **breakthrough innovation with ongoing
> improvement**, all aligned to your main business objective."

⛔ **Without the goal at the top this is just a list of ads.** The goal is what makes a result mean
something: a variation that lifts CTR and drops ROAS below 2.5 has failed against her example goal
and passed against a traffic goal.

---

### Level 1: the big swings

> "Level 1 is all about launching **brand-new creative concepts that are fundamentally different**
> from what you've run before. Some people also refer to these as **themes** or **large swings.** The
> goal here is to explore a wide range of directions and see which angle resonates most."

#### ⭐ The worked example: Moonbird

*A handheld device that guides users through calming breathing exercises to reduce stress and
anxiety and improve sleep.* **Three distinct Level 1 concepts:**

| # | Concept |
|---|---|
| 1 | A **static** with a hand holding the product, with USPs |
| 2 | A **split screen** with lifestyle imagery and benefits |
| 3 | The **iPhone Notes format** — a video mimicking the Notes app, including product info and a product video |

**The result:** *"The static with the hand holding product, focused on the **Sleep Training** angle,
stood out — delivering a **38% lower CPA than the account average.**"*

> "This is the power of Level 1 testing: by exploring diverse, bold concepts, **you can quickly
> identify which creative direction unlocks the best results.**"

⭐ **Note what varied: format, layout and angle all at once.** That is correct for Level 1 and wrong
for Level 2. You are not isolating a variable here, you are finding a direction.

⚠️ **The benchmark is "the account average", not a control.** A 38% lower CPA against the account
average is a real signal and not a clean experimental result. Say which it is when reporting one.

---

### Level 2: the variations

> "While this level is often associated with optimizing your current top-performing creatives, it
> actually serves **several important purposes.**"

| # | Purpose | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ⭐ **Validate the concept** | *"It allows you to validate concepts by not relying on one variation only. Testing multiple variations at the same time helps **confirm that your success isn't simply by chance**"* |
| 2 | **Pinpoint and extend what works** | *"By identifying the strongest elements, you can keep your creative fresh and relevant for longer"* — this is the fatigue defence |
| 3 | **Optimise** | *"Enhancing what's working and removing or improving weaker elements"* |

⭐ **Purpose 1 is the one people skip and the most important.** One winning ad is an anecdote. Four
variations of it that all perform is evidence the concept works rather than that one execution got
lucky.

#### ⭐ The worked example: Moonbird again

**Four variations of the same winning creative.** *"Each ad uses the same visual hierarchy — a hand
holding the product, clear USPs, a clean layout. **The only element that changes is the headline.**"*

| Headline |
|---|
| Anti-anxiety |
| Travel anxiety relief |
| Sleep training |
| Focus booster |

**The result:** *"All versions generated a strong ROAS"* — and **the Anti-Anxiety version drove three
times the volume of conversions** compared to the other variants.

> "By making focused, data-driven iterations on a proven concept, you can quickly identify **which
> angle truly resonates** and scale efficiently what works."

⭐ **"All of them" is a real answer.** Nothing failed, and the test still located where the volume
is. A test only counts as inconclusive when it cannot change what you do next.

⭐ **And the finding travels.** *"This piece of insight can be leveraged to optimise other creatives
as well, and to allocate budgets more effectively around the messaging that resonates best."* One
Level 2 test told them which pain point to lead with everywhere.

### The five things a variation can change

> "Level 2 isn't just about changing one word — it's about **systematically exploring all the
> elements that make up your ad.**"

| # | Type |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Messaging** |
| 2 | **Visuals** |
| 3 | **Creators** |
| 4 | **Hooks** |
| 5 | **Formats** |

#### 1. Messaging — Loop Earplugs

*"Loop Earplugs is widely recognized as a trailblazer in paid media creative strategy. Their approach
— **running thousands of highly targeted, use-case-driven ads** — has set a new industry standard."*

**Same visual, headline varies to hit a different pain point:**

| Headline | Who it speaks to |
|---|---|
| *"Is this the REAL reason you're tired?"* | People struggling with sleep, suggesting noise as the hidden issue |
| *"Is this the REAL reason you can't focus?"* | Those easily distracted, reframing focus as a noise problem |
| *"Is this the REAL reason you hate going out?"* | Social discomfort, positioning noise reduction as the solution |
| *"Is this the REAL reason you're overwhelmed?"* | Anyone overstimulated, offering relief through noise control |

⭐ **Her framing of what this actually is:** *"By running these micro-variations, we are tapping into
different pain points that represent real use cases in different contexts of use, **creating a micro
targeting which is channeled through creatives.**"* This is the creative-is-targeting argument made
operational — the ad chooses the audience.

#### 2. Visuals — Glossier, and three ways to iterate

| Iteration | What she says |
|---|---|
| **Product image, same visual hierarchy** | ⭐ *"Here's a little secret from years of testing: **adding a human element — like a hand — without taking focus away from the product often outperforms product-only visuals.** The human touch creates connection and relatability."* **A test worth running on your accounts** |
| **A simple colour change** | *"Different colors evoke different emotions."* Calming blues for a wellness crowd, bold reds for urgency. ⭐ **Her pro tip:** *"whenever I've tested it, updating creatives to reflect **seasonal colors—like** more summery or wintery tones—has consistently improved performance."* |
| **Video variations** | On founder testimonials: transitions, music, video length, text overlays, different hooks |

**Two video examples she calls out:**

- ⭐ **A green-screen edit of a founder testimonial**, overlaying the founder on visuals of the
  product and website. *"This combines the credibility element with a more concrete visualization of
  the product."*
- **A good-performing video plus an article-style headline in a listicle format** — *"Why busy moms
  choose this every morning."* *"This adds authority and boosts credibility by framing the product as
  a trusted solution backed by clear reasons."*

> "visual iterations range from small tweaks like color and product placement to more sophisticated
> visual changes especially when it comes to videos."

#### 3. Creators

> "The impact of a creative isn't just about **what's being said, but who's saying it — and how.**
> The energy, relatability and authenticity of the creator can make all the difference, even when the
> script and structure remain the same."

⭐ **Her method, and the instruction inside it:** *"If you find a winning script, **give multiple
creators the artistic license to interpret it in their own style.** Let them bring their unique
energy, personality and perspective. Then test these variations to see which creator's delivery
resonates most."*

⚠️ **That is deliberately not a controlled test**, and it is right not to be. Handing creators a
script to perform identically would test nothing about them.

#### 4. Hooks

> "A hook is **anything that grabs attention within the first three seconds** — it could be engaging
> text, a surprising statement, or a striking visual."

**Three video hooks tested for a food spice brand:**

| Opening line |
|---|
| *"Struggle to find the time to cook?"* |
| ⭐ *"Biggest NoNo when cooking at home"* — **the winner, 25% lower CPA** |
| *"I wasted so much time and money"* |

**Why she says it won:** *"It taps directly into **curiosity and self-reflection.** The phrase
'Biggest NoNo' makes people wonder if they're making a mistake they didn't know about — it's a
pattern interrupt that sparks instant interest. **Instead of just stating a problem, it invites the
viewer to find out what they might be doing wrong**, which is a powerful motivator to keep watching."

⭐ **The transferable rule: an implied accusation beats a stated problem.** The two losing hooks both
describe the viewer's situation; the winner suggests they are getting something wrong and withholds
what.

**And a visual hook, where the text stayed the same:** a video opening on a **split screen showing
two product colours**. *"Especially effective for products like clothing, where colour preference
plays a big role. By displaying multiple colour options upfront you immediately appeal to a wider
range of potential buyers"* — lifting hook rate and potentially conversion rate too.

#### 5. Formats

> "If you have a static ad that's performing well, **don't stop there** — explore how that same
> creative can work in other formats, like video, carousel or collection ads."

Take a high-performing static and turn it into a short video with motion graphics, or break it into a
carousel that tells a story across frames.

⭐ *"This is a simple variation, but it's one that's **surprisingly easy to overlook.**"* It is also
the cheapest of the five: no new shoot, no new creator, no new message.

### Producing a test plan

**Deliver:**

1. **The brand goal at the top**, with its number. ⛔ If it was not supplied, ask — do not invent one
2. **Level 1 concepts**, each genuinely different in direction rather than in execution
3. **Level 2 variations under each**, with ⛔ **the held-constant elements named explicitly** and the
   one changed element identified
4. **Which of the five types** each variation exercises, and the spread across them
5. **A hypothesis per test** in the form `changing X will move Y because Z`
6. ⛔ **What each result would cause you to do next**, win or lose

⚠️ **A Level 2 test that changes two things is a Level 1 test with fewer options.** If more than one
element moves, say so and expect a direction rather than an explanation.

## Reading results, and producing the next test

> "Testing is just one piece of the creative strategy puzzle. **The real value comes from what you do
> next**: analyzing your results and turning those insights into smarter, more effective creative
> decisions."

### The metrics she reads

> "This is where metrics matter. By looking at data points like **hook rate, hold rate, CTR, CVR, CPA
> and ROAS**, you can see exactly how your creative performs at every stage of the funnel. **Each
> metric tells you something different — where you're grabbing attention, where you might be losing
> people, and where you're converting.**"

⚠️ **This course names the metrics and does not define them.** No formulas, no thresholds, no bands.
Course 402 has all three; take the definitions from there rather than inventing any here.

⭐ **Her reading pattern is always a pair, never a single number.** Hook rate *with* hold rate tells
you whether an ad stopped people and then kept them. Either alone is ambiguous.

### Worked example 1: Medik8

*A skincare brand. A table of top-performing creatives — "ads that drove the most conversions
efficiently at scale, with strong CPA and ROAS."*

**What she noticed:** *"One video stands out with the **highest hook rate and the second-highest hold
rate.** That tells us it's **not only stopping the scroll, but also keeping people engaged longer**
than most of the other ads."*

**Why that creative worked, in her reading:**

1. ⭐ **A strong, direct hook**: *"If you're not using retinoids in your skincare, then what are you
   even doing?"*
2. **A mashup of different creators**, *"which adds variety and keeps the energy high"*

**The three next tests she derives:**

| Next test |
|---|
| Create similar **mashups using new b-roll from different creators**, to keep the content fresh |
| Take the same winning script and **have it shot by your very best creators** — see who delivers it most persuasively |
| Test the impact of **male versus female creators** |

⭐ **And she prioritises rather than listing.** *"If I had to pick one to prioritize, I'd double down
on the mashup format with new creator footage. I have seen UGC mashups working so well across the
board."*

⚠️ **Note what the priority rests on: her prior experience across accounts, not this account's data.**
That is a legitimate way to break a tie between three equally-supported options, and it should be
labelled as what it is when you do the same.

---

### Worked example 2: two TikTok videos

*"Both creatives drove very strong results and conversions at scale, but one clearly outperformed the
other in terms of **thumbstop rate and hold rate.**"*

> "This data highlights the importance of **not just looking at conversions but also engagement
> metrics** like thumbstop and hold rate to understand creative effectiveness."

**The observation she makes before playing the video** — and it is the whole method in one move:

> "Pay attention to the **thumbnail**: one video shows the product clearly with the brand visible,
> while the other features a person holding the product. **This visual difference can have a big
> impact on initial attention and ultimately performance.**"

**The winning hook:** *"You need to add this to your skincare routine if you want youthful skin."*
*"It speaks directly to the audience's desire and creates urgency."*

**The next test, stated as a hypothesis:**

> "Our hypothesis: **showing the product prominently in the thumbnail, combined with a strong hook,
> will outperform a thumbnail featuring a person holding the product.**"

> "This test will help us understand which visual approach drives better engagement and conversions,
> allowing us to refine our creative strategy and **potentially rolling out this insight to other
> creatives.**"

⚠️ **This hypothesis contradicts her own Glossier finding**, where *"adding a human element — like a
hand — often outperforms product-only visuals."* Neither is wrong: one is about a static's product
image and the other about a video thumbnail on TikTok, and she is proposing a test rather than
asserting an answer. ⛔ **But do not carry either as a rule.** She calls the Glossier one *"a test
worth running on your accounts"*, which is the correct status for both.

### ⭐ The method, extracted

Both worked examples run the same five steps. This is the reusable part of the course.

| # | Step |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Start from the winners**, not the losers. Both examples open on top-performing creatives |
| 2 | **Read a pair of engagement metrics**, not one — hook rate with hold rate |
| 3 | **Watch the actual ad** and name the specific features that might explain the number |
| 4 | **Turn one feature into a hypothesis** in the form `X will outperform Y` |
| 5 | **Ask whether the insight generalises** to other creatives in the account |

⛔ **Step 3 is the one that gets skipped, and skipping it makes the rest guesswork.** A number cannot
tell you why. Somebody has to look at the ad and say what is in it.

⭐ **Step 5 is where the value compounds.** A test that improves one ad pays once; an insight rolled
across the account pays every time.

### Producing the analysis

**Deliver:**

1. **A short read of what the numbers say**, always pairing an attention metric with a retention or
   conversion one
2. **The named features** of the standout creative — the actual hook line, the format, the
   thumbnail, who is on camera
3. **Two to four next tests**, each a hypothesis in the form `X will outperform Y, because Z`
4. ⭐ **One of them prioritised**, with the reason stated — this account's data, or prior experience,
   and say which
5. **Whether the insight should be rolled out** to other creatives, or is specific to this one

⛔ **Never state a cause you have not been shown.** `The thumbnail is why it won` is a hypothesis
until it is tested — which is exactly how Carlotta phrases it, and the phrasing is the discipline.

⛔ **Never invent a metric value.** If hook rate and hold rate were not supplied, say which numbers
would settle the question rather than producing a plausible table. This course's persuasive force
comes entirely from real figures attached to named brands, and a fabricated one inherits that
authority without earning it.

⛔ **One claim reverses a correlation.** *"Our data shows a strong positive correlation between ad
spend and the number of new creatives launched"* is then read as *"the more you invest in fresh
creative, the greater your potential for scale."* Bigger accounts produce more creative because they
can afford to; the correlation does not establish the direction she draws from it.

### ⚠️ The course contradicts itself once, and it is instructive

The **Glossier** finding says *"adding a human element — like a hand — without taking focus away from
the product **often outperforms product-only visuals.**"*

The **TikTok thumbnail hypothesis** says *"showing the product prominently in the thumbnail... **will
outperform a thumbnail featuring a person holding the product.**"*

⭐ **Neither is stated as a law and that is the point.** She calls the first *"a test worth running on
your accounts"* and frames the second as a hypothesis. Different surfaces (a static's product image
against a TikTok video thumbnail) and different platforms. ⛔ **Carry both as tests, not findings** —
which is what the course itself does.

### ⛔ Where this course meets the others

**It names metrics and defines none.** Hook rate, hold rate, CTR, CVR, CPA and ROAS are all used and
none is given a formula, a threshold or a band. **Course 402 has all three.** Taking the definitions
from there is correct; inventing one here would be attributing a number to a course that carefully
gives none.

**⚠️ Its own tracker groups metrics in a way Course 402 contradicts.** ACA's Creative Testing Tracker
puts **CTR (Link) under ACTION METRICS** and **CTR (All) under CREATIVE METRICS**. Course 402 argues
CTR is one of the two metrics creative owns, and that CTR (All) *"should not be used to evaluate
creative effectiveness"* — an inversion on both counts. The banner merge also spans Live Date and
Results, which are not metrics, so sloppy merging is possible. **Recorded as a discrepancy to
resolve, not as ACA's settled position.** `aca-track-creative-tests` carries it.

**It does not say how much to test.** Volume by spend level is Course 601's ladder.

**Course 601 names the same tracker** and calls it the *"Creative Testing Tracker & Pipeline
template"*, where Carlotta calls it *"the Creative Test Tracker I use with most of my clients"*. Same
artifact, two names, two courses — worth knowing when someone asks for it by either.

### ⛔ Carlotta's own list of what to track omits three things her sheet needs

She says to capture, per test: **the concept name and specific variation · the KPI · the channel ·
the test hypothesis · the priority and live date · a clear summary of results · the next steps.**

**The shipped tracker has 19 columns and no column for KPI, Channel or Priority.** Measured against
the workbook. `aca-track-creative-tests` emits ACA's sheet as it ships and flags the three.
