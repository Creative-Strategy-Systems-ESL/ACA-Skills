# The four principles

Each is stated as **how to make an ad terrible**, then flipped. Keep the inversion framing: it is
what makes the advice land, because the failure mode is recognisable in a way the platitude is not.

---

## Principle 1 — Avoid customers

### The terrible version

> "Avoid customers. Don't speak to them. Don't look at them. Even better, call them customers.
> Dehumanize them in your head. Keep them as far away as you can. Sit there in your coffee shop
> thinking about how you're going to manipulate this blob of customers out there that you're trying
> to get to buy your product. They're just this universal blob that you don't really know that much
> about."

George on how often he has done it himself:

> "One of the biggest mistakes I've made and have made again and again and again is avoiding
> speaking to the customers who are actually buying the product."

### Inverted

Speak to them as much as you can, and **retire the word "customer"**:

> "They're not a customer. They're a father. They're a mother. They live in New York. They live in
> London. They have specific personalities. They are a human being. And the more you get into how
> they think, the easier it's going to be to advertise to them."

### How, concretely

**1. One-on-one interviews.** Arrange as many as you can, **record them**, and look for key bits of
language. Study how they speak, then use how they speak in the ads themselves.

**2. The data version, at scale.** If you have history, take the comment section and all the
reviews, scrape the lot, and work with an AI to extract:
- What keywords do my customers use?
- What are the actual pain points they talk about?

Then put that language back into the advertising.

**3. Subreddits and forums**, for specific pain points in the language people actually use.

### Why it works

> "Then all of a sudden when the person sees the advert, it's talking to them rather than some
> person far, far, far away trying to get them to buy something."

And the rule of thumb underneath it:

> "The best ads don't look like ads and the best sales people don't look like sales people."

### The writing test

George borrows from Dwarkesh Patel, on writing but applied to ads:

> "What are you trying to say here? Then you answer that question and then you just say that."

His gloss: if you were talking to an actual human being about the product, what is being said? Use
that language and let it write itself.

> "You don't have to sit there trying to come up with something unique and salesy."

⚠️ **This is not "understand your audience".** That is a platitude everyone agrees with and skips.
The claim is sharper: you are probably speaking to nobody, and the fix is booking the calls and
recording them. If someone cannot name the last customer they spoke to, that is the finding.

## Principle 2 — Avoid facial expression changes

### The terrible version

Never look at anyone's face while they see your ad. Never notice your own.

### The reasoning

> "If you study human beings, language itself has actually not been around that long. Thing that's
> been around much longer than language is our facial expressions. **Your face doesn't lie.**"

Then the chain that makes it matter:

> "Emotion is the liquid energy that gets somebody from where they are scrolling on a newsfeed or
> seeing a billboard to actually taking action. If there's no emotion, just the advertising laws of
> physics conclude that person will not take action."

> "**The upstream from emotion is the facial expression muscles.**"

So the face is not evidence of emotion arriving later. It is the first observable event in the
chain, which is why it is available before you have any performance data.

### Inverted, and how to run it

> "you want to study facial expressions like you've got a PhD in it. Whenever you hand somebody an
> ad, look. Whenever you see an ad yourself, study it in yourself. It is the gut signal, the
> strongest signal that an advert is going to work that you can know before you run MOD"

Two tests:

- **On other people.** Show the ad and watch. A change in the facial muscles is a good sign.
- **On yourself.** When the idea arrives, do you smile, frown, or raise your eyebrows? Those are
  the signals.

> "Once you see this principle, you can't see it. Notice every advert that you end up enjoying or
> buying from, you will have some kind of facial expression muscle change."

### The compression test

From George's friend Sean: great advertising can be compressed down to a few well-known sounds or
acronyms.

> "it could be, yes, it could be, LFG, it could be, ouch, anything that compresses it all the way
> down. **Those sounds are basically just representations of our facial expression muscles
> changing.**"

So design with one of those in mind: which sound is this ad trying to produce?

> "And if you do that, you've massively simplified what you're trying to achieve. And remember,
> without their facial expression muscles changing, there is no emotion. And without emotion,
> there's no liquid energy to go out and actually buy the product."

⚠️ **This is the most testable idea in the course and the one most likely to be dismissed as soft.**
Showing an ad to three people and watching their faces takes ten minutes and produces a signal you
otherwise wait days of spend for.

## Principle 3 — Give them no reason to share

### The terrible version

> "Under no circumstance, encourage them to share the advert with anybody. Make sure it's purely
> about that individual. There's no call to action to share. There's no reason to share. You put no
> thought into why they would share."

### The arithmetic

This is what makes it a numbers argument rather than a nice-to-have:

> "Let's say it costs me one dollar to get somebody to click on this advert. However, let's say in
> another scenario, it costs me one dollar, but on average, each time somebody sees it, they share
> it with one person. **All of a sudden, I've dropped my acquisition click targets in half** just
> by baking in that factor."

And it is not only cheaper:

> "That person is coming at it from an organic word of mouth lens. They haven't seen it as an
> advert. They've seen it as their friend sharing it with them. **So not only is it cheaper, it's
> way more effective.**"

George invokes the K factor, familiar from COVID: the higher you push it, the larger the reduction
in ad spend.

### Dark social

⚠️ **The recording says "Adam Asori"**; the head of Instagram is **Adam Mosseri**. Quoted as rendered.

> "Adam Asori, who's the head of Instagram, said the following that most content consumed on
> Instagram comes via sharing, comes via DMs. It's all going on in direct messages and group chats."

> "But we don't see that as advertisers. You just see the advert go live... you don't see the dark
> social that exists and it's everywhere. And it's obvious when you look at your own behavioral
> patterns that that's where you consume a lot of content and most importantly, **where you trust a
> lot of content**."

### Inverted, and how to design for it

> "Design your adverts with the group chat first. So you want to focus on word of mouth, but most
> importantly, **the word of WhatsApp, the word of iMessage, the word of Slack, the word of
> Instagram DMs**."

The questions to ask about the person from principle 1:

- Who is their brother? Their mother? Their father? Their husband or wife?
- Who is their boss? Their colleagues?
- **What is the angle they would share it for?**

For B2B specifically:

> "What is the person who views this or who is the person that views this and how are they going to
> share this advert across their channels? Why are they going to share it?"

⚠️ **A share button is not a share reason.** The question is what the sharer gets: looking
informed, looking funny, being useful to someone, having their own view confirmed. If there is no
answer, adding a prompt to share will not create one.

## Principle 4 — Avoid the unit economics

George's own ranking:

> "I think this might be the most important one because of how common it is."

### The terrible version

> "**To make terrible ads, never open a spreadsheet.** Don't factor in, okay, how much is this going
> to cost per click? What's the CPA? What's the average order value? What's the lifetime value? Is
> that actually sustainable? Who am I competing against in these ad options?... Just avoid all that
> and just focus on all the creative stuff."

He notes what you are actually up against in the auction:

> "Maybe there's a customer that can pay 10 times the price of me that I'm competing against."

### Inverted

> "Don't avoid the mathematics. **Obsess over them.** You should know inside out before you start
> running advertising campaigns, does my business make sense? Does the advertising side of my
> business make sense? Does the LTV justifies the customer acquisition costs?"

The full argument, including the margin model, the art/science/maths split and the factory map, is
in `the-unit-economics.md`, because it is the part most likely to actually change a decision.
