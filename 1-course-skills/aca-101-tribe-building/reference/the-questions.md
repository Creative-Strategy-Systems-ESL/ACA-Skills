# The questions, and how the course closes

Godin returns to a small set of questions repeatedly. They are the practical output of the course:
the things a strategist is supposed to have answered before any creative work starts.

## The four questions

> "If you're not here to make a change, we probably don't need to spend our time and money with
> you. But if you are here to make a change, let's be clear about what that change is."

1. **Who is it for?**
   > "Who exactly are we seeking to serve? When we show up with what we've got, who wants it?"
2. **What is it for?**
   > "If I'm going to take attention from somebody, if I'm going to show up in front of them, why?
   > Is it something as simple and selfish as because I'll make a profit, or am I serving them?"
3. **What's worth doing?**
4. **Who would miss us if we were gone?**

The fourth is the one people skip and the one that does the most work. It is also how he defines
the strategist's job:

> "The strategist is the one who takes 10 steps back and says, 'If we disappeared, who would miss
> us? What is the change we seek to make?'"

## Strategy as a philosophy of becoming

> "**Strategy is a philosophy of becoming.** What we seek to do is help people get to where they
> want to go, because it is very difficult to persuade someone to go somewhere they don't want to
> go."

The consequence, and it is a good test for an offer:

> "Most successes in the last five years are brands offering services or products, software,
> nonprofits, that when people encounter them, they don't need to be persuaded or hustled. They
> simply need to know of it, to learn of it, to be able to say, **this is what I want**."

If the plan depends on persuading people to want something they do not want, the strategy is doing
the wrong work.

## Tribes, and the seven words

> "**People like us do things like this.** Just seven words, the essence of what it is to be
> human."

His framing: culture drives humanity, and there is no monoculture however much anyone would like
there to be. A thousand years ago a tribe on the steppes of East Asia had a different culture from
one near the Hudson River or one around Stonehenge. There are tribes everywhere.

And the limit that matters:

> "You are not going to be able to capture a tribe, own a tribe, make them your tribe. But what you
> can do, what is the opportunity of our lives, is **find a tribe that needs a leader**, to find a
> tribe that wants to add something to its culture, to find a tribe that needs service, to honor
> them, to connect them, to lead them."

## What do we make?

> "What do we make? We don't make ads. We don't even make shoes. **We make a difference. We make
> choices. We make decisions.**"

He follows this with the good-decisions-versus-good-outcomes argument (in `frameworks.md`), because
the reason people avoid making decisions is that they expect to be judged on outcomes they do not
control.

## Deciding what to ignore

A quieter point, and an unusually concrete one:

> "It requires you to raise your hand and be on the hook to make your assertions out loud, to
> determine **what you're going to measure and what you're going to intentionally ignore**."

His own examples: he told his publishers he would not look at the New York Times bestseller list,
"because it's gamed and because it's measuring the wrong things", and that he does not read his
one-star Amazon reviews because he learns nothing from them.

> "When we make choices about where we're going to show up in social media, we're also making
> choices about where we're not going to show up. **Can you announce what those choices are and
> why?**"

## What the job actually is

> "When we show up to do our work as an ad professional, as an ad creative, as a creative
> strategist, we don't get to skip all these things and just say to the client, 'What kind of ad do
> you want?'"

> "We don't get to say, 'Isn't the ad funny or well-filmed?' No one cares. **We're not trying to
> win awards. We're trying to win trust. We're trying to win connection. We're trying to win the
> benefit of the doubt.** That doesn't happen because we're being authentic. It happens because
> we're being consistent, because we are creating tension, because we're building a bridge from
> here to there, because we're creating culture."

## The posture

> "Here, I made this. I made this. **I'm on the hook to make a change happen.**"

## Go make a ruckus

The closing instruction, and he defines it precisely so it cannot be misread as permission to be
loud:

> "Go make a ruckus. What I mean by that is simple. **Not** go spam people, go hustle people, go
> interrupt people. We're better than that. What it means to make a ruckus is to **find a place
> where you would be missed if you didn't show up**, to create possibility, to engage with people
> in a way they want to be engaged with, and to make things better."

> "Because the truth is, change is going to happen whether we want it or not. But better, better is
> possible."

## Using these in practice

These questions work as an intake at the start of a project, and they work as a diagnostic when
something is not landing. When an ad account is decaying, "who would miss us if we were gone?" is
often a more productive question than any creative brief, because it surfaces whether there is a
tribe to serve at all or only an audience being rented from a platform.

Do not present all four as a form to fill in. Ask the one that bites on the situation in front of
you, and let the answer pull the others along.
