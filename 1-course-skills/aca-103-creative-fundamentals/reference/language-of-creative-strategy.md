# The language of creative strategy

Fifteen terms. The reason to be precise about them is that most disagreements in creative reviews
are vocabulary problems wearing a disguise: two people arguing about "the concept" while one means
the strategic idea and the other means the script.

## Core strategy elements

The strategic foundation of any creative.

### 1. Offer — the WHAT

What you are selling. Not merely the product, but **how it is packaged and presented**.

An offer is not just a discount, though a discount can be part of one. It is the complete value
proposition: a physical product, a subscription, a service, a lead magnet.

> "Understanding the offer is crucial because it determines every other aspect of your creative
> strategy."

### 2. Angle — the WHY

The reason someone should buy **right now**. The big idea that captures interest and inspires
action.

> "When analyzing an ad, always identify the primary angle. Is it emphasizing scarcity?
> Educational value? Emotional transformation? This insight reveals what's driving conversions."

`angle-types.md` holds all ten types in full.

### 3. Target audience / persona — the WHO

Who the creative speaks to. Platforms handle technical targeting; **your creative must visually and
verbally speak to a specific persona**.

An effective definition goes beyond demographics into psychographic traits, behavioural patterns
and specific pain points.

### 4. Concept — the strategic intersection

The combination of offer, angle and audience. The core idea that drives a **set** of ads.

| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| Offer | WHAT are we selling? |
| Angle | WHY should they care? |
| Audience | WHO are we talking to? |

> "Each concept typically spawns multiple ads that test different executions of the same strategic
> idea."

This is the term most often used loosely. A concept is not an ad. If two "concepts" share offer,
angle and audience, they are one concept with two executions, and testing them tells you about
execution, not strategy.

## Execution elements

### 5. Ad — the creative execution

The specific execution of a concept, the actual piece the audience sees. Defined by its platform,
the creative formula it follows, and its execution approach.

### 6. Iteration — concept variations

A variation of a concept adapted to a new format or style. Ranges from minor adjustments to
complete reimaginations of the same core idea.

Types:
- **Minor variations** — changing the hook visual while keeping the same script
- **Format adaptations** — converting a video concept into a static image
- **Execution style changes** — turning a studio shoot into a UGC-style video

> "Systematic iteration is one of the most powerful tools in creative strategy."

### 7. Format / asset — size and placement variations

Size and placement variants of an ad: feed (1:1), Stories and Reels (9:16), in-stream (16:9).

> "Each ad should be created in multiple formats to maximize reach and performance across different
> placements."

## Critical ad elements

### 8. Hook — the scroll-stopper

The first **3 seconds**. The moment that decides whether someone stops.

> "It's consistently the most important element of any winning creative. Without an effective hook,
> your message never gets a chance to be heard."

### 9. Script structure — the building blocks sequence

How the ad's content is organised. See `building-blocks.md`.

### 10. Visuals — the show-don't-tell elements

Everything visual: shot types, transitions, text overlays.

> "In social advertising, visuals often communicate more powerfully than words."

### 11. Supers / text overlays

Copy placed over the visuals. Titles or closed captions.

### 12. Pacing — the rhythm of information

How quickly information is presented.

> "The rule of thumb is new information or visual changes approximately every 1.5 seconds."

A concrete, checkable standard. An ad that holds one frame for six seconds has a pacing problem
regardless of how good the copy is.

### 13. Congruency — the seamless experience

Consistency between the ad and the post-click experience. The messaging, visuals and promises in
the ad must align with what users find after clicking.

Congruency failures show up as good CTR with bad conversion, which is easy to misdiagnose as a
landing-page problem when it is really a mismatch.

### 14. Funnel — the post-click journey

The series of pages users experience after clicking.

> "Your ad's job changes based on the funnel type and complexity."

A long funnel lets the ad do less; a direct-to-cart funnel makes the ad carry the whole argument.

### 15. Conversion objective — the ultimate goal

The specific action you want taken: completing a purchase, submitting a lead form, and so on.

## Why the precision pays

Using these terms consistently makes three things possible that vague language does not:

- **Diagnosing performance to a level.** "The concept is wrong" and "the hook is wrong" are
  different problems with different fixes, and only one of them means rebuilding.
- **Briefing without ambiguity.** A brief naming offer, angle, audience, and required formats
  cannot be misread the way "make it feel premium" can.
- **Testing something specific.** If you cannot name which element changed between two ads, the
  test result cannot teach you anything transferable.
