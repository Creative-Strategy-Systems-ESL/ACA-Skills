# The competitor audit

Module 2: *The Competitor Audit — A Systematic Research Workflow* (8m 22s). The course's operational
core, taught as a live walkthrough on a real brand.

## The warning it opens with

> "you can't just scroll through your competitors Facebook pages and go, cool at and expect to get
> any better. That's how you end up a copycat or worse, a copycat with no strategy."

> "Looking at competitors for inspiration can be your greatest unlock. Or you could fall into the
> dark pit of being a meta brand that looks exactly like everyone else in your space."

**Both outcomes come from the same activity.** What separates them is whether there is a system
underneath. That is what this module supplies.

---

## The five steps

1. **Audit your own brand first and name the content gaps.** You cannot see what to borrow until you
   know what is missing.
2. **Study 5 to 6 competitors across price points**, not only direct ones.
3. **Check historical and seasonal campaigns** to find proven winners rather than current guesses.
4. **Remix the patterns with AI** into brand-specific variations.
5. **Find non-obvious inspiration from adjacent brands** that sell to the same person in a different
   category.

---

## Step 1: The worked example, and what a gap analysis looks like

The brand is **James Perse**, a luxury Los Angeles apparel label. *"Beautiful products, incredible
quality. The kind of brand where people pay $250 for a t-shirt because the fit and the fabric are
genuinely that good. They built a real lifestyle brand."*

> "But here's the honest truth. I had creative is not that great. And I say that with love."

The diagnosis, read straight off their ad history:

| What the audit found | What it means |
|---|---|
| Static images, no video testing | No format experimentation at all |
| No active hooks | Nothing engineered for the first three seconds |
| Videos are purely brand films | Made for brand, not for performance |
| Low creative volume | Too few shots on goal to learn anything |
| Zero UGC | The cheapest, highest-volume format is unused |

> "This is a brand with a ton of equity and an audience that already loves the product. But they're
> leaving so much performance on the table because their creative strategy is in brand mode."

⭐ **"In brand mode" is the diagnosis worth carrying.** It names a specific, common and fixable
condition: a brand whose creative is competent and beautiful and is not built to be tested. The
audit does not say the work is bad. It says the work is doing a different job from the one the
budget needs.

⚠️ **The transcript gives two different prices for the same t-shirt.** *"$250 for a t-shirt"* early
on, then *"True Classic Tees at $30 has a completely different creative engine than James Perse at
125"* and *"someone who spends $125 on a t-shirt"* later. Both are carried where they appear; the
$125 figure is the one used twice and the one the competitor comparison rests on.

⛔ **The discrepancy propagates, which is why it matters.** The competitor set below is built as a
price ladder around this brand: True Classic below it, Everlane and Theory near or above. Those
relationships only hold at **$125**. At $250 the ladder inverts and the worked example stops
demonstrating what it claims to. Use $125 and know that the source is not clean.

## Step 2: Competitors across price points

> "Here's something that most people get wrong about competitor analysis. They only look at direct
> competitors, brands that are at the same price point selling the same product. That's useful, but
> it's incomplete."

> "You should be studying competitors based on **every price point**, because a brand like True
> Classic Tees at $30 has a completely different creative engine than James Perse at $125. And
> that's exactly where interesting patterns live."

⭐ **This is the most transferable idea in the module.** The reason is mechanical rather than
aesthetic: **price point determines creative engine.** A $30 brand must convert on volume and
therefore tests aggressively, uses bundles, leans on urgency, and produces a lot. A $125 brand
converts on desire and can afford restraint. So the cheap brand is running the experiments the
expensive brand never will, and the results are free to read.

The set Zach builds for James Perse:

| Brand | Why it is in the set |
|---|---|
| **True Classic Tees** (~$30) | *"an absolute crusher, you know, aggressive performance, creative, great bundle ads with high volume of testing."* |
| **Everlane?** ⚠️ **NAME INFERRED, NOT STATED** (higher price point) | *"Transparency-driven creative with strong editorial approaches"* |
| **Outerknown** | *"Really aligned with the James Perse outdoor lifestyle"* |
| A premium basics brand | The direct competitor, *"to see how luxury does a performance."* |
| **Theory** | *"similar pricing tier. But you know enterprise level creative team"* |

⚠️ **One brand name in the transcript is garbled.** The second entry is rendered *"everything a
little bit higher, drug price point, transparency-driven creative"*. **Everlane** is the reading used
here: it is a higher-priced basics brand whose entire market position is transparency, which is the
only part of the phrase that survives intact. Treat the name as inferred, not as stated.

**What to read on each competitor:** every ad they have launched, how long each one ran, what formats
they use, and what hooks they are testing.

## Step 3: History is where the proof is

> "I can go back and look at what worked for these brands in Black Friday of last year, what they
> tested, what they scaled. If you're planning a Q4 campaign, this is intel you literally can't get
> anywhere else."

⭐ **Two signals do most of the work, and both come from timing rather than from the creative:**

| Signal | How to read it |
|---|---|
| **Ads launched on the same day** | A creative test. The brand was putting these against each other |
| **An ad that ran a long time** | The winner. *"That's the pattern to study"* |

**Run length is the closest thing to performance data you can see from outside.** A brand does not
keep paying for an ad that is not working. Nothing else visible from the outside carries that
information, which is why it is worth more than how good the ad looks.

⚠️ **Four months is the worked example's number, not a threshold.** Zach reads one ad that had run
four months as the winner in that account. The course sets no minimum anywhere, and what counts as
long depends entirely on the category's testing tempo. Read run length **relative to the other ads
in the same set**, not against a fixed figure.

⚠️ **Nor is there a defined window for "same day".** Ad libraries show start dates by day and brands
stagger launches, so treat a cluster within a few days as a probable test cohort and say that is what
you are doing.

⚠️ **Run length is a proxy and it lies in specific ways.** An ad can run long because it is an
evergreen brand asset nobody reviews, because it is contractually committed, or because the account
is badly managed. Long-running plus part of a same-day test cohort is a much stronger signal than
long-running alone.

## Copying versus remixing

The distinction the whole course turns on, stated on True Classic's bundle ads:

> "James Perse would never price that way, but the format showing a curated bundle style together
> with a clear value anchor, that's something James first could absolutely adapt to the price
> point."

> "**That's the difference between copying and remixing. You're not stealing the price point. You're
> stealing the structure.**"

| Copying | Remixing |
|---|---|
| Takes the execution | Takes the structure |
| Transfers the offer, the price, the claim | Transfers the mechanism and re-fits it |
| Works only if you are the same kind of brand | Works across price points and categories |
| Produces a brand that looks like the category | Produces something that feels new from proven parts |

**The test:** name the thing you are taking without naming the source brand's product, price or
audience. If you cannot, you are copying.

## Step 4: What to actually make

Two categories, and the distinction is about effort, not quality:

| | **Single base hits** | **Home runs** |
|---|---|---|
| What | Take one winning ad pattern and adapt it quickly | Take a deeper insight and build a full creative concept around it |
| Effort | Low, fast turnaround | High |
| Use for | Volume, filling a test slate | The concept the quarter is built on |

**For base hits**, Zach's flow is: put the reference ad into ChatGPT or Claude, **refine the
messaging first**, then either brief a designer or generate the asset with an image tool. He names
**Nano Banana, Higgsfield and Artlist** as the generation options and says he personally uses
Higgsfield or Artlist.

⭐ **"Refine the messaging first" is the step that gets skipped**, and skipping it is how this
becomes a machine for producing polished versions of someone else's ad. The image is the last step,
not the first.

**For home runs**, the worked example: Outerknown's longest-running video is an employee store tour
with product-specific highlights. Broken down, it works because it

- builds trust by showing a physical, brick-and-mortar store
- focuses on performance and function
- touches sustainable messaging

> "The best part is the James Perse stores literally belong in architectural magazines. They could
> heavily lean into this format."

**That is the reasoning to imitate:** the pattern was chosen because the target brand has an
unusually strong version of the asset the pattern needs. A store tour is a weak idea for a brand with
ugly stores.

## Step 5: Adjacent brands, the step most people skip

> "here's where it gets really interesting. And it's the part that most people skipped your best
> creative inspiration often doesn't come from your direct competitors. It comes from **adjacent
> brands,** companies that sell to the same person as you, but doing a completely different
> category."

The method is to describe the customer rather than the product. For James Perse: *"someone who
spends $125 on a t-shirt, cares about what they put in their body, what their home looks like, and
where they travel."*

That description produces the adjacent set: **luxury wellness, premium home goods, high-end
fitness.**

> "These are the brands to sell to the exact same avatar with completely different creative
> strategies. That is where original feeling ideas come from."

> "This is how to stop being a meta copycat and start building creative that feels genuinely fresh,
> even though every element of it came from proven patterns."

**How to generate the list yourself:** write one sentence describing the customer that mentions no
product category, then ask what else that person spends money on. The sentence is the work; the
brands follow from it.

with a verification discipline this course does not have. Where that skill is installed alongside
this one, route the adjacent-brand question there and keep this one for the audit.

## Doing this without the tool

The walkthrough runs inside **Foreplay**, and several steps are named as product features: **Spyder**
(competitor tracking), **boards** (saving), **Creative Target Expansion** (finding brands that share
an audience), automatic video transcription, and historical ad data.

⛔ **Zach founded Foreplay.** See `reference/provenance.md`. The method is separable from the
product, and here is which parts survive without it:

| Step | Without a paid tool |
|---|---|
| Audit your own creative | Free. Your own ad account, plus the public ad library |
| Competitors across price points | Free via the platforms' public ad libraries. Slower, and you build the list by hand |
| **Ads launched the same day** | ⚠️ Visible in public libraries via start dates, but you have to spot the grouping yourself |
| **Run length** | ✅ Public libraries show start dates, so this survives, and it is the single most valuable signal |
| **Historical and seasonal** | ⛔ **This is the real loss.** Public libraries generally show active ads. Last Black Friday is largely gone unless you saved it at the time |
| Video transcription | Any transcription tool |
| Adjacent-brand discovery | Manual, from the customer sentence. Or Course 230's prompt |

⭐ **The honest summary: most of this workflow runs on free public ad libraries.** Of the seven steps
above, five survive intact, one survives with manual effort, and one does not survive at all. What
you lose is historical depth and the automatic grouping of test cohorts. **Say that** rather than presenting
the product as required, and note the one workaround that matters: if you want seasonal history,
start saving now, because it cannot be recovered later.
