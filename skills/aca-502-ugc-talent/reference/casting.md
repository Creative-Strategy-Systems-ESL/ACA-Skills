# Reviewing talent and casting

> "Once the submissions start rolling in, it's easy to get overwhelmed. You'll likely get a mix of
> strong contenders and a lot of noise."

⚠️ **How much filtering you do depends on where you posted.** On Trend or Cohely *"you'll already
have some built-in filters — demographics, category experience, content style."* On Backstage or a
broader platform *"expect to do a lot more manual filtering. That's not a bad thing, it just means
you need a system."*

## The first pass: three buckets

> "I like to start by sorting creators into three buckets: **Strong yes · Maybe · No.**"

## What she looks at, in her order

### 1. ⭐ Real UGC samples, first and above everything

> "The first thing I look at? **Do they have actual UGC samples** — not just acting clips or glam
> reels, but real, vertical format social content where they speak to camera."

> "No matter how strong someone's photography or narrative film work is, **if they don't have any
> evidence they can sell a product authentically on camera, it's a risk.**"

### 2. Presence and energy

- Do they make strong eye contact with the lens?
- Are they expressive?
- Can they deliver with natural conviction?

> "If someone mumbles, avoids the camera, or seems unsure, they're probably not a great fit for
> performance-driven content. **Even if they have the perfect look!**"

⭐ **Her strongest claim in the course, and it should be quoted whenever a client pushes for looks:**

> "**9/10 times the more relatable talent with strong energy outperforms traditionally attractive
> talent with no charisma.**"

### 3. Tone and persona against the brief

> "If your brief called for a warm, maternal vibe, and the creator is giving influencer-hype energy,
> **they might not be right for this particular shoot, even if they're great on camera.**"

⭐ **"Not right for this shoot" is not "not good."** That distinction is what makes the grid worth
keeping rather than deleting, and it is the reason for the reuse column below.

## ⭐ The casting grid, and producing it

> "That's why using a casting grid can be incredibly helpful. You can build one in a simple
> spreadsheet."

**Chloe's five scoring criteria, and her own descriptions of each:**

| Criterion | What she says to record |
|---|---|
| **Demographic fit** | *"Age, skin tone, hair type, etc."* |
| **On-camera energy** | *"Calm? Upbeat? Playful?"* |
| **Vocal style** | *"Chatty, deadpan, bubbly, serious"* |
| **Believability** | *"Would you trust them as a real user of this product?"* |
| **Standout factor** | *"What makes them unique?"* |

**The scale:** *"Use a scale — like 1 to 5, or Must Have / Nice to Have / Not a Fit — and jot down a
few notes."*

> "The goal isn't perfection. **It's to narrow your pool to the top 3–5 people who best fit the
> brief.**"

⭐ **Keep the near-misses in the grid.** *"You can also keep folks who might have rated high on
certain criteria, but aren't perfect for this specific project, in the grid to cast for future
projects that might fit for them."*

### Emit it in this shape

```csv

```

⛔ **Fill the "Real UGC samples?" column first and let it gate the rest.** It is the criterion she
puts above all others, and a grid that averages it away with four other scores defeats the point.

### ⛔ Before you write a word in this grid

Chloe's grid scores **demographic fit** on age, skin tone and hair type, and her worked rationales
describe hair colour, accent and body type. That is ordinary casting for advertising: you are
casting someone to read as your customer, and appearance is part of the role.

**It is also a written, scored, durable record about named real people.** So:

- **Write what the content needs, not a rating of the person.** *"Brief calls for 35+, this reads
  35+"* is a note about the role. A number out of five next to someone's skin tone is a note about
  them.
- **Keep every cell defensible against the brief.** If a criterion is not in the brief, it should
  not be scoring anyone.
- **Never record anything you would not want read back to the applicant**, because a spreadsheet
  shared for client approval is a document that outlives the shoot.

⚠️ **The course does not raise any of this.** It is added here because the grid is the artifact this
skill produces, and producing it is the moment the question arises.

## ⭐ Presenting talent for approval

> "Here's something I've learned the hard way: **don't present talent for approval without
> context.** If you need buy-in from a client or internal stakeholder, don't just drop a few
> headshots in a Slack message. **Give a one-sentence rationale for each person.**"

**Her two examples:**

> "Katie has a really bubbly, inviting presence. Her red hair and British accent make her instantly
> memorable."

> "Brian isn't a typical male model, but he's been a top performer in our pants ads. His relatable
> tone and 'no-BS' energy connects with our 35+ male audience."

> "When you frame it like that, **decision-makers understand the strategy and are more likely to
> trust your recommendation.**"

⭐ **The second example is the more useful pattern**, because it leads with performance evidence and
pre-empts the objection ("isn't a typical male model") instead of waiting for it. A rationale that
names a past result is much harder to overrule on taste.

## Tracking who performs

> "Once you start producing content with a few creators, **keep tabs on which ones consistently
> deliver great results.** If someone's content consistently drives high CTR, low CPA, or strong
> hook rates, keep them in your rotation."

**At an agency:** *"tag creators in your internal tracker for potential reuse across other brands or
product categories."*

> "At OpenStore, we have a running **'Favorite Talent' list** based on ad performance. It saves time
> and gives us a reliable bench when we need to scale fast."

⭐ **This is the compounding part of the whole course.** Casting is expensive the first time and
nearly free the fifth, but only if somebody wrote down who worked. Add the performance column to the
grid after the ads run rather than starting a separate document.

⚠️ **Judge the creator on the metrics creative actually owns.** CTR and hook rate reflect the
performer and the hook; CPA is contaminated by the landing page, the price and the offer, so a
creator marked down on CPA may have been let down by something they never touched.
