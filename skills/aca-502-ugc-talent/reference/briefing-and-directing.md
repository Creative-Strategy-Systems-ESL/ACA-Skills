# Briefing a self-directed shoot, and directing a live one

> "This is where so many UGC projects fall apart — **not because the creator wasn't talented, but
> because the brief didn't give them what they needed to succeed.**"

> "A vague brief is a wasted budget."

> "**80% of great UGC is in the brief.**"

---

## The five parts of a brief

### 1. Product Information

> "Be clear about what the product is, what it does, and what problem it solves. You don't need to
> include every spec, but you do need to make sure the creator knows what makes the product special.
> **Especially the emotional benefit — how it makes the customer feel.**"

**Her worked example:**

> "This is a skincare mist designed to calm redness and replenish the skin barrier. Customers love
> it for how gentle it feels and how quickly it takes down inflammation. It's perfect for anyone
> who's self-conscious about redness."

⭐ **Three sentences: what it is, what customers say about it, who it is for.** Notice the middle one
is customer language, not a feature list — that is what the creator will end up paraphrasing.

### 2. ⛔ Script Prompts, not a script

> "This is one of the most important distinctions. **Very very rarely is it s good idea to hand a
> UGC creator a full script and say, “Say these exact words.”**"

**Two reasons she gives:**

1. It kills authenticity.
2. ⚠️ *"It increases your risk of content rejection on Meta or TikTok. Platforms know when something
   is scripted, and often flag that content as low-quality or 'inauthentic behavior.'"*

⚠️ **The second reason is her claim and carries no source.** See `reference/provenance.md`. The first
reason stands on its own and is the one to lead with.

**Use bullet-point prompts instead. Her examples:**

- Describe the problem you were having before using the product
- Show your real reaction to unboxing or using it for the first time
- Tell us one thing you didn't expect, but loved

> "When you let creators use their own words, the footage feels natural, human and native to the
> platform."

### 3. Structure Guidance

> "You don't need to control every word, but you do need to guide the flow."

**The structure she says most high-performing UGC follows:**

| # | Beat |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Before state / problem state** — the problem I was experiencing |
| 2 | **Category discovery** — other things I tried that didn't work |
| 3 | **Product discovery / experience** |
| 4 | **Solution / resolved state** |

⭐ **Beat 2 is the one people drop, and it is the one that does the persuasive work.** Naming what
failed first is what makes the product's claim credible rather than another promise.

**How she words it in an actual brief:**

> "Start with a strong hook that calls out a problem youve experienced and any solutions you tried
> in the past that you were disappointed with Then show how the product works or talk through your
> experience using it. End with a clear recommendation or why you'd tell a friend to try it."

### 4. Visual & Audio Instructions

> "This part gets overlooked, but it's so important."

| Instruction |
|---|
| **Format: always vertical, 9:16** |
| **Lighting** — natural light is great, avoid dark or blown-out shots. She recommends shooting in front of a window |
| **No background music**, unless requested |
| **No cluttered backgrounds or visible brands** |
| **No jump cuts**, unless specified |
| **Stable framing** — no handheld wobble unless it adds energy |

> "I always include a visual reference when possible — either from past winning content or a similar
> format that nailed the vibe."

### 5. Do's and Don'ts

> "Finally, include any compliance notes or brand safety guardrails."

- **Words to avoid**, like *"miracle"* or *"cure"*
- **Claims that require disclaimers**
- **Legal reminders** to avoid competitor mentions, profanity or sensitive topics

> "It's better to spell these out upfront than to get unusable footage and lose time on reshoots."

⭐ **This section is the cheapest insurance in the whole process.** A compliance problem discovered
in the edit costs a reshoot; the same problem prevented costs three lines in a document.

## What a good brief sounds like

**Hers, quoted from real creator briefs:**

> "Talk to the camera like you're FaceTiming a friend about this product."

> "Show us your real reaction when you use it — don't be afraid to make a face!"

> "Include a quick demo if possible, but keep it casual. You're not doing a tutorial."

**And a bad one:**

> "Say this line exactly: 'This shampoo changed my life!'"

> "Nope. That can be a red flag for authenticity and for performance."

⭐ **Every good example gives a relationship or a situation, not an instruction.** "FaceTiming a
friend" tells the creator how to be, which they can act; "be authentic" tells them a quality, which
they cannot.

## ⭐ The creator's own checklist

> "Always include a checklist at the end of the brief."

| Filmed in vertical 9:16 |
|---|
| Spoke clearly and confidently |
| No music or filters |
| Product shown or used clearly |
| Hook, story and CTA all included |

⛔ **This checklist is written for the creator, in their voice, and belongs at the end of the brief.**
It is not the same document as your own deliverables checklist in `reference/deliverables.md`, which
is what you check the returned footage against.

> "A strong brief isn't just a doc. **It's your quality control system.**"

## Directing a live shoot

> "**You're not a critic. You're a coach.** Live direction is not about control, it's about
> collaboration."

⚠️ **Start from who is actually in front of you.** *"Most UGC creators, and even many professional
models or influencers, aren't trained actors. They're real people trying to look comfortable on
camera, talk about a product, and follow your lead all at once."*

### 1. Start with warmth

> "Before you even start rolling, spend **about five to ten minutes just chatting** with the talent.
> Ask how they're feeling, talk about something totally unrelated to the shoot. Show them the set,
> the lighting, the camera setup. Anything to reduce anxiety"

⭐ **And she is specific about awkward subject matter**, which is where this matters most:

> "Especially if the shoot involves sensitive or slightly embarrassing subject matter — like shaping
> underwear, acne products, or body odor — **you need to be the first one to bring warmth and
> energy.**"

**Her worked example**, a Jack Archer testimonial shoot built on a line about *"pants that make your
butt look good"*: *"Instead of tiptoeing around it, I leaned in. I made jokes. I told the talent,
'This is a fan favorite, people LOVE these pants. Let's hype it up like we're on QVC.' And they
matched my energy. They loosened up."*

> "**Your confidence gives them permission to relax.**"

### 2. Direct with intentions, not line reads

**Avoid:** *"No, say it more like this."* · *"That didn't sound natural."*

**Instead:**

> "Can you imagine you're explaining this to your best friend who's skeptical?"

> "Let's try one where you sound totally over it — like this product saved your sanity."

> "That was great — can we also get one that's a bit slower, a bit more thoughtful?"

> "These types of notes give the creator **an emotional context to work with, rather than just
> trying to parrot your delivery.**"

⭐ **Shoot for options even when the first take is good.** *"Have them repeat the same line or beat
2 to 3 times in slightly different ways. Even if the first take is great, get another. This gives
you options in the edit, especially if you're cutting multiple variants or testing different hooks."*

### 3. When the energy is low, pivot

> "Sometimes talent just isn't “on” that day."

⭐ **Don't force it** is the operative advice.

| If the delivery is flat | Try |
|---|---|
| They are locked to the script | **Ask them to improvise.** Let them tell a story or demo the product without pressure |
| Testimonial mode is not working | **Change the format** — switch to a reaction or unboxing |
| They are stiff in the body | **Use movement.** Walk and talk, or hold and handle the product while speaking |

> "**If it's not working, don't push. Pivot.** Optimize the time you have on set. It's better to
> redirect to something that works than to waste 45 minutes forcing something that doesn't."

### 4. Four common on-set problems

| Problem | Her fix |
|---|---|
| **Talent seems stiff or robotic** | ⭐ A **throwaway take** where they say whatever they want. *"Usually this is when they loosen up, and that take might even be the winner"* |
| **The creator keeps messing up a line** | **Let them paraphrase.** If it is a critical claim, break it into parts or write it on a cue card out of frame |
| **They're too influencer-y or salesy** | Ask for one version that feels like a voice note to a friend: *"Imagine you're texting your friend about this product. What would you say?"* |
| **They don't believe in the product** | ⛔ *"Don't try to manufacture excitement."* Pivot to a different angle — curiosity or social proof. *"Just tell us what stood out to you in the instructions or packaging"* |

⭐ **The fourth one is the ethically important one and she gets it right.** The fix is to change what
you ask them to say, not to coach them into sounding convinced. A testimonial performed by someone
who does not believe it is the thing the whole format's credibility depends on not doing.

### 5. Leave space for magic

> "Some of the best moments in UGC happen when you don't over-direct. A funny improv, a moment of
> surprise, a real emotion — that's the stuff people stop scrolling for."

> "Once you've got your 'safe' takes, give the talent room to play. Say: **'We got what we needed,
> now let's try one just for fun.'** You might capture something better than anything in the brief."

⭐ **The order matters.** Safe takes first, play second. Reversed, a flat shoot has no usable footage
and no time left.
