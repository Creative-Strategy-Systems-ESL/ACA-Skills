# AI across the creative process

⚠️ **Quotes are verbatim from a spoken transcript**, which mishears several words. Each is flagged in place.

---

## Where AI came from, in marketing

> "AI first entered marketing in a supporting role, **as an automation tool.** Think back of the early days of
> Facebook and Google Apps. Advertisers relied on **manual campaign setup, demographic targeting, and static
> creative testing.** The AI used at the time was simple."

⚠️ *"Google Apps"* is the transcript's rendering of *Google Ads*.

⭐ **And the framing he insists on**, which is the reason the course exists:

> "understanding AI's impact on creative strategy **goes beyond just using a tool. It's about shifting how we
> think about the creative process itself.**"

> "This is not just about productivity, **it's about unlocking entirely new ways to approach creativity.**"

---

## ⭐ The five areas

> "We are going to break this down into **five key areas** where AI is making a massive impact."

| # | Area | What AI does there |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Research** | *"the time-consuming work of searching and compiling information for multiple sources that produce robust audience and marketing insights that **form the base of creative strategy**"* |
| 2 | **Planning** | *"helps us build more and more educated creative plans **from concept ideation to entire content calendars**"*, and *"refining practicalities around structuring and managing your productions"* |
| 3 | **Production** | *"generating scripts, images, and videos and enabling us to do even more complex edits on assets **without technical editing skills**"* |
| 4 | **Analysis** | *"gather and process large amounts of data quickly to help us understand better **what works and why**"*, then *"provide actionable recommendations for future iterations"* |
| 5 | ⭐ **Agentic workflows** | *"When AI becomes agentic, it can help us **run complete workflows autonomously**"* |

⭐ **The observation worth repeating**, because it corrects where most attention goes:

> "**Most of the things we see getting media hype is actually the most front-end part of AI usage.**"

That front end is area 3, generation. **Four of the five areas are not that.**

⭐ **What area 5 feels like, in his words:**

> "Think of a scenario where **a dozen of AI assistants are working on the exact same routines you work on
> today on a weekly basis** ... **all at the same time, without you even looking.** Ideally, **you would just
> have to check the outputs** and things are ready to be pushed forward."

### On research specifically

> "Before we create anything we need to understand the landscape. AI can **analyze millions of data points**
> to help us identify **what's trending, what content formats perform the best, what messaging resonates with
> different audiences**"

⭐ **The two capabilities he singles out as the real unlock:**

- **Proprietary data:** *"AI can connect to your own proprietary data sources and combine that with data
  available on open source and data that this has been trained"*
- ⭐ **Memory:** *"More advanced tools will enable AI to **store and retrieve things from its own memory**"* —
  which *"greatly improves a creative strategist's capability to **structure their thinking and build more
  robust type of thesis**"*

⚠️ **This passage stutters in the transcript**, repeating the thesis sentence twice. The repetition is the
recording's, not a second claim.

### On planning specifically

Four things it does: **concept generation** (*"suggesting creative ideas, themes, and storytelling
structures"*) · **content calendars** *"optimized for engagement patterns and creative demand"* ·
**production structuring** (*"creating shooting schedules, asset breakouts, and timetables"*) · and drafting
campaign and marketing plans.

⛔ **With a condition attached, and it is the course's stance in miniature:**

> "**Under supervision of a talented creative strategist**, AI can become powerful in drafting indoor
> campaigns and marketing plans."

⚠️ *"indoor campaigns"* is the transcript's rendering of *end-to-end* or similar; the sense is campaign plans.

### On production specifically

**Pre-production:** *"generating scripts, storyboards and even shot lists"*, and drafting *"ad copy and video
scripts optimized for engagement"*.

**His worked example**, which is a hook-problem-solution-CTA structure: for *"a 15 second TikTok ad"*, AI can
suggest *"an attention-cracking hook, a way to address customer problem and how the advertised product solves
the problem with a clear call to action, and even generate the entire script with multiple variations based on
past performing ads."*

**Post-production:** *"AI can automate voiceover creation, sound design, captions, back-count replacements and
even product placement."*

⭐ **And the argument for why this matters beyond cost:**

> "you can leverage AI to **create hundreds of new variations of your content in no time.** This not only
> improves **the unit economics of production**, but also improves **the creative testing velocity, which is
> key to unlocking creative learnings.**"

⭐ **That second half is the better argument** and the one usually dropped: more variations is not about
cheapness, it is about learning rate. It is also Course 602's argument, arrived at independently.

### On analysis specifically

> "AI can **track engagement, click-through rates and conversions.** It can **analyze results of AP tests** to
> refine messaging, visuals and call to action, and even **predict which creatives will perform best before
> they even run.**"

⚠️ *"AP tests"* is the transcript's rendering of *A/B tests*.

⚠️ **He is candid that this area is immature**, which is worth carrying because it is the only hedge of its
kind in the course: *"Personally, **I'm very curious to see how we can improve the quality of AI-driven
creative analysis and recommendations**, and we are actively working towards this goal."*

## ⛔ Consent, disclosure and synthetic likeness

⭐ **This is the only place in the entire programme that addresses the ethics of AI-generated people**, and it
arrives immediately after Jesse demonstrates an AI replica of himself presenting the course.

**The demonstration:** *"This is an AI replica of myself, an AI avatar as you may see. It moves and sounds like
me, but everything that I'm saying now comes from a written script. **I modelled my voice** to make it sound
like me, and **I trained the model to understand how I present things to the camera.**"*

⭐ **And he plays the uncertainty for effect**, then turns straight to the rules: *"Back to real me. Or is it
me? I think it is. I wouldn't be so sure."*

**The three rules, in his words:**

| # | Rule |
|---|---|
| 1 | ⛔ **Disclosure is platform-mandated in places.** *"Some platforms force their users to label AI-generated content, so **make sure you have read the guidelines before you start advertising with AI-generated content**"* |
| 2 | ⛔ **Consent for likeness.** *"**do not copy a person's appearance or voice without their permission**"* |
| 3 | **Documented permission and legal alignment.** *"we always make sure that we have the creator's permission to use the likeness in AI enhanced production, and we make sure we are aligned with the latest legal frameworks"* |

**And the framing:** *"It is important that we approach these powerful technologies with brand and
creator-safe way ... It is also important to be **ethical and respectful** when these technologies become more
popular."*

⛔ **Carry these three rules into Courses 802 and 803.** Both teach cloning techniques — a brand's visual
signature, a product's exact appearance, a character model trained on a person's face — and **neither mentions
consent, disclosure or likeness at all.** Course 802 carries a narrower disclaimer about brands
(*"try to only do this on your brand"*) and nothing about people. ⭐ **This course is where the missing half
lives.**

## ⭐ What an AI agent actually is

The definition is worth using precisely, because the word is applied to everything.

> "**An AI agent is not just an LLM responding to your prompts or a single workflow automated with AI.** It's
> **a self-operating system that works towards a goal**, like improving your performance, using a series of
> intelligent steps."

**His analogy:** *"compare it to a human-like agent, one that has been **given a clear mission** to complete
something you want."*

⭐ **The four verbs, which are the actual test:**

> "**It observes** what's happening, **makes decisions**, **executes tasks** across different platforms, using
> different tools, **and learns from outcomes.**"

⛔ **A pipeline that does not learn from outcomes is an automation.** Course 803 builds exactly such pipelines
and correctly calls system prompts *"the precursor to agent"* rather than agents themselves. **The two courses
agree, and neither cites the other.**

⚠️ **His productivity claim:** *"I believe AI agents can truly **enable a person to do work of a 10 or
more**."* ⛔ **Unsourced, and stated as belief** — he says *"I believe"*. Carry it as opinion.

## ⭐ The four-step agentic workflow

The course's best teaching device: each step shown as it is done today, then as an agent would do it.

### Step 1: Data retrieval and pattern recognition

| | |
|---|---|
| **Before AI** | *"you dig through Meta Ads Manager or TikTok Ads to check CTR ... watch time cost per result. **You make a manual report and then compare across creatives.**"* |
| ⭐ **With an agent** | *"could get to work **every morning at 6 a.m.** to pull performance data from all active campaigns"*, then *"**identifies patterns** such as at fatigue, drop-off points in video or platform-specific trends"*, then *"**flags anomalies** of top performance and **ranks the creative elements** that are driving or hurting performance and **delivers those insights in your inbox before you wake up**"* |

**His worked example:** *"AI flags that your 15-second TikTok ad has 65% drop-off rate in the first two
seconds. It highlights that **the hook used a static visual** and recommends testing motion-based openers."*

⚠️ **That 65% is an illustration, not a measurement.** ⚠️ *"at fatigue"* is *ad fatigue*.

### Step 2: Creative iteration

| | |
|---|---|
| **Before AI** | *"you manually brief a creative team. Can we change this call to action on this? Try a new hook, add sharper captions."* |
| **With an agent** | It takes *"yesterday's low-performing video"* and generates variants: new hooks *"from trending keywords and comments from later customer reviews"*, bold text overlays, swapped music *"to align with the trending sounds on best performing assets"*, ⭐ *"trims intros of videos by 1.5 seconds based on retention data"*, and a new CTA *"based on an upcoming event"* |

⭐ **And the end state:** *"This variants are then put on a line for testing **with no human brief needed**"*.

⚠️ *"emotion-tricking visuals"* appears in the transcript; the sense is *emotion-triggering*.

### Step 3: Autonomous deployment

| | |
|---|---|
| **Before AI** | *"you would upload assets to TikTok, set up split tests manually, and adjust budget distribution"* |
| **With an agent** | It pushes variants into the ad platforms, *"set up an automated split test based on campaign goals"*, and *"**adjust bidding strategy or budget allocation based on early performance signals**"* |

**His worked example:** *"The AI deploys three variants at 8am. By noon, it notices that version 2 is
outperfalling by 30% on CTR and **shifts more budgets towards it, pausing the rest.**"*

⚠️ *"outperfalling"* is *outperforming*. ⚠️ **The 30% is illustrative.**

⛔ **This is the step to question hardest.** Budget reallocation by noon on a CTR signal is a media-buying
decision made on an engagement metric, and Course 402 is explicit that CTR does not predict conversion.
**Reading this step as a recommendation rather than an illustration would be a mistake.**

### Step 4: Real-time feedback loop

> "**With every cycle, the AI would get smarter.** It learns **which hooks resonate** with specific audio
> segments, **which format converts better** across platforms, and **what frequency or ad-fatic thresholds to
> avoid.**"

⚠️ *"ad-fatic"* is *ad-fatigue*. ⚠️ *"audio segments"* is likely *audience segments*.

⭐ **The one concrete cadence in the course:**

> "you could set up your agent to **refresh static creators every 4 days and videos every 7-10 days based on
> creative decay curves**"

⚠️ *"static creators"* is *static creatives*.

⛔ **These numbers collide with three other courses.** Course 210 gives its own refresh thresholds, Course 602
says a new creative *"only performs at its peak for a few weeks"*, and Course 702 claims *"only 10% of ads
last more than two weeks"*. ⛔ **Four different answers, none sourced, none cross-referenced.** If a real
refresh cadence is needed, derive it from the account's own decay data rather than picking one.

⭐ **His honest close on the whole section:** *"We are still in **very early days, where most of the use cases
for agents are yet to be discovered**."*

## ⭐ The role change

The question the course is really answering.

> "You might ask, **if AI does all this, what's my role as a creative strategist?**"

> "History is full of evolution of work. **This is the industrial revolution of creative industries, where you
> move from task executor to a systems architect and a creative director.**"

**The four responsibilities that replace the old ones:**

| # | What the job becomes |
|---|---|
| 1 | ⭐ **Set the right KPIs and constraints** |
| 2 | **Feed the AI with high-quality input data and reference content** |
| 3 | **Review AI's outputs for brand alignment and narrative cohesion** |
| 4 | ⭐ **Guide the AI system towards better marketing outcomes** |

Plus training it over time: *"the AI to understand your voice, style and strategy over time."*

⭐ **The line that compresses it:**

> "**The creative strangest of tomorrow doesn't write every script. They build the machine that writes
> hundreds.**"

⭐ **That sentence is Course 803's entire thesis and Course 702's argument for volume**, stated by a third
faculty member who cites neither. ⚠️ **Three independent arrivals at the same conclusion** is the strongest
form of agreement available in this programme, and none of the three courses notices it.

## ⭐ The three assignments

The course sets homework, and it is genuinely good homework. **Use these when someone asks where to start.**

| # | The assignment | ⭐ Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *"**Develop your personal AI stack** with the tools you want to test"* | Starts from tools you choose, not a prescribed list |
| 2 | *"**Think about what part of your current workflow could be automated today.** Write down"* your ideas *"and tomorrow, start exploring, **has someone built an agent to do it for you?**"* | ⭐ Starts from the work, not the technology — and checks whether the thing already exists before building |
| 3 | ⭐ **Build an AI guideline** | See below. The most useful of the three |

### ⭐ Assignment 3: the AI guideline

> "Build an AI guideline and test it with different AI modules. **Write down your brand tone of voice with
> examples. Give it your best performing hooks from past campaigns. Give it do's and don'ts for visual
> identity.**"

⭐ **And the reason, which is the best single sentence in the course:**

> "**This ensures AI works within your sandbox and not outside of it.**"

⭐ **This is the same artifact Course 803 calls a Visual Brand Profile**, reached from a different direction:
Jesse's version adds tone of voice and proven hooks to the visual half. ⛔ **Neither course cites the other**,
and together they describe a more complete guardrail than either does alone.

⚠️ *"AI modules"* is the transcript's rendering of *AI models*.
