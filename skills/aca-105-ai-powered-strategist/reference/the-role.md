# The role: where it came from and why brands need it

## The origin

Reza dates the role precisely, and the reason it appeared is a shift in where the money was.

> "if you go back just a few years, maybe like to 2020 and 2021, **I don't think anyone even knew
> what a creative strategist was,** right? Like it was a brand new role."

Before that, for most of digital marketing's history, targeting was the lever:

> "Probably the thing that was the most important when it came to revenue impact was around audience
> targeting. So if I'm if I'm a yoga brand and I can figure out how to get in front of yoga
> enthusiasts, that was probably the biggest revenue unlock."

Then two things happened at once. iOS 14 removed a lot of the data, **and** the algorithms got good
enough that targeting stopped being a competitive edge anyway:

> "the even bigger shift that happened was that the distribution algorithms just got really good at
> targeting. Right? So up until that point, if so much time and energy went towards targeting the
> right person, and now all of a sudden the algorithms are just really good at doing that. They
> basically opened up this big question of, okay, well **where's the big revenue impact?**"

> "And then everyone very immediately understood that **creative was where the big revenue unlock
> was**."

⚠️ Note the second half of that. The usual telling is "privacy changes broke targeting". Reza's
version is that the algorithms also got good enough to do it themselves, which matters because it
means the shift is not reversible by better data. Targeting stopped being where the advantage lives
for two independent reasons.

## The bridge

The role appeared to solve a specific organisational problem: two groups who both mattered and did
not speak the same language.

> "if you take the media average media buyer at that time, they had like a million spreadsheets and
> they were huge data nerds. And then you had the creative team all the way on the other side. That
> was just making stuff.... **that was not rooted in actual decisions from the data.** And so
> creative strategists emerges as the bridge between these two worlds."

Who filled it:

> "these are the individuals who, like, naturally, were pretty good at being part analytical, part
> creative. We all we all know people like that, whether they started out on the media buyer side or
> on the creative side. These were the types of people that were like **almost unicorns,** a little
> bit in the sense that they had a bit of both"

**This is the frame that makes the AI argument work.** The role was always a hybrid, formed by
someone pulling two disciplines together. Adding a third is a continuation, not a mutation.

> "In some ways, it's just a continuation of that and saying, okay, these, these almost like
> **polymath type individuals** need to figure out, okay, now how do I work AI into the mix as
> well?"

## Why a brand needs it

Paid advertising is not optional for an online business:

> "if you're selling anything online, paid advertising is going to be like absolute critical channel
> to get right. And so from that standpoint, getting paid advertising as a channel is basically a
> non-negotiable for an online brand."

And the biggest component of that channel is the creative:

> "The biggest component to that is your **creative flywheel,** right. Because at this point, if
> you're spending anything north of like even 20, 30, 40 or $50,000 a month in media spend, the
> amount of creative that you need to run that kind of a budget is pretty intense."

## In-house or agency

Reza is not anti-agency, and the distinction he draws is the useful part:

> "in the early days, like, you might just go and hire an agency, and I think you can still do that
> today. And I think that's fine. There are a lot of terrific agencies, but there are some things
> that feel like they need to be core competency. You know, they **they say that** you **you want to
> outsource execution, but strategy and direction**"

And having it in-house makes external partners better, rather than redundant:

> "even when you work with content creators or, performance agencies, you will set them up to do
> even better work, right? REZA Because they, they would love to work with a brand that has that
> kind of expertise"

## ⭐ The title does not matter, the work does

The most practically useful thing in this section, especially for a small brand deciding whether to
hire:

> "You don't have to have the role of creative strategists. I think about at a certain organization
> size, it makes sense to hire that full time. **At least someone at the team has to wear the hat of
> creative strategist.** REZA **It could be the media buyer, could be the founder, could be the head
> of marketing. Actually, it doesn't really matter who does it, but the work does need to be done,**
> and it should be done in-house at the brand,"

Two ways this gets used:

- **A brand too small to hire** does not get a pass. Someone still has to do the work, and naming
  who is the decision that matters.
- **Someone whose job title is something else** can make the case that they are already doing this
  work, which is often the honest description of a media buyer or marketing lead at a growing
  brand.

## Why the bar moved

Context for anyone who remembers an easier era:

> "those early days of like, I'm going to open up an online store, run some ads, do some
> dropshipping, and make a ton of money, like **those days are obviously gone.** REZA And the reason
> is that the market has matured. There's a lot of there's a lot of people doing that. **The bar has
> gone up.**"

> "You're up against a ton of really good performance marketers."

This is the commercial argument for the whole discipline: the work is now hard enough that doing it
well is a genuine advantage, which is precisely why it justifies a dedicated person.
