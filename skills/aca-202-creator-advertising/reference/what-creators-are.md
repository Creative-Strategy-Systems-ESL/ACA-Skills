# What creators are, and what they are for

Lessons 1 and 2.

## The definitions this course uses

**A creator** is a person who makes content a brand can use in a marketing or advertising context.
That covers a UGC creator, an influencer, an expert, a customer, a founder, an employee, or a
professional creator who makes content for brands.

**Creator content** is what that person makes for the brand. It might show the creator on camera or
only the product; it might explain, demonstrate, build trust, create relevance, or drive an action.

**"Creator content" is the umbrella term throughout**, chosen deliberately because the other words
are contested.

## People-led advertising is not new

> "Brands have always, always, always used people to make products feel more relatable, credible,
> desirable, or easier to understand. Think about spokespeople, testimonials, celebrities, brand
> ambassadors,"

What changed is **how it gets made, where it shows up, and how many jobs it can do** inside a
brand's advertising system. The same idea now appears as a TikTok-style demo, an Instagram
influencer post, a founder video, a customer testimonial.

## ⛔ Three words worth settling before anything else

The language is genuinely broken, and it costs money because two people agree while meaning
different things.

> "A client says we need UGC. Someone else says we need influencers and someone else says, can't we
> just find someone with like a phone? Someone else says, let's just post it first and see if it
> picks up. And suddenly everyone thinks they were talking about the same thing, but they are not."

### UGC — source or style?

**Originally:** user-generated content. Real customers making content unprompted and unpaid.

**In practice today:** a *style*. Casual, self-shot, native to the platform, less polished, less
obviously brand-produced.

⭐ **So UGC can mean where the content came from, or what it looks like, and the distinction
matters.** Paid creator content can look like UGC without being user-generated in the original
sense.

### Influencer — influence over whom?

The word smuggles in an assumption: that this person can influence an audience to buy, try or care.

> "An influencer can have a million followers and influence no one to care about your product.
> Another creator can have 10,000 followers, be deeply trusted inside a niche community and sell the
> heck out of your product."

**The better question is not how many followers.** It is *"influence over whom, in what context, and
for what purpose?"*

### Organic — distribution or style?

⚠️ **The one that causes the most damage**, because both meanings are common:

- **Organic distribution** — posted without paid media behind it. Whatever reach it gets, it gets
  from the platform.
- **Organic style** — feels natural, human, casual, native, not overly produced. It belongs in the
  feed.

> "A video can look organic, but be built very intentionally for paid media. In fact, a lot of great
> creator advertising is exactly that. It looks native and human, but it is still planned, briefed,
> edited, contract and approved and distributed as advertising."

⭐ **When a client says "we want organic creator content", ask which they mean.** *"Do they mean
organic distribution with no paid media, or do they mean organic style? Those are completely
different strategies."*

# The eight creator jobs

The framework the rest of the course runs on. What creator content can do that a brand struggles to
do on its own.

Ashley's framing of the competition: in a feed, *"your ad is not just competing with other ads, it
is competing with someone's friends, vacation, some delicious food, a cat falling off the sofa, and
my personal favorite grown men chopping wood."*

| # | Job | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Attention** | A format, tone or opening that feels native to the platform, so people are less likely to scroll away |
| 2 | **Explanation** | Making something complicated feel simpler, clearer, less corporate |
| 3 | **Demonstration** | Showing the product used, opened, tested, tasted, styled, compared, assembled, packed, cleaned, cooked with, travelled with. *"Sometimes showing is much more powerful than just explaining"* |
| 4 | **Trust** | From expertise, experience, category knowledge, community trust, or simply that it is not the brand saying it about itself |
| 5 | **Relevance** | Showing how the product fits a specific moment, audience, culture, market, lifestyle or use case. Makes it less abstract |
| 6 | **Objection handling** | Too expensive · don't understand how it works · don't believe the claim · need proof. Addressed in a more human way |
| 7 | **Variety** | Different hooks, people, tones, formats, use cases, objections and reasons to care — instead of one polished brand film trying to do everything |
| 8 | **Learning** | Which angles, messages, formats, product moments and creator styles people respond to. Makes the next round smarter |

## ⚠️ All eight are written from an acquisition posture

Every one of these describes reaching a stranger: `so people are less likely to scroll away`,
makes the next round smarter. **There is no job here about someone who has already bought.**

That is a real blind spot, and it bites on a specific and common brief: **a product people misuse,
return, or abandon after purchase.** A return-rate problem, an onboarding problem, a
churn-after-first-renewal problem — the course has no job that names any of them, and a brief filled
straight from this list produces a clean acquisition plan that drives more traffic to the same
problem and hits every metric while making things worse.

⛔ **The jobs still work; the framing has to be redirected.** A post-purchase problem is a **second
need state** attached to a **second objective**, and the same eight jobs apply to it:

| The post-purchase problem | The job that addresses it |
|---|---|
| They use it wrong and blame the product | **Explanation** — but it has to land *before* purchase, not in the box |
| They do not believe it is working | **Demonstration** of what "working" looks like, including when it is undramatic |
| They regret the price after buying | **Objection handling**, aimed at the post-purchase moment |
| They stop before the benefit arrives | **Trust** — someone who stuck with it saying how long it took |

⭐ **The timing point is the one that matters most and the course never makes it.** If the fix is
"you are using it wrong", the content has to reach them *before checkout*. By the time they read the
leaflet they have had three bad days and the return is already decided.

## ⚠️ These jobs are not funnel stages

The most useful caveat in the lesson, and the easiest to get wrong:

> "A testimonial is not automatically awareness. A product demo does not mean automatically it's consideration. A creator handling a price objection is not now bottom of the funnel."  ⭐ **These are creative jobs, not rigid measurement buckets.**

What determines the job is the brand problem, the content, the distribution, and what you are trying
to learn or achieve.

## Why creator content is not a shortcut

> "creator content does not automatically make better. Adds a bad creator ad is still a bad ad. An
> authentic looking video with no clear brand or message is still unclear. A famous creator making
> boring content is still the wrong choice, and a creator reading a script that sounds like it was
> written by the client's legal team. Well, it will not magically be authentic because they made a
> vertical video."

**Creator content is a creative tool inside a brand's advertising system.** Not a magic wand, and
not a substitute for strategy.
