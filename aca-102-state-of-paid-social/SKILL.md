# Course 102: State of Paid Social Advertising & Consumer Psychology

Mirella Crespi (Creative Milkshake), 27m 13s, 2 modules: The New Paid Social Reality, and
Data-Driven Creative Strategy.

This is the course that explains **why the strategist role exists** and then hands over the
psychology used to decide what an ad says. It ends in an artifact: a filled Consumer Psychology
Worksheet.

## What this skill needs from you

This skill teaches first and asks second. If the question is "how do awareness stages work",
answer it, no inputs needed. The inputs below are for **producing the worksheet**.

**Required, to produce the worksheet:**

1. **The product or service**, and what it actually does
2. **Who it is for**
3. **What is known about them** — research, reviews, support tickets, sales calls, or an honest
   best read

**Optional, and it sharpens the artifact:**

4. Current messaging or existing ads
5. Price point and the offer
6. What competitors are saying

⚠️ **If a required input is missing, ask for it.** Do not invent an audience or a pain point. A
plausible invented input produces a plausible wrong worksheet, and nobody downstream can tell it
was fabricated. An honest "we're guessing here" from the user is a usable input, because it travels
with a warning label. A guess the skill made silently does not.

### Three states, and the middle one is the trap

**1. They supplied the research.** Work from it. Quote it.

**2. They NAMED a source but did not supply it** — "we have 400 Amazon reviews", "there's a bunch
of Instagram comments", "sales talks to these people daily". ⚠️ **This is not the same as having
the research, and it is the easiest case to get wrong**, because the sentence sounds like evidence
was provided. Nothing has been provided. Ask for a bounded sample rather than the whole corpus:

> Paste 20 to 30 review lines, weighted towards the critical ones and the ones that repeat, plus any
> themes you have already noticed. That is enough to ground Sections 1 and 2.

Asking for all 400 gets refused; asking for a specific, achievable slice gets an answer. Skewing
the request towards negative and repeated reviews is deliberate: complaints name the pain point in
the customer's own words, which is exactly what Section 2 needs.

**3. They genuinely have no research.** Say what would be needed, then offer to proceed on
assumptions **explicitly marked as assumptions in the worksheet itself**.

### What may be reasoned and what must be supplied

The line worth holding, because it decides whether a flagged draft is useful or misleading:

| Fair to reason towards, flagged | Must come from the user |
|---|---|
| How this product category generally behaves | What **this** brand's customers actually say |
| Which awareness stage a category typically sits at | Any specific number, claim, timeframe or result |
| Which emotional drivers a category usually touches | Testimonial content, review quotes, named benefits |
| Structural advice: which angle suits which stage | Anything a competitor could sue over or a customer could disprove |

General market reasoning is a legitimate starting draft. **Brand-specific invention is not**, even
flagged, because a fabricated quote or an invented "3x faster" survives copy-paste long after the
flag falls off.

## Why this course exists

Before the 2020-2021 privacy changes, targeting was the lever. Rich third-party pixel data made
granular lookalikes, exclusions and time-windowed retargeting possible, and the algorithm put the
ad in front of the right person. In Mirella's words, you did not even need great ads.

When that data went away, targeting went broad and the algorithm began reading first-party in-app
signals instead: whether someone stops scrolling, watches, engages, shares, clicks. Those signals
are produced by the creative.

> "Creative has become your targeting."

That is the whole argument, and everything downstream follows from it. If the creative is the
targeting mechanism, then deciding what the creative *says* is the highest-leverage decision
available, which is what the rest of this course equips.

The frameworks are only worth anything applied. "Your audience is problem aware" is not an

## Awareness and market sophistication

The first layer of the stack, and the one that constrains everything after it. Awareness decides
what a message can attempt; sophistication decides whether the way you say it will still land.

### The 5 stages of market awareness

Mirella maps these to funnel position, which is the part people usually drop and the part that
makes them actionable.

#### TOP OF FUNNEL

**1. Unaware** — they do not even realise they have a problem or a need.

Tactical approach:
- Focus on raising awareness of the problem itself
- Use pattern interrupts and curiosity triggers
- Lead with stories that illuminate an unrecognised pain point

> Example: *"The hidden reason you're exhausted even after 8 hours of sleep"*

**2. Problem Aware** — they recognise the problem but are not sure how to solve it.

Tactical approach:
- Validate their experience: "yes, this is real, and you're not alone"
- Educate about the problem's true nature and implications
- Introduce broader solution categories **without pitching your specific product yet**

#### MIDDLE OF FUNNEL

**3. Solution Aware** — they know solutions exist, but not yours specifically.

Tactical approach:
- Differentiate your approach from the alternatives
- Focus on unique mechanisms or methodologies
- Highlight what makes your solution category superior

> Example: *"Why natural supplements outperform synthetic options for sustainable energy"*

**4. Product Aware** — they know your product but have not decided it is right for them.

Tactical approach:
- Address specific objections
- Provide detailed proof: studies, testimonials, demonstrations
- Compare directly to the alternatives they are considering

#### BOTTOM OF FUNNEL

**5. Most Aware** — they are ready to buy with the right offer.

Tactical approach:
- Focus on urgency, scarcity, or special offers
- Reduce friction to purchase
- Reinforce decision confidence with guarantees or bonuses

### Using the stages

The worksheet's Section 1 asks you to place your audience on this spectrum and then derive the
messaging approach from that placement. The condensed version, as the worksheet states it:

| Stage | Focus on |
|---|---|
| Unaware | Problem identification, disruption, curiosity |
| Problem Aware | Problem validation, education, hope |
| Solution Aware | Differentiation, unique mechanism, comparison |
| Product Aware | Objection handling, proof, specific benefits |
| Most Aware | Offers, urgency, easy action steps |

**The most common failure this framework catches** is a mismatch: differentiation copy aimed at
someone who does not yet know they have the problem, or problem-education copy aimed at someone
already comparing two brands. Both read as competent writing and both underperform, because the
message is answering a question the reader has not asked yet or has already moved past.

**A second thing worth noticing.** A single audience is rarely at one stage. Broad targeting means
one ad set can contain people at all five. This is part of why a portfolio of creative
outperforms one perfect ad: different creatives catch people at different stages.

### Market sophistication

Awareness is about the *person*. Sophistication is about the *market*: how many times this
audience has already heard a claim like yours.

As a market matures, straightforward claims stop working, not because they became untrue but
because they became familiar. Everyone in the category is making them, so they no longer carry
information.

Mirella names two escapes:

1. **Present a unique mechanism.** *"How our proprietary technology works differently."* You are
   not making a bigger claim, you are explaining a *different reason* the claim is true. The
   mechanism is what makes a tired promise credible again.
2. **Reframe the problem entirely.** *"You've been solving the wrong problem all along."* This
   moves the conversation rather than competing inside it, and it can reset an audience's
   awareness stage in the process.

The AI prompt for this (Prompt 3, Market Sophistication Analysis) invokes **Eugene Schwartz's 5
levels of market sophistication** and asks which claims are becoming cliché, which is the
diagnostic question. If every competitor ad in the category makes the same promise, the promise
is spent, and volume will not revive it.

### How the two interact

Awareness tells you **what to say**. Sophistication tells you **how hard you have to work to be
believed when you say it**.

A problem-aware audience in an unsophisticated market can be reached with a plain statement of the
problem. The same audience in a saturated market has heard that statement a hundred times, and
needs either a mechanism or a reframe before the same content will register at all.

## Emotional drivers

The second layer. Awareness tells you what the person can hear; this tells you what they actually
want underneath the purchase.

### Why feelings before features

The course's position is that people do not buy features, they buy the feeling a feature produces.
A feature is evidence for a feeling; on its own it is a specification. The practical consequence is
that the emotional driver should be chosen **before** the copy is written, because the driver
determines which features are even worth mentioning.

Two frameworks are used to name the driver.

### Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

Five levels, from the base up:

1. **Physiological** — survival basics
2. **Safety** — security, stability, protection
3. **Belonging** — love, connection, community
4. **Esteem** — respect, recognition, status, achievement
5. **Self-actualization** — fulfilment, growth, creativity, purpose

Maslow is the coarse cut. It is useful for locating roughly which register a product operates in,
and less useful for writing a specific line, which is what the Life-Force 8 is for.

### The Life-Force 8

Eight drives described as hard-wired rather than learned. Each carries the explanation as the
course gives it, because the explanation is what makes the drive usable.

**1. Survival, enjoyment of life, life extension**
Products promising health, vitality, or safety tap this drive.

**2. Enjoyment of food and beverages**
We are programmed to seek pleasurable taste experiences.

**3. Freedom from fear, pain, and danger**
People spend heavily to eliminate worry, whether financial, physical or emotional.

**4. Sexual companionship**
Beyond romance, this includes feeling desirable and attractive.

**5. Comfortable living conditions**
Products that make life easier, more efficient, or more luxurious.

**6. To be superior; winning**
Appeals to status, achievement, and recognition.

**7. Care and protection of loved ones**
Particularly powerful for parents, pet owners, and caregivers.

**8. Social approval**
We crave acceptance and respect from our peers.

#### Using them without flattening the product

Most products touch several drives. The worksheet asks you to check all that apply, then identify
**the primary** transformation, and the second step is the one that matters. A message aimed at
three drives at once usually reads as aimed at none.

The tie-breaker worth applying: which drive explains why they would buy **now**, rather than
agreeing that the product is nice in principle. Freedom from fear and care of loved ones tend to
produce urgency; comfortable living conditions and social approval tend to produce agreement.

### The emotional transformation

The frame the worksheet uses to turn a driver into copy direction:

> **FROM:** [current negative emotion] → **TO:** [desired positive emotion]

And two supporting lists:

- What the product helps customers **avoid feeling**
- What the product helps customers **feel**

**Write these in the customer's language, not the brand's.** Research, reviews and support tickets
carry the actual words, and those words outperform any paraphrase, because they were produced by
someone in the state you are trying to name. "I'm tired of waking up tired" is worth more than
"combats fatigue".

**Emotions, not outcomes.** "From tired to energised" is an emotional transformation. "From no
subscription to subscribed" is a transaction, and it will not generate a hook. If the FROM state
reads like a description of not owning the product yet, it needs another pass.

### How this feeds the rest of the worksheet

The emotional driver chosen here is the raw material for:

- **Section 4, the message framing matrix** — the same driver expressed as a benefit, a pain
  avoidance, a status claim, a mechanism, and a story
- **Section 5, hook development** — hooks that trigger the driver, pitched at the right awareness
  stage
- **Section 6, sequencing** — the emotional trigger column, exposure by exposure

Getting the driver wrong is expensive, because everything downstream inherits the error and each
piece will look internally consistent.

## The persuasion principles

The third layer. Awareness says what they can hear, emotional drivers say what they want, these
say what makes them act.

⚠️ **The course teaches 14. The worksheet's selection grid offers 10.** Work from the 14. The four
the worksheet omits are named at the bottom of this file, so anyone working from the printed
worksheet alone can see what is missing rather than assuming the grid is the whole set.

### Cialdini's seven

**1. Reciprocity**
Giving something valuable first. The obligation to return a favour is close to universal, and it
is why free tools, samples and genuinely useful content outperform straight asks.

**2. Commitment and consistency**
People strive to be consistent with their previous actions and statements. Small yeses lead to
bigger ones, which is the mechanism behind quizzes, low-friction first steps and micro-conversions.

**3. Social proof**
When uncertain, people look to others' actions for guidance. Strongest when the others are
recognisably *like them*, which is why "250,000 customers" is weaker than "250,000 parents of
toddlers".

**4. Authority**
We trust experts and authority figures. Credentials, expertise and endorsement transfer confidence,
and they work fastest in categories where the buyer cannot easily judge quality themselves.

**5. Liking**
Establishing rapport and relatability. We say yes to people we like, and similarity, familiarity
and warmth all feed it. This is much of why creator-led ads work.

**6. Scarcity**
We value what is limited or potentially unavailable. Limited availability or limited time. It
converts and it is the easiest to abuse: manufactured scarcity that gets found out damages the
trust the other principles depend on.

**7. Unity**
We identify with and favour groups we feel part of. Not merely similarity but shared identity, a
sense of *us*. Tribal identity is the strongest and slowest-built of the seven.

### The seven the course adds

**8. MAYA — Most Advanced Yet Acceptable**
People are attracted to innovation but need familiarity to feel comfortable. The most novel thing
the audience will still accept outperforms both the safe version and the genuinely alien one.
Useful when a mechanism is unfamiliar: it has to feel new enough to matter and known enough to
trust.

**9. Loss aversion**
The pain of losing something outweighs the pleasure of gaining something equivalent. Framing the
same fact as what they will lose by not acting hits harder than what they will gain by acting.

**10. Anchoring**
Initial information creates a reference point that influences every judgement after it. Setting a
value reference before naming a price is the common application.

**11. Pratfall effect**
Minor flaws make strong claims more believable. Strategic vulnerability buys credibility, because
a claim with no admitted downside reads as advertising while a claim with one reads as honest.

**12. Distinctiveness**
What stands out gets remembered. This is a memory argument as much as a persuasion one: an ad that
is indistinguishable from the category cannot be recalled as belonging to you.

**13. Self-centeredness**
People care more about their own problems than your product. The corrective for copy that opens
with the company, the founding story or the technology instead of the reader's situation.

**14. Primacy and recency**
People remember the beginning and the end of a message most clearly. The middle is where things go
to be forgotten, which is why the hook and the CTA carry disproportionate weight in a short video.

### Choosing between them

The worksheet asks for **2 to 3 primary principles**, not all fourteen, and the constraint is the
point. An ad attempting every principle achieves none of them, because each needs room to actually
operate.

Some pairings reinforce, some fight:

- **Authority plus social proof** compound well: an expert says it *and* people like you agree.
- **Scarcity plus loss aversion** compound, and also compound the risk. Both push urgency, so
  overuse reads as pressure.
- **Pratfall plus authority** is a deliberate tension that works: admitting a real limitation makes
  the expertise more credible, not less.
- **Liking plus authority** can pull against each other. Warm and relatable sits awkwardly beside
  credentialed and expert, and the ad has to choose a register.

Pick the principles the *evidence* supports. Social proof without numbers, authority without
credentials and scarcity without real limits are all claims the audience can check, and being
caught costs more than the principle was worth.

### ⚠️ What the worksheet grid omits

The worksheet's Section 3 offers ten principles to select from: reciprocity, unity, commitment,
loss aversion, social proof, anchoring, authority, pratfall effect, liking and scarcity.

It does **not** list:

- **MAYA**
- **Distinctiveness**
- **Self-centeredness**
- **Primacy and recency**

They are taught in the course and are not on the sheet. Distinctiveness and self-centeredness in
particular are among the most commonly violated in practice, so their absence from the checklist
is worth knowing. If you are filling the printed worksheet, consider them anyway.

## Repetition and sequencing

The fourth layer, and the one that treats an ad as part of a sequence rather than a single event.
Someone rarely converts on first contact, so the question is not only what the ad says but what it
says **given that they have seen you before**.

### Three mechanics

#### 1. Same message, different formats

One core message expressed across multiple creative formats. The message repeats; the wrapper does
not. This buys frequency without the fatigue that comes from showing the identical asset again,
and it also hedges format preference, since the person who scrolls past a static may stop for the
same claim delivered as a talking head.

#### 2. Message sequencing

Deepen the narrative as users see more of your ads. The four-exposure structure the course gives:

| Exposure | Job |
|---|---|
| **1st** | Introduce the problem |
| **2nd** | Reveal your unique solution |
| **3rd** | Provide proof it works |
| **4th** | Present the offer with urgency |

This maps onto the awareness stages deliberately. The sequence is walking someone from problem
aware to most aware across four touches, rather than asking a single ad to do all four jobs at
once, which is what produces the overstuffed ad that tries to educate, differentiate, prove and
close in fifteen seconds.

**The practical read:** if your account only ever runs bottom-of-funnel offer creative, exposures 1
to 3 never happened, and the offer is landing on people who have no reason yet to care.

#### 3. Creative refreshing

Update visuals and headlines while maintaining the same core message. Distinct from sequencing: the
narrative does not advance, the surface changes so the asset stops feeling seen.

The distinction is worth holding, because "our ads are fatigued" gets treated as one problem when
it is usually two. If the message is right and the asset is stale, refresh. If the audience has
already accepted the message and not acted, refreshing the visual changes nothing, and the sequence
needs to advance instead.

### Section 6 of the worksheet

The sequencing plan is a table, one row per exposure:

| Exposure | Awareness target | Key message | Emotional trigger | Call to action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | | | | |
| 2nd | | | | |
| 3rd | | | | |
| 4th | | | | |

Four columns, and each pulls from an earlier layer: the awareness target from Section 1, the
emotional trigger from Section 2, the key message from the framing matrix in Section 4. The
sequencing plan is where the whole stack gets assembled into something a media buyer can act on.

**Fill it left to right, row by row.** The awareness target for each exposure should advance, and
if rows 1 and 4 have the same awareness target the sequence is not sequencing, it is repeating.

### Why repetition works here specifically

Broad targeting means the algorithm decides who sees what, based on engagement signals. A person
who engages with exposure 1 becomes more likely to receive exposure 2. The sequence is therefore
not a fixed schedule you control, it is a set of messages the system will deliver in roughly this
order to people who keep responding.

That is why each ad still has to work standing alone. Someone may enter at exposure 3. The sequence
shapes the portfolio; it cannot be a story that only makes sense read in order.

### Section 4 — the message framing matrix

One core message, five ways to say it.

The point is **not** five different claims. It is one claim tested five ways, so that when one
outperforms you have learned something about the audience rather than about which of five ideas
happened to be best.

**Settle the core message first.** It comes from Section 2's emotional transformation: what the
product moves someone away from and towards. If the core message is not written down before the
matrix is filled, the five angles drift into five products.

The five frames:

| Frame | Answers | Pulls from |
|---|---|---|
| **Benefit** | What do I get? | The "TO" side of the transformation |
| **Pain avoidance** | What do I stop suffering? | The "FROM" side |
| **Status / identity** | Who does this make me? | Social approval, to be superior, esteem |
| **Process / mechanism** | Why does it work? | Market sophistication: the mechanism escape |
| **Testimonial / story** | Who else has done this? | Social proof |

Two checks on a finished matrix:

- **Swap test.** Could a competitor put their name on any of these lines unchanged? If yes, that
  frame is describing the category, not the product.
- **Same-claim test.** Read all five aloud. They should feel like one argument from five angles. If
  they feel like five arguments, the core message was never settled.

The mechanism frame is the one people skip and the one that matters most in a saturated market,
because it is the escape from sophistication described in `awareness-and-sophistication.md`.

### Section 5 — hook development

Three hooks, one per awareness band, because the hook is where the awareness assessment either pays
off or is wasted.

| Audience | The hook's job |
|---|---|
| **Unaware / problem aware** | Create curiosity, or name the problem they have not articulated |
| **Solution aware / product aware** | Differentiate. Why this, not the alternative they are considering |
| **Most aware** | Urgency or offer. They are convinced; remove friction |

**Every hook should be traceable to Sections 1 and 2.** A hook that does not follow from the
awareness stage and the emotional driver may still be a good line, but it is not the output of this
process and should not be presented as though the research produced it.

#### Two hook menus from ACA's prompt pack

Use these when a hook will not come, or to force variety past the two or three shapes everyone
defaults to.

**Ten psychological approaches** (Prompt 5): fear of missing out · curiosity gap · identity
reinforcement · unexpected statement or statistic · problem agitation · dream scenario · pattern
interrupt · status elevation · loss aversion · before/after contrast

**Ten structural approaches** (Prompt 12): question-based · statistic-based · story-based ·
identity-based · pain-point · curiosity · counterintuitive statement · bold promise ·
future-pacing · exclusivity

The two lists cut differently: the first is *what the hook does to the viewer*, the second is
*what shape the sentence takes*. A useful pass is to pick one from each, which produces
combinations the obvious approach misses (a statistic-based pattern interrupt reads differently
from a question-based one).

⚠️ **Hooks are the part most likely to drift into invention.** A hook containing a specific claim
(a number, a timeframe, a result) needs that claim to be real. If the research does not support it,
either soften it to something the product can stand behind, or mark it as needing verification
before it ships. A hook that tests well and cannot be substantiated is worse than one that never
ran.

### Section 7 — the evaluation checklist

Ten questions, run against **finished creative**, not against the plan.

That distinction is the whole value. Ticking ten boxes on a worksheet proves nothing; the checklist
exists to catch the drift between what was planned and what actually got made, which is where most
of the loss happens.

The ten:

1. Does it clearly address the right awareness level?
2. Does it tap into at least one powerful emotional driver?
3. Does it emphasise transformation, not just features?
4. Does it leverage 2 to 3 persuasion principles effectively?
5. Is the hook strong enough to stop the scroll?
6. Is the primary benefit immediately clear?
7. Would I be excited to see this ad if I were the target customer?
8. Have I created pattern interrupts or distinctive elements?
9. Does it address objections the audience might have?
10. Is the call-to-action clear and compelling?

**Question 7 is the one that catches what the others miss.** The other nine can all pass on an ad
that is technically correct and lifeless. It is also the one most easily answered dishonestly, so
it is worth answering as the customer rather than as the person who made it.

Note that questions 1 to 4 are direct checks against Sections 1, 2 and 3 of the worksheet. If the
worksheet was filled honestly, they are quick. If they are hard to answer, the usual cause is that
the creative drifted from the strategy rather than that the checklist is difficult.

---

### Where the three sections fail

- **Section 4 fails** when the core message was never settled, producing five unrelated ideas that
  cannot be compared.
- **Section 5 fails** when hooks are written first, from instinct, and the awareness assessment is
  back-filled to justify them.
- **Section 7 fails** when it is run against the plan instead of the creative, or when question 7
  is answered by the author rather than imagined from the customer's side.

## The Creative Strategist's Consumer Psychology Worksheet

The artifact this course produces. Seven sections to fill, run in order because each depends on the
ones above it, plus a Section 8 of ready-made formulas that is reference rather than a section to
complete.

**Output it as filled sections with headings, not as prose.** The worksheet is meant to be handed
to someone else, referred back to when writing creative, and argued with. Prose loses that.

⚠️ **Mark every section that rests on an assumption rather than on something the user supplied.**
A worksheet with three grounded sections and one flagged guess is useful. One where the reader
cannot tell the difference is worse than useless, because it launders a guess into a decision.

**Put the flag INSIDE the section, not in a note around it.** Open every filled section with one
of these lines, so the provenance survives being copy-pasted into a deck or a brief:

```
GROUNDED IN: [what the user supplied — e.g. 34 Amazon review quotes, Oct 2025]
```
```
⚠️ ASSUMPTION, NOT VERIFIED: [what was assumed, and what would confirm it]
```

A flag that lives in the conversation around the worksheet is gone the moment someone copies the
worksheet. A flag on line one of the section travels with it.

---

### Section 1 — Audience Awareness Assessment

Place the target audience on the awareness spectrum.

| Stage | They are | Focus on |
|---|---|---|
| **Unaware** | They don't know they have a problem | Problem identification, disruption, curiosity |
| **Problem Aware** | They know the problem but not the solution | Problem validation, education, hope |
| **Solution Aware** | They know solutions exist but not your specific solution | Differentiation, unique mechanism, comparison |
| **Product Aware** | They know your product but haven't purchased | Objection handling, proof, specific benefits |
| **Most Aware** | They're ready to buy with the right offer | Offers, urgency, easy action steps |

**Fill:** `Based on this assessment, my primary messaging approach should be: ______`

State *why* the audience sits where it does, citing the evidence. "Problem aware, because reviews
and support tickets describe the symptom repeatedly but almost never name a product category"
is an assessment. "Problem aware" alone is a guess wearing a label.

---

### Section 2 — Emotional Drivers Analysis

Which core human needs does the product address?

**Maslow's Hierarchy** — check all that apply:
- [ ] Physiological (basic survival needs)
- [ ] Safety (security, stability, protection)
- [ ] Belonging (connection, relationships, community)
- [ ] Esteem (recognition, status, respect, achievement)
- [ ] Self-actualization (growth, creativity, purpose)

**Life-Force 8** — check all that apply:
- [ ] Survival, enjoyment of life, life extension
- [ ] Enjoyment of food and beverages
- [ ] Freedom from fear, pain, and danger
- [ ] Sexual companionship
- [ ] Comfortable living conditions
- [ ] To be superior; winning
- [ ] Care and protection of loved ones
- [ ] Social approval

**Then narrow to one transformation:**

> The primary emotional transformation my product delivers is:
> **FROM:** [current negative emotion] → **TO:** [desired positive emotion]

> My product helps customers **avoid feeling:** [list negative emotions]
> My product helps customers **feel:** [list positive emotions]

Use the customer's own words where the research has them.

| Principle | Is | How I'll use it |
|---|---|---|
| Reciprocity | Giving something valuable first | |
| Unity | Creating tribal identity | |
| Commitment | Small yeses leading to bigger ones | |
| Loss aversion | Focus on what they'll lose by not acting | |
| Social proof | Others like them are using it | |
| Anchoring | Setting value reference points | |
| Authority | Expert endorsement or credentials | |
| Pratfall effect | Strategic vulnerability for credibility | |
| Liking | Establishing rapport and relatability | |
| Scarcity | Limited availability or time | |

⚠️ The course teaches four more that this grid omits: **MAYA, distinctiveness, self-centeredness,
primacy and recency**. Consider them too.

### Section 5 — Hook Development

Hooks built from the awareness level and the emotional driver above.

- **For unaware / problem aware audiences:** [hook that creates curiosity or identifies the problem]
- **For solution / product aware audiences:** [hook that differentiates your approach]
- **For most aware audiences:** [hook that emphasises urgency or a special offer]

Each hook should be traceable to Sections 1 and 2. A hook that does not follow from the awareness
assessment and the emotional driver may still be a good line, but it is not the output of this
process and should not be presented as though it were.

For campaigns with multiple touchpoints.

### Section 7 — Creative Evaluation Checklist

Before finalising creative, verify:

- [ ] Does it clearly address the right awareness level?
- [ ] Does it tap into at least one powerful emotional driver?
- [ ] Does it emphasise transformation, not just features?
- [ ] Does it leverage 2 to 3 persuasion principles effectively?
- [ ] Is the hook strong enough to stop the scroll?
- [ ] Is the primary benefit immediately clear?
- [ ] Would I be excited to see this ad if I were the target customer?
- [ ] Have I created pattern interrupts or distinctive elements?
- [ ] Does it address objections the audience might have?
- [ ] Is the call-to-action clear and compelling?

**Areas for improvement:** ______

Run this against real creative, not against the worksheet. Ten ticks on a plan means nothing; the

---

### Section 8 — Winning Creative Formulas

Not a section to fill. Four ready-made structures printed at the bottom of the sheet, in Mirella's
words *"quick reference formulas that have proven effective across countless campaigns"*, there
for when a section stalls.

| Formula | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Problem → Agitation → Solution** | Identify a painful problem · Intensify the emotion around it · Present your solution as the relief |
| **Feature → Advantage → Benefit** | What it is · Why that matters · How it improves their life |
| **Before → After → Bridge** | Show the painful "before" state · Show the desirable "after" state · Position your product as the bridge |
| **Objection → Counter → Reframe** | Acknowledge their concern · Counter with new information · Reframe the perspective |

They map onto the sections above rather than replacing them. *Before → After → Bridge* is Section
2's transformation made into a script. *Feature → Advantage → Benefit* is the corrective for a
benefit frame in Section 4 that never got past the feature. *Objection → Counter → Reframe*
answers checklist item 9.

#### ⭐ How the worksheet ends, and it is not a formula

ACA closes the sheet on this, and it is the frame the whole course is meant to be read through:

> Great creative strategy balances psychological insight with authentic value. Your goal is not
> manipulation, but connecting people who have real problems with solutions that genuinely help
> them.

Mirella says the same thing closing the module: *"the goal isn't manipulation. It's connection.
You're using these principles to connect people who have real problems with solutions that will
genuinely improve their lives."*

Worth stating plainly when someone is handed fourteen persuasion principles and a hook menu. Every
technique here works on a product that does not deliver, right up until the refunds start.

## Worked examples

Mirella's two, showing how the layers combine. These are the shape of a good answer, not templates
to copy.

### Example 1 — Supplement brand

| Layer | Choice |
|---|---|
| Life-Force drive | Survival and life extension |
| Awareness level | Problem aware |
| Emotional trigger | Fear of decline, hope for vitality |
| Persuasion principles | Authority, scarcity, social proof |

Note the coherence: a problem-aware audience gets validation rather than differentiation, the
emotional trigger is a pair (a fear and its matching hope) rather than a single note, and the three
principles all serve credibility, which is what a health claim needs most.

### Example 2 — Productivity app

| Layer | Choice |
|---|---|
| Maslow level | Esteem needs (achievement, confidence) |
| Life-Force drive | To be superior; winning |
| Awareness level | Solution aware |
| Emotional trigger | Pride, ambition, fear of falling behind |
| Persuasion principles | Social proof, loss aversion, distinctiveness |

Here the audience already knows productivity tools exist, so the work is differentiation. Maslow
and the Life-Force drive agree with each other (esteem and winning), which is a good sign the
driver was correctly identified rather than picked from a list.

### Carrying an example through to the end

Mirella's two examples stop at the layer level, which shows how Sections 1 to 3 resolve but not
what Sections 4, 5 and 7 look like filled. Continuing the supplement brand through the rest of the
worksheet, to show the shape:

**Section 4, framing matrix.** Core message: *the tiredness you have accepted as normal is not
normal.*

| Frame | Line |
|---|---|
| Benefit | "Wake up before your alarm, because you actually slept" |
| Pain avoidance | "Stop planning your day around the 3pm crash" |
| Status / identity | "The people who seem to have endless energy are not lucky" |
| Process / mechanism | "Why magnesium timing matters more than magnesium dose" |
| Testimonial / story | "I thought I needed more coffee. I needed the opposite." |

All five are the same claim. The mechanism frame is doing the sophistication work.

**Section 5, hooks by awareness.**

- *Unaware / problem aware:* "You are not tired because you are busy."
- *Solution aware / product aware:* "Most magnesium supplements use the form your body absorbs
  worst."
- *Most aware:* "Your first month, and if it does nothing, keep it."

Each traces back: the first names the problem for someone who has normalised it, the second
differentiates on mechanism, the third removes friction for someone already convinced.

**Section 7, the gate.** Run against the finished ad, the question that catches this one is #3,
transformation not features. A cut that opens on capsule shots and absorption percentages fails it
even though the strategy behind it was sound, which is precisely the drift the checklist exists to
catch.

⚠️ Every line above is illustrative. Real versions of these need the brand's actual research
behind them, and the mechanism claim in particular would need to be true of the actual formulation
before it could ship.
