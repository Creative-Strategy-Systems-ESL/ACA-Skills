# The five-slide presentation, and turning findings into position

> "A simple 5-slide presentation template... perfect for when you need to share your competitor
> findings with clients or team members."

## The deck

| Slide | Contents |
|---|---|
| **1. Cover and scope** | The competitor list · **time period covered** · platforms examined |
| **2. Competitors 1 and 2** | Side by side: formats · angles · messaging · talent |
| **3. Competitors 3 and 4** | Same four rows |
| **4. Ad examples** | The creative itself, with the analysis attached |
| **5. Key patterns across competitors, and creative approaches** | Including **cross-category inspiration** |

**The four rows repeated on every competitor slide:**

| Row | What it holds |
|---|---|
| **Formats** | Primary ad formats used — video, carousel, static |
| **Angles** | Main selling angles and approaches |
| **Messaging** | Key value propositions and claims |
| **Talent** | Type of people featured and their demographics |

⭐ **Slide 1's "time period covered" is the honesty field.** It is what stops a reader treating a
snapshot as the permanent state of the category, and it is the field most likely to be skipped.

⚠️ **Slide 5 is where the deck earns its existence.** Slides 2 to 4 are inventory. The pattern across
competitors is the finding, and a deck that stops at slide 4 has described the category without
saying anything about it.

## The five strategic moves

The section the whole analysis exists to produce. Only the first two are about differentiating; the
rest are about borrowing well.

### 1. Identify white space

> "Look for angles, hooks, or claims that nobody in your space is making. Sometimes the most powerful
> position is the one everyone else has overlooked."

**How to actually find it:** list every angle observed across the set, then list the angles the
category *could* use and does not. The second list is the deliverable.

⚠️ **An empty space is not automatically an opportunity.** Sometimes nobody makes a claim because it
does not work, is not credible, or is not legal in the category. **Say which reading you think
applies**, because "nobody is doing X" presented as a recommendation is how a client ends up testing
something four competitors already abandoned.

### 2. Differentiate your positioning

> "If all your competitors are pushing 'affordable', maybe you emphasize 'premium quality'. If
> they're all about 'quick results', perhaps your angle is 'sustainable, long-term success'."

### 3. Elevate proven approaches

> "When you see an angle that's clearly working, don't just copy it — elevate it."

⭐ **The four concrete moves**, from the delivered session:

- Make it **more authentic**
- Add **stronger social proof**
- Connect it more to **your unique value proposition**
- **Agitate the problem more**

That is what "elevate" means operationally, and it is the difference between this step and copying.

### 4. Import cross-category inspiration

> "A B2B SaaS company might learn from how DTC fashion brands create urgency."

The method: describe the customer in one sentence that names no product category, then ask what else
that person spends money on.

⚠️ **ACA ships a dedicated prompt for this**, the Cross-Vertical Creative Inspiration Finder. Its
tracked-download list files it under Course 230, and this module names it as its own third resource,
so it is shared between the two. Where a skill for it is installed alongside this one, route there
rather than paraphrasing.

### 5. Track creative evolution

> "If everyone suddenly shifts to a new hook style or claim, there's probably a reason — either the
> market is changing or they've all discovered something that works."

**This is the move that requires the cadence.** It only exists across repeated passes, which is why
the analysis date on each worksheet matters.

## Producing the deck

1. **Scope slide first** — competitors, period, platforms, and how many ads the analysis saw
2. **One row per competitor per dimension**, kept parallel so they can be read across
3. **Real ad examples**, captured rather than described
4. **The patterns slide**, with white space stated as a list
5. ⛔ **A limitations line.** What could not be seen: landing pages, offers, retargeting, spend,
   objectives, and every ad turned off before you looked

⚠️ **Mark inferences on the slide, not in the speaker notes.** A deck outlives its presenter. *"High
testing velocity"* is observed; `they are winning with UGC` is a guess about a business whose
numbers you do not have, and it needs to say so where a reader will see it.

> "The goal isn't to become a carbon copy of your competitors. It's to understand the landscape so
> you can carve out your own distinctive place within it — or disrupt it entirely."
